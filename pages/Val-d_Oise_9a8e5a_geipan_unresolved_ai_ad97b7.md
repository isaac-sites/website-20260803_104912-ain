## Introduction

GEIPAN’s Val-d’Oise files show that an unexplained report is not necessarily the most visually impressive one. Several witnesses have described formations, fiery orange lights or oddly shaped objects, yet investigators matched them to ordinary aircraft by reconstructing sight lines, consulting aeronautical charts and checking radar tracks. The quieter Maffliers case remains unresolved because its most plausible explanation — the rising Sun distorted by unusual cloud layers — does not fit the reported direction.

That distinction matters. GEIPAN, the French space agency CNES’s investigation unit for unidentified aerospace phenomena, does not classify cases according to how dramatic a witness’s story sounds. It asks whether a proposed explanation fits the time, direction, movement and environmental conditions, and whether the available evidence is sufficiently reliable. ## What the Maffliers classification means


<div class="content-callout content-callout--key content-callout--takeaway" markdown="1">

At about 6.06am on 29 June 2012, a walker near Maffliers reported seeing a huge white luminous object stationary above a field beside woodland. The witness said the object appeared flat underneath and concave above. After less than 20 seconds, it supposedly rose slightly, tilted and departed rapidly into the cloud cover without detectable noise or smoke. No second witness, photograph, video or instrumental record confirmed the observation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/fr/cas/2012-06-08256" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">GEIPANMAFFLIERS (95) 29.06.2012 &#124; GEIPAN...</span></span></span>

</div>

GEIPAN investigators considered an ordinary but visually powerful explanation: the rising Sun seen between cloud layers. The timing, brightness and unusual weather made that hypothesis attractive. The previous day had reportedly brought heat and heavy rain, followed by substantial cloud cover on the morning of the sighting. A low Sun shining through gaps or interacting with layered cloud can produce an object-like patch of intense light whose apparent size and boundaries are difficult to judge.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/fr/cas/2012-06-08256" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">GEIPANMAFFLIERS (95) 29.06.2012 &#124; GEIPAN...</span></span></span>

Three reported details prevented investigators from adopting that explanation with confidence:

* **The direction was wrong.** GEIPAN calculated a difference of about 54 degrees between the witness’s stated viewing direction and the Sun. Investigators visited the site and concluded that ordinary uncertainty in the witness’s orientation could not account for such a large discrepancy.
* **The light appeared to tilt before disappearing.** GEIPAN acknowledged that glare or perception might create an impression of movement or inclination, but could not establish that this had happened.
* **Its apparent size was extreme.** The witness’s estimate was almost ten times the apparent diameter of the Sun. People commonly overestimate the size of the Sun or Moon near the horizon, but the reported exaggeration was unusually large.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/fr/cas/2012-06-08256" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">GEIPANMAFFLIERS (95) 29.06.2012 &#124; GEIPAN...</span></span></span>

A second site visit explored whether a complex reflection might have displaced sunlight into the reported direction, but investigators found no satisfactory optical mechanism. Because the exact cloud and atmospheric conditions could not be reproduced, the investigation reached an evidential dead end: the observation resembled a known natural phenomenon, but an important part of the geometry did not fit.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/fr/cas/2012-06-08256" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">GEIPANMAFFLIERS (95) 29.06.2012 &#124; GEIPAN...</span></span></span>

The file has an apparent classification wrinkle. Its explanatory text describes it as a **D1** case: an unexplained phenomenon of medium evidential consistency. GEIPAN historically used D1 for strange cases supported by moderate evidence, often involving one witness and no recording, while D2 denoted unexplained cases with greater strangeness or consistency. The live Maffliers page, updated on 16 June 2026, now displays the broader classification **D**, although its retained conclusion still says D1. GEIPAN’s current classification guidance notes that the D1/D2 subdivision was used after 2008 but that its public scheme has since been presented more generally as A, B, C and D. The substantive verdict has not become stronger: the case remains unexplained on the available information, not confirmed as an extraordinary craft.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/fr/cas/2012-06-08256" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">GEIPANMAFFLIERS (95) 29.06.2012 &#124; GEIPAN...</span></span></span>

## How flight paths resolved brighter reports

The contrast with a March 2013 report from Domont is especially useful. A witness watched five orange and white lights appear and disappear over more than an hour, viewed them through binoculars, photographed them and called the gendarmes. The lights were described in vivid terms, including an impression resembling an orange object on fire. On testimony alone, that report could sound more substantial than the short Maffliers encounter: it lasted longer, produced photographs and was observed in the presence of police officers.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cnes-geipan.fr/fr/cas/2013-03-08445" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">GEIPANDOMONT (95) 18.03.201330 Jan 2015 — DOMONT (95) 18.03.2013; Date d&#x27;observation. 18/03/2013; Région. Autre; Département. Val-d&#x27;Oi...</span></span></span>

The photographs nevertheless supplied the information needed to solve it. GEIPAN compared buildings in the images with the local landscape to establish the viewing direction. One prominent light lay on an azimuth of approximately 266 degrees, matching the published approach heading for runway 27R at Paris-Charles de Gaulle Airport. Lines projected from the runway and the witness’s position passed between the same buildings and through the photographed lights. Other images corresponded to aircraft departing from additional Charles de Gaulle runways. GEIPAN therefore classified the report **A**, meaning the explanation was established: several airliners approaching or departing the airport.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cnes-geipan.fr/fr/cas/2013-03-08445" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">GEIPANDOMONT (95) 18.03.201330 Jan 2015 — DOMONT (95) 18.03.2013; Date d&#x27;observation. 18/03/2013; Région. Autre; Département. Val-d&#x27;Oi...</span></span></span>

The apparent appearances and disappearances were not evidence of objects materialising. An aircraft approaching nearly head-on can look like a stationary bright point because its sideways motion is minimal. Its landing lights may dominate while the fuselage remains invisible, particularly at night or through haze. A slight turn, cloud passage or change in viewing angle can then cause the light to weaken abruptly. Multiple aircraft using parallel runways can produce a slowly changing formation that appears organised without representing a single structured object. The Domont reconstruction was persuasive because several independent elements — photographic direction, runway alignment and known airport procedures — converged on the same explanation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cnes-geipan.fr/fr/cas/2013-03-08445" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">GEIPANDOMONT (95) 18.03.201330 Jan 2015 — DOMONT (95) 18.03.2013; Date d&#x27;observation. 18/03/2013; Région. Autre; Département. Val-d&#x27;Oi...</span></span></span>

A 2023 observation along the A16 between Montmorency and Beauvais provides an even clearer example of instrumental checking. A motorist reported a white, pearly object shaped like an inverted V moving slowly on a slightly descending path. No image of the object was obtained, but the witness supplied a precise location, photographs of the observation site and a drawing. A radar map from France’s air-defence planning and operations centre showed a Cessna 152 in the correct area at the stated time, performing a manoeuvre over the motorway in the reported direction.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/fr/cas/2023-04-51432" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on cnes-geipan.fr.</span></span></span>

GEIPAN found that the aircraft’s colour, speed, altitude and trajectory agreed with the account. Broken cumulus cloud could have hidden parts of the aeroplane and altered its apparent outline, while traffic and cabin noise inside the car could explain why the engine was not heard. The case was classified **A** as an identified Cessna 152. Here, a radar track outweighed the witness’s unusual shape description because it supplied a time-stamped object following the required path.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/fr/cas/2023-04-51432" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on cnes-geipan.fr.</span></span></span>

## Why photographs do not automatically settle a case

GEIPAN’s Val-d’Oise records illustrate a recurring paradox: a photograph can weaken an extraordinary interpretation rather than support it. In Domont, the images did not show recognisable aircraft bodies, but they preserved alignments with buildings and the horizon. Those fixed reference points allowed investigators to calculate azimuths and compare them with airport approaches. The evidential value lay less in the glowing pixels than in the geometry around them.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cnes-geipan.fr/fr/cas/2013-03-08445" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">GEIPANDOMONT (95) 18.03.201330 Jan 2015 — DOMONT (95) 18.03.2013; Date d&#x27;observation. 18/03/2013; Région. The 2010 Cergy case shows the opposite limitation. A witness reported a point of light moving in a chevron-like pattern at roughly two-second intervals. GEIPAN compared the description with a long-exposure photograph of an airliner, in which alternating navigation or anti-collision lights created separated bright marks along a straight route. It proposed that blinking lights had been perceived as changes in position. Because this identification was considered probable rather than demonstrated through a matching flight record, the case received classification **B**, meaning an ordinary explanation was strongly favoured but not established to the standard of an A case.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cnes-geipan.fr/fr/cas/2010-07-02592?order=field_classification_des_cas&amp;page=83&amp;sort=desc" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on cnes-geipan.fr.</span></span></span>

An **A** verdict generally rests on a specific identification supported by converging details, such as an aircraft track matching the reported time and trajectory. A **B** verdict means that a conventional cause fits very well but some confirmation is missing. ## The limits of witness estimates

The hardest measurements in a light-in-the-sky report are often those witnesses express most confidently: size, distance, altitude and speed. Without a known scale, a small nearby object and a large distant object can occupy the same angle in the sky. A witness who assumes that a light is low and close will consequently infer a large physical size and extraordinary acceleration, even though neither distance nor speed was independently measured.

Clouds further complicate the impression. They can conceal an aircraft’s wings and fuselage while leaving a landing light visible, or reveal only changing portions of a sunlit region. Atmospheric haze can soften boundaries, make lights seem larger and remove familiar depth cues. In the Montmorency case, GEIPAN considered cloud obstruction a plausible reason that a conventional light aircraft appeared as a pearly V-shaped object. In Maffliers, unusual cloud conditions made a solar or atmospheric explanation plausible but impossible to reproduce precisely.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/fr/cas/2023-04-51432" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on cnes-geipan.fr.</span></span></span>

Memory is another constraint. GEIPAN’s methodology separates **strangeness** — how poorly a report matches known phenomena after investigation — from **consistency**, meaning the amount and reliability of usable information. A sincere witness can supply a highly strange description with only moderate consistency, especially when an event lasts seconds and leaves no record. Conversely, a photograph or radar trace may provide enough external structure to correct a sincere but mistaken impression. GEIPAN therefore requires stronger evidential consistency as the residual strangeness of a case increases.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/fr/node/58788" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">* Le GEIPAN (Groupe d’Études et d’Informations sur les Phénomènes Aérospatiaux Non identifiés) est un service technique du CNES (Centre N...</span></span></span>

## What the verdicts say about Val-d’Oise

The department’s proximity to Charles de Gaulle Airport makes aircraft misidentification a central part of its UFO record. Taken together, the files undermine two common assumptions. First, several witnesses, photographs or police attendance do not guarantee that a report will remain unexplained; the Domont lights were resolved through ordinary aviation geometry. Second, an unresolved classification does not rank a case as stronger than an identified one. It states only that no tested explanation adequately accounts for the report. In Val-d’Oise, the official record is therefore less a catalogue of extraordinary craft than a demonstration of how flight paths, radar and careful reconstruction can solve impressive sightings — and why a brief observation may remain open when one crucial measurement refuses to fit.
