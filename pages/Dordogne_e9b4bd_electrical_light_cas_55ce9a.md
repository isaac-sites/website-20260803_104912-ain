## Introduction

Two of [Dordogne]({{ 'why-dordognes-ufo-mysteries-often-faded/' | relative_url }})’s most dramatic UFO reports became less mysterious when investigators looked closely at electricity, weather and the order in which witnesses told their stories. Near Eymet in April 1976, a couple described green luminous discs plunging towards the ground during violent weather while nearby television sets reportedly lost sound and picture. At Saint-Michel-de-Villadeix in December 1988, a man saw an intensely bright blue-violet sphere near power lines, felt pressure and an electrical discharge, and watched his dog flee.

Neither case is now treated by France’s official investigators as strong evidence of an unknown craft. GEIPAN, the unidentified-aerospace-phenomena unit of the French space agency CNES, classifies both as category B: probably explained, in this instance as electrical or plasma phenomena. The second case is especially instructive because the witness’s account changed after contact with neighbours and the media. Together, the incidents show why vivid testimony must be compared with power-system clues, weather conditions and the earliest available statement.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/fr/cas/1976-04-00300" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">GEIPANEYMET (24) 24.04.1976 &#124; GEIPAN...</span></span></span>

## The green discs reported near Eymet

On 24 April 1976, at about 4pm, a couple were in their garden in the countryside near Eymet. The weather was stormy, with large dark clouds obscuring the Sun. According to the gendarmerie report, an abnormal noise resembling a powerful rush of air drew their attention. A few hundred metres away they saw two luminous, jade-green discs. The lights appeared to plunge almost vertically towards the ground at very high speed and then disappear. The brief display was said to occur again twice.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/fr/cas/1976-04-00300" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">GEIPANEYMET (24) 24.04.1976 &#124; GEIPAN...</span></span></span>

The observation sounded striking because several features appeared to reinforce one another: two witnesses, daylight, apparent proximity, unusual colour, rapid movement and an associated noise. Early newspaper coverage pushed the story towards the language of flying saucers, even suggesting that the objects had caused a snowstorm. The surviving police file, however, records a more cautious sequence. A violent gust followed the lights, snow then fell, and residents in the area reportedly experienced temporary loss of television sound and picture.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/sites/default/files/PV%20n%C2%B0378%20%281976309228%29.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on cnes-geipan.fr.</span></span></span>

Those details changed the direction of the inquiry. A local woman familiar with storms reportedly heard an unusually loud thunderclap and went indoors. Investigators considered the television interference, severe local weather and luminous effects together rather than treating the discs as isolated flying objects. Their report proposed an electrical disturbance associated with a rare form of storm activity, described at the time as a possible “white storm”. GEIPAN’s current summary similarly calls the incident a probable electromagnetic phenomenon caused by a local weather event and places it in the electricity-and-plasma category.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/fr/cas/1976-04-00300" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">GEIPANEYMET (24) 24.04.1976 &#124; GEIPAN...</span></span></span>

This does not mean that investigators reproduced the event or proved the exact physics. “Plasma” is sometimes used too loosely in UFO discussion, as though naming ionised gas automatically solves a case. In Eymet, the responsible conclusion is narrower: the timing of the lights, gust, snowfall and television disturbance gives an electrical-weather explanation more support than the idea of two engineered objects.

The case also illustrates how reporting can amplify a sighting. The gendarmerie file criticised inconsistencies and sensational wording in the press coverage. One later article presented the witnesses as denying that they had seen UFOs at all and describing the occurrence instead as rare atmospheric magnetism. Whether that wording precisely reflected their scientific understanding is uncertain, but it shows that the public “UFO” narrative was not a stable, uncontested version of the event.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/sites/default/files/PV%20n%C2%B0378%20%281976309228%29.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on cnes-geipan.fr.</span></span></span>

## The blue-violet sphere at Saint-Michel-de-Villadeix

The Saint-Michel-de-Villadeix case occurred on 1 December 1988 at about 8.20am. A man walking near his home with his dog reported an intense light roughly 60 metres away. He compared its brightness with an electric arc and described a round, blue-violet form travelling silently in a straight south-to-north direction at very high speed. The observation lasted only three or four seconds. During it, he felt pressure and an electrical shock; his dog ran towards the house and ignored his commands.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/fr/cas/1988-12-01155?order=field_date&amp;page=125&amp;sort=asc&amp;undefined=" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">GEIPANSAINT-MICHEL-DE-VILLADEIX (24) 01.12.1988 &#124; GEIPAN...</span></span></span>

As a narrative, it contains features often regarded as signs of a high-quality close encounter: a nearby luminous object, a physical effect on the witness and unusual animal behaviour. The immediate surroundings, however, supplied unusually specific conventional clues.

The light was reported close to a crossing of two electricity lines and a transformer. Its course was aligned with one of the lines. Residents experienced a sudden, brief voltage drop at approximately the same time, while the witness’s physical sensations were themselves compatible with a strong electrical disturbance. GEIPAN argues that ionisation of air or airborne material in an intense electric field could produce a luminous form, comparing the basic principle with gas glowing inside a neon tube.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/fr/cas/1988-12-01155?order=field_date&amp;page=125&amp;sort=asc&amp;undefined=" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">GEIPANSAINT-MICHEL-DE-VILLADEIX (24) 01.12.1988 &#124; GEIPAN...</span></span></span>

The hypothesis is plausible without being a complete laboratory reconstruction. Scientific reviews describe ball lightning and related luminous electrical events as rare, incompletely understood phenomena reported most often in association with lightning or strong electrical conditions. Many proposed mechanisms exist, but no single model accounts securely for every report. That uncertainty matters: Saint-Michel-de-Villadeix should not simply be labelled “ball lightning” as though the term identified a proven mechanism. The better-supported claim is that a short-lived electrical light near power infrastructure fits the observed setting more closely than an independent airborne vehicle does.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5917226/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed Central (PMC)]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Pub Med Central (PMC)The Riddle of Ball Lightning: A Review</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">One of the most intriguing and enduring scientific challenges is to find an explanation for ball lightning, the shining fireballs that so...</span></span></span>

## The account that changed after ten days

The most important evidence in the Saint-Michel-de-Villadeix file is not the colour of the sphere or the frightened dog. It is the existence of two gendarmerie statements.

The witness made his first deposition on the day of the event. Ten days later, he gave another account. According to GEIPAN’s re-examination, the later version introduced several luminous balls and placed the phenomenon farther away, in a field rather than near the road, transformer and crossing power lines. The 1988 assessment had relied heavily on that expanded version and left the case unexplained.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/fr/cas/1988-12-01155?order=field_date&amp;page=125&amp;sort=asc&amp;undefined=" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">GEIPANSAINT-MICHEL-DE-VILLADEIX (24) 01.12.1988 &#124; GEIPAN...</span></span></span>

When GEIPAN revisited the case, it gave greater weight to the first statement. That was not because early testimony is automatically accurate, but because it was recorded before prolonged discussion and publicity. The later account had followed numerous contacts or approaches from neighbours and media representatives. GEIPAN concluded that these interactions could have influenced the witness, even without any deliberate embellishment. Once the initial location and single-light description were restored to the centre of the analysis, the connection with the electricity network became much stronger. The case was reclassified from D, unexplained, to B, probably explained as an electrical and plasma phenomenon.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/fr/cas/1988-12-01155?order=field_date&amp;page=125&amp;sort=asc&amp;undefined=" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">GEIPANSAINT-MICHEL-DE-VILLADEIX (24) 01.12.1988 &#124; GEIPAN...</span></span></span>

Research on eyewitness memory supports the general caution behind that decision. Memory is reconstructive rather than a fixed recording. Information encountered after an event can alter later recall, and repetition can increase both acceptance of misleading details and confidence in them. This does not show that the Dordogne witness invented anything, nor does it allow psychologists to reconstruct exactly what he saw. It does explain why investigators value a prompt, independently recorded account over a later narrative developed through repeated conversations.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S000169181100223X" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sciencedirect.com">[sciencedirect.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sciencedirect.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">People who witness the same event will often remember it differently: a student remembers her teacher saying that the father of psycholog...</span></span></span>

There is also a risk in the opposite direction. Investigators can prefer an early statement simply because it supports a convenient explanation. A fair assessment must therefore ask whether the first account has independent support. Here, it does: the reported location, alignment with a power line, nearby transformer, brief voltage drop and electrical sensation form a connected set of clues. The later account weakens that connection but does not provide equivalent physical evidence for several distant objects.

## Why the power-system clues carried more weight

Both cases became less strange when investigators stopped treating the luminous shape as the whole event. The surrounding conditions supplied information that the witnesses could not easily manufacture through interpretation alone.

**At Eymet**, the lights coincided with storm conditions, a violent gust, snowfall and reported television interference. These details point towards an energetic local atmospheric or electromagnetic disturbance. The identification remains probable rather than demonstrated, because the surviving record lacks modern instrumental measurements.

**At Saint-Michel-de-Villadeix**, the light appeared beside intersecting electricity lines and a transformer, travelled along a line, coincided with a voltage reduction and produced an apparent electrical effect on the witness. That is a more tightly connected physical pattern. The later shift in location would have removed the phenomenon from the very infrastructure most relevant to explaining it, which is why the chronology of the testimony mattered so much.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/fr/cas/1976-04-00300" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">GEIPANEYMET (24) 24.04.1976 &#124; GEIPAN...</span></span></span>

GEIPAN’s wider methodology reflects this distinction between an extraordinary experience and extraordinary evidence. Human testimony is central to its work, but the agency explicitly treats testimony as fragile and potentially altered by perception, memory, emotion, discussion and questioning. It therefore compares accounts with environmental data and classifies cases according to both residual strangeness and consistency—the quantity and reliability of the information available.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.geipan.fr/en/node/58788" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: geipan.fr">[Geipan]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on geipan.fr.</span></span></span>

That approach helps explain why neither Dordogne incident is classed as definitively identified. Category B means that a conventional explanation is regarded as probable, not certain. The available evidence supports electrical events, but it does not establish exactly which discharge process occurred, how the visible forms were sustained or whether every reported detail was perceived accurately.

## Did later reporting strengthen or weaken the cases?

Later investigation weakened the interpretation of both incidents as unknown craft, but in different ways.

At Eymet, the principal change came from putting the sighting back into its meteorological and electromagnetic setting. Newspaper treatment had separated the green discs from the storm and turned a complicated local event into a simpler UFO story. The gendarmerie inquiry reconnected the lights with weather, television disruption and other witnesses’ experience of the storm. The explanation is not mechanically complete, but the extraterrestrial or technological reading lost much of its force.

At Saint-Michel-de-Villadeix, the decisive change was evidential rather than merely interpretive. GEIPAN compared two statements from the same witness and judged that the earliest one was more reliable. The later version made the case more spectacular—more lights and a location less obviously connected to electrical equipment—but also less trustworthy because it emerged after social and media attention. Re-examination therefore reduced both the case’s residual strangeness and its evidential consistency.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/fr/cas/1988-12-01155?order=field_date&amp;page=125&amp;sort=asc&amp;undefined=" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">GEIPANSAINT-MICHEL-DE-VILLADEIX (24) 01.12.1988 &#124; GEIPAN...</span></span></span>

The two cases should not be dismissed as witnesses “seeing nothing”. Both involved genuine, startling experiences, and both may represent unusual luminous electrical effects that ordinary observers would have little chance of recognising. Their value in Dordogne’s UFO history lies precisely there. They show how a report can remain memorable and physically real while its initial interpretation becomes weaker.

## A cautious verdict

Eymet and Saint-Michel-de-Villadeix are best understood as probably explained electrical-light cases rather than robust unresolved UFO encounters. Eymet has a strong association with violent local weather and electromagnetic interference, although the exact phenomenon was never instrumentally established. Saint-Michel-de-Villadeix presents the clearer power-system connection: lines, a transformer, a voltage drop, an aligned trajectory and electrical sensations, reinforced by the greater evidential value of the witness’s first statement.

Neither file proves a specific plasma model, and neither justifies ridicule of the witnesses. The lesson is methodological. Brightness, proximity and physical sensation can make a report seem unusually strong, yet the most important evidence may lie outside the luminous object itself—in power failures, weather records, geography and the difference between what was said immediately and what was remembered after the story began to circulate.
