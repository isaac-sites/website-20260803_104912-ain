## Introduction

Official investigation has repeatedly changed the status of [Morbihan]({{ 'how-morbihans-ufo-mysteries-changed-over/' | relative_url }})’s best-known unusual-sighting reports. Cases once filed as unexplained have later been identified as the Moon, thunderstorms or auroral activity; others have been downgraded not because an ordinary cause was proved, but because the surviving evidence was too weak to support any firm conclusion. France’s official archive therefore shows that “unexplained” is not a permanent verdict and does not mean that investigators have confirmed an extraordinary craft.



<img src="{{ "/assets/images/Morbihan_61a3f8_morbihan_geipan_recl_73078e-overview.webp" | relative_url }}" alt="Overview image for Official Files" loading="eager" decoding="sync" fetchpriority="high">

The clearest example is the long observation made on the island of Groix in April 1982. Witnesses regarded the bright object as something other than the Moon, and the case was initially treated as unexplained. A later GEIPAN review reconstructed the sky and concluded that the reported light’s position, appearance and slow movement matched the Moon closely enough for the file to be reclassified as fully identified. Other Morbihan records demonstrate the remaining possibilities: probable explanations where the fit is strong but incomplete, and unresolved files whose main feature is missing or unreliable information rather than compelling evidence of an unknown vehicle.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/sites/default/files/Notes%20d%27enquete17.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Notes d&#x27;enquete17</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">GEIPANNOTES D&#x27;ENQUETE GROIX (56) 13.04.1982June 27, 2017 — 13 Apr 1982 — Une vérification via Stellarium pour Groix (coordonnées: 47°38...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 27, 2017</span></span></span>

## How GEIPAN’s classifications change a case

GEIPAN, the unidentified-aerospace-phenomena unit of the French space agency CNES, collects witness reports, examines possible explanations and publishes anonymised case material. Its partners include the gendarmerie, police, civil and military aviation bodies, the national weather service and scientific organisations. That network can supply information that witnesses usually lack, such as astronomical positions, weather conditions, aircraft movements and reports from other locations.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cnes.fr/en/projects/geipan" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes.fr">[CNES]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">GEIPAN7 Jul 2025 — GEIPAN, the French UAP research and information group created by CNES in 1977, collects, analyses and archives inf...</span></span></span>

The distinction between C and D is especially important. A C file is not a strong mystery. It means that the report cannot be tested properly because details are missing, contradictory or too imprecise. A D case has resisted explanation after investigators judged the evidence adequate enough for meaningful analysis. GEIPAN also states that C and D files may be revisited when new information or improved analytical methods become available.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/en/node/58787" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ified due to lack of data or information.Read more...</span></span></span>

Reclassification is therefore built into the system. In 2017 GEIPAN announced that it had re-examined roughly twenty older D cases from 1978–98 and moved them into A, B or C. The review covered forty witness statements and included the Groix case. GEIPAN attributed such changes partly to better software and partly to the investigative experience accumulated since the original inquiries.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/fr/actualites/geipan-revisite-cas" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-title">geipan revisite cas</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">GEIPANLe GEIPAN publie 3 nouveaux cas D1 et revisite...28 Jul 2017 — Le GEIPAN publie 3 nouveaux cas D1 et revisite une vingtaine d&#x27;anci...</span></span></span>

This matters because a classification can change in two very different directions. An investigation may discover a persuasive conventional source, moving a report to A or B. Alternatively, it may decide that the original D label overstated the quality of the evidence, moving the case to C. The second outcome does not explain what the witness saw; it corrects how much confidence should be placed in the mystery.

## Groix: a three-hour mystery becomes the Moon

On the night of 14 April 1982, several people on Groix observed a conspicuous light over the sea. According to GEIPAN’s summary, the sighting continued from different parts of the island, with some witnesses watching until about 5 a.m. One person returned home and woke another witness at around 3 a.m.; that person also saw a bright, apparently fixed object and reported a similar observation the following night. The long duration and multiple witnesses helped the event acquire the appearance of a substantial case rather than a fleeting mistake.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/fr/cas/1982-04-00928" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">GEIPANGROIX (56) 14.04.1982GROIX (56) 14.04.1982. Date d&#x27;observation. 14/04... Sur L&#x27;île de Groix... PAN alors que les témoins affirmen...</span></span></span>

The witnesses did not believe they were looking at the Moon. Some testimony explicitly distinguished the object from it, while another witness reportedly stated that there was no Moon visible. Such statements can sound decisive in a retelling: if several observers knew the Moon and rejected it, why should investigators identify it as the cause?

GEIPAN’s later review treated that disagreement as something to test rather than accept at face value. Investigators used astronomical reconstruction software to calculate the Moon’s position from Groix during the observation. They found that it was large and bright, with about 70 per cent of its disc illuminated, and that it occupied the same restricted eastern to south-eastern area in which the phenomenon had been reported. Near the end of the sighting it would have been roughly 20 degrees above the ocean in the stated viewing direction.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/sites/default/files/Notes%20d%27enquete17.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Notes d&#x27;enquete17</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">GEIPANNOTES D&#x27;ENQUETE GROIX (56) 13.04.1982June 27, 2017 — 13 Apr 1982 — Une vérification via Stellarium pour Groix (coordonnées: 47°38...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 27, 2017</span></span></span>

The apparent behaviour also fitted. A celestial object watched for several hours changes position gradually, and its movement can be difficult to judge when the observer has few fixed reference points. Thin cloud, haze or uneven cloud cover can hide the Moon’s recognisable outline, enlarge its glow or make its brightness fluctuate. GEIPAN notes more generally that bright planets, stars and other fixed lights can also seem to move because of autokinesis, a visual effect that occurs when a small light is viewed against a dark, poorly referenced background.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/sites/default/files/Notes%20d%27enquete17.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Notes d&#x27;enquete17</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">GEIPANNOTES D&#x27;ENQUETE GROIX (56) 13.04.1982June 27, 2017 — 13 Apr 1982 — Une vérification via Stellarium pour Groix (coordonnées: 47°38...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 27, 2017</span></span></span>

The investigators therefore gave more weight to the reconstructed geometry than to the witnesses’ confidence that the Moon was absent. The final record lists Groix as classification A, with “Moon” as the phenomenon, very low strangeness and only moderate evidential consistency. The case was no longer treated as an unexplained object.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/index.php/fr/temoignage/3784" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on cnes-geipan.fr.</span></span></span>

This conclusion does not require the witnesses to have been dishonest or careless. It shows how recognition can fail when a familiar object appears in unfamiliar conditions. A bright Moon seen through broken cloud over a dark sea may look larger, more isolated or more structured than expected. Several people can then reinforce one another’s interpretation while still observing the same ordinary source.

## Why several witnesses did not settle the issue

Multiple testimony usually improves a file because it can confirm that something visible was present. It does not automatically establish the object’s identity, distance or physical size. In the Groix case, the additional witnesses strengthened the conclusion that a bright light occupied the sky for a long period. They did not provide independent measurements proving that it was nearby, mechanical or separate from the Moon.

Witness agreement can also be less independent than it first appears. People observing together may use the same estimated direction, repeat the same comparisons or adopt another person’s interpretation. Waking someone and drawing attention to a particular part of the sky confirms visibility, but it may also guide that person towards the same target. The official reconstruction therefore compared the shared description with an external reference: the calculated night sky.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/sites/default/files/Notes%20d%27enquete17.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Notes d&#x27;enquete17</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">GEIPANNOTES D&#x27;ENQUETE GROIX (56) 13.04.1982June 27, 2017 — 13 Apr 1982 — Une vérification via Stellarium pour Groix (coordonnées: 47°38...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 27, 2017</span></span></span>

Duration, often presented as evidence against an astronomical explanation, actually supported it here. A meteor or fast aircraft would not remain in roughly the same region for hours. The Moon would. The feature that made the case seem especially extraordinary in popular summaries became part of the ordinary explanation once investigators considered the observation as a timed astronomical event rather than simply a story about a luminous object.

The Groix file also illustrates the difference between witness sincerity and witness accuracy. Official reclassification did not declare that the observers invented the light. It concluded that they misidentified a real and highly visible celestial body. That distinction is central to reading Morbihan’s official UFO record fairly.

## Probable explanations are not disguised certainties

Not every Morbihan report provides enough evidence for an A classification. GEIPAN uses B when an ordinary explanation fits the principal features but cannot be demonstrated completely. Several local files involving drifting orange or reddish lights have been classified as probable Thai lanterns, including reports from Ploemeur, Arradon, Inzinzac-Lochrist, Plouharnel, Neulliac and Saint-Pierre-Quiberon.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/fr/recherche/cas/tab?field_agregation_index_value=&amp;field_date_d_observation_value%5Bmax%5D=&amp;field_date_d_observation_value%5Bmin%5D=&amp;field_date_value=&amp;field_departement_target_id%5B0%5D%5Btarget_id%5D=245&amp;field_document_existe_ou_pas_value=All&amp;field_is_new_value=All&amp;field_is_revisited_value=All&amp;field_latitude_value%5Bmax%5D=&amp;field_latitude_value%5Bmin%5D=&amp;field_longitude_value%5Bmax%5D=&amp;field_longitude_value%5Bmin%5D=&amp;field_phenomene_target_id=&amp;field_type_de_cas_target_id=All&amp;page=0&amp;select-category-export=nothing" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on cnes-geipan.fr.</span></span></span>

Lantern identifications typically depend on behaviour rather than recovered objects. A light may rise slowly, drift with the wind, change brightness as the flame moves and disappear when the fuel is exhausted. Several lights may follow similar paths if released together. Those features can produce a strong explanation, but without finding a lantern or identifying the launch, investigators may retain a degree of uncertainty.

The same principle applies to other B files. A March 2012 sighting at Ploeren was classified as probably Sirius, while a March 2022 case at Marzan was attributed probably to an illuminated balloon. The archive is not claiming laboratory-level proof in such cases. It is recording that a known phenomenon provides the most coherent account of the observations.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/fr/recherche/cas/tab?field_agregation_index_value=&amp;field_date_d_observation_value%5Bmax%5D=&amp;field_date_d_observation_value%5Bmin%5D=&amp;field_date_value=&amp;field_departement_target_id%5B0%5D%5Btarget_id%5D=245&amp;field_document_existe_ou_pas_value=All&amp;field_is_new_value=All&amp;field_is_revisited_value=All&amp;field_latitude_value%5Bmax%5D=&amp;field_latitude_value%5Bmin%5D=&amp;field_longitude_value%5Bmax%5D=&amp;field_longitude_value%5Bmin%5D=&amp;field_phenomene_target_id=&amp;field_type_de_cas_target_id=All&amp;page=0&amp;select-category-export=nothing" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on cnes-geipan.fr.</span></span></span>

This cautious wording is valuable. An A classification says that the investigation regards the identification as secure. A B classification says that the conventional hypothesis is stronger than the alternatives but leaves room for incomplete documentation, uncertain timing or imprecise witness estimates. Treating both categories merely as “debunked” loses that difference.

## A former D case split between thunderstorms and missing data

The 25–26 May 1985 case associated with Saint-Malo-des-Trois-Fontaines shows that a single incident can contain observations of very different quality. Four witnesses reported several phases while travelling and later while watching the sky from a property. During the first phase they saw numerous coloured flashes appearing in different directions. Later, they watched pairs of lights whose movement and spacing seemed unusual.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cnes-geipan.fr/fr/cas/1985-05-01068" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on cnes-geipan.fr.</span></span></span>

The case had previously been classified D. On review, GEIPAN concluded that the initial flashes were probably lightning from thunderstorms. That phase became B because meteorological activity offered a strong explanation. The later observations were not retained as unexplained in the stronger sense. Investigators decided that they were weakly documented, not very strange in themselves and possibly influenced by the alarm created by the first display. Those portions were assigned C because reliable information was lacking.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cnes-geipan.fr/fr/cas/1985-05-01068" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on cnes-geipan.fr.</span></span></span>

The revised result is more informative than one label for the whole night. It separates a probably explained trigger from later reports that cannot be reconstructed. It also recognises a common sequence in prolonged sighting episodes: an initially striking event causes people to scrutinise the sky, after which ordinary lights, stars or poorly perceived movements take on greater significance.

This reclassification weakened the original UFO claim in two ways. It found a plausible natural source for the most dramatic phase and judged the remaining material too poor to count as a robust unexplained case. Nothing in the revised file requires an unknown craft, even though not every individual light was specifically identified.

## The aurora seen from Morbihan and the Paris region

A large luminous display on 13 March 1989 produced reports from Saint-Pierre-Quiberon in Morbihan and from witnesses in the Yvelines near Paris. Observers described extensive light in the clouds with yellow, green and red colouring. The wide geographical spread made a small local object unlikely and pointed towards a large atmospheric or astronomical event.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/fr/cas/1989-03-01172" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on cnes-geipan.fr.</span></span></span>

The gendarmerie inquiries connected the reports with solar activity covered in the news at the time. The case had originally been placed in category B as a spectacular natural light-propagation phenomenon. On later review, GEIPAN identified it more specifically as an aurora and published the combined reports as an A case.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/fr/cas/1989-03-01172" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on cnes-geipan.fr.</span></span></span>

This example shows the value of looking beyond departmental boundaries. A light reported from a Morbihan beach might initially appear local, particularly if clouds obscure its structure. Reports at similar times hundreds of kilometres away transform the analysis. They support a phenomenon operating on a regional scale and allow investigators to compare the colours and timing with known space-weather activity.

The case also shows that reclassification need not overturn the basic direction of an earlier conclusion. The original investigators already suspected a natural event. Later work improved the identification from a broad probable explanation to a specific one.

## What classification C really means in Morbihan

Morbihan’s archive contains numerous C cases described as lacking reliable information. An observation at Ploërmel in July 1981 is listed in this category with a gendarmerie record but no sufficiently secure identification. Later examples include Plouharnel in August 2009 and Ploemeur in July 1985. More recent files at Pontivy in December 2024 and Plouhinec in January 2023 are also classified C in the current public database.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/fr/cas/1981-07-00879?field_is_new_value=All&amp;field_is_revisited_value=All&amp;field_type_de_cas_target_id=All&amp;order=field_date&amp;page=145%2C34&amp;sort=desc" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on cnes-geipan.fr.</span></span></span>

A C label can result from missing direction, uncertain time, no angular measurements, delayed reporting, poor photographs, contradictory accounts or the absence of independent checks. In such circumstances investigators may be unable to test aircraft, satellite, astronomical or weather explanations reliably. The correct result is not “extraordinary object”, but “insufficient evidence”.

The Plouhinec file illustrates the point particularly well because GEIPAN’s database describes it as lacking information despite a high level of reported strangeness. A very unusual narrative does not necessarily become a strong case. Indeed, the more unusual the claimed behaviour, the more important precise timing, geometry, corroboration and original data become. Without them, the strangeness cannot be separated from errors in perception, memory or description.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/fr/recherche/cas/tab?field_is_new_value=All&amp;field_is_revisited_value=All&amp;field_type_de_cas_target_id=All&amp;order=field_departement_textuel&amp;page=63%2C34&amp;sort=desc" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on cnes-geipan.fr.</span></span></span>

Readers should therefore resist counting every C file as an unexplained Morbihan sighting. Category C measures an evidential limitation. It often tells us more about what investigators cannot reconstruct than about what occupied the sky.

## Ordinary causes dominate the modern record

The published Morbihan list contains a varied set of identifications: the Moon at Bono, Jupiter at Colpo, an aircraft at Kervignac, a cloud at Vannes, a condensation trail at Plouhinec, the International Space Station at Landévant and a group of satellites at Saint-Thuriau. Other reports are assigned probably to lanterns, a bright star or an illuminated balloon.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/fr/recherche/cas/tab?field_agregation_index_value=&amp;field_date_d_observation_value%5Bmax%5D=&amp;field_date_d_observation_value%5Bmin%5D=&amp;field_date_value=&amp;field_departement_target_id%5B0%5D%5Btarget_id%5D=245&amp;field_document_existe_ou_pas_value=All&amp;field_is_new_value=All&amp;field_is_revisited_value=All&amp;field_latitude_value%5Bmax%5D=&amp;field_latitude_value%5Bmin%5D=&amp;field_longitude_value%5Bmax%5D=&amp;field_longitude_value%5Bmin%5D=&amp;field_phenomene_target_id=&amp;field_type_de_cas_target_id=All&amp;page=0&amp;select-category-export=nothing" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on cnes-geipan.fr.</span></span></span>

These explanations are not interchangeable. They arise from different checks:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--checklist" markdown="1">

* **Astronomy** tests whether the Moon, a planet or a bright star occupied the reported direction at the stated time.
* **Satellite reconstruction** compares the observation with predicted orbital passes and formations.
* **Meteorology** examines clouds, storms, wind direction, haze and unusual atmospheric displays.
* **Aviation checks** consider aircraft routes, lighting patterns and condensation trails.
* **Behavioural matching** assesses whether drifting, fading or grouped lights resemble lanterns or balloons.

</div>

The archive therefore does not apply one standard debunking answer to every report. It uses the reported time, place, direction, appearance and movement to search for a source capable of producing the particular observation. When those details are absent, the result generally becomes C rather than a forced identification.

The pattern also explains why better reporting can reduce the number of enduring mysteries. Precise timestamps make satellite and astronomical calculations possible. Original photographs preserve metadata. Compass directions distinguish a planet from an aircraft route. Weather records test whether cloud or lightning was present. A report may sound less detailed when stripped of dramatic adjectives, yet become far more valuable once it contains measurable information.

## What reclassification has done to Morbihan’s UFO history

Official review has weakened several of Morbihan’s apparently strongest cases, but it has not erased the witnesses or reduced every report to the same explanation. Groix became a well-supported lunar misidentification. The 1985 Saint-Malo-des-Trois-Fontaines episode was divided into probable lightning and poorly documented residual observations. The 1989 multi-region display became a confirmed aurora. Numerous modern cases have been matched with aircraft, satellites, planets, clouds, lanterns and balloons.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/fr/cas/1982-04-00928" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">GEIPANGROIX (56) 14.04.1982GROIX (56) 14.04.1982. Date d&#x27;observation. 14/04... Sur L&#x27;île de Groix... PAN alors que les témoins affirmen...</span></span></span>

The main lesson is that an official “unexplained” label records the state of an inquiry at a particular time. It can change when investigators obtain better astronomical software, combine reports from different regions, reassess witness consistency or recognise that the original file never contained enough dependable information.

For Morbihan, that process has produced a more restrained history than the older UFO literature often suggests. Some sightings were genuinely puzzling to the people who experienced them. A smaller number remain undecided in the archive. Yet the surviving official record is dominated not by evidence of exceptional vehicles, but by familiar phenomena seen under difficult conditions and by cases whose details are too incomplete to carry the weight later retellings placed upon them.
