## Introduction

The Cognac radar case is Charentes most dramatic instrument-based UFO report, but it is not the departments strongest evidence for an extraordinary aircraft. On 17 December 1957, military personnel at Cognac air base reportedly watched two unidentified echoes cross a radar display. Each track was later calculated at about 2,880 kilometres per hour, an exceptional speed for the period. Yet no object was seen in the sky, no aircraft was sent to intercept it, and the surviving public file does not provide enough technical data to establish that the echoes represented solid objects at all. GEIPAN, the French space agency unit responsible for investigating unidentified aerospace phenomena, therefore classifies the incident as category C: too poorly documented for a reliable conclusion.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://geipan.fr/en/node/46660?field_date_value=2004-04-23&amp;field_is_new_value=All&amp;field_is_revisited_value=All&amp;field_type_de_cas_target_id=All&amp;order=field_date_d_observation&amp;page=165&amp;sort=desc" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: geipan.fr">[Geipan]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Geipan[AERO CTL] COGNAC (16) 17.12.1957 &#124; GEIPAN...</span></span></span>

That distinction is crucial. The Cognac returns may have been unusual radar targets, atmospheric effects, equipment artefacts or aircraft whose movements were incompletely recorded. The evidence does not identify the cause, but neither does it justify treating the calculated speed as a confirmed performance measurement.

## The two radar tracks recorded in December 1957

According to GEIPANs present case summary, four military personnel were present at Cognac air base when the first echo appeared at 15:01 Universal Time on 17 December 1957. It was reported at a range of roughly 40 kilometres, moved towards the radar and disappeared at about 16 kilometres after approximately 30 seconds. Dividing the 24-kilometre change in displayed range by the elapsed time gives 0.8 kilometres per second, or 2,880 kilometres per hour.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://geipan.fr/en/node/46660?field_date_value=2004-04-23&amp;field_is_new_value=All&amp;field_is_revisited_value=All&amp;field_type_de_cas_target_id=All&amp;order=field_date_d_observation&amp;page=165&amp;sort=desc" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: geipan.fr">[Geipan]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Geipan[AERO CTL] COGNAC (16) 17.12.1957 &#124; GEIPAN...</span></span></span>

A second echo appeared at 15:45. This return was said to move from approximately 37 kilometres to 25 kilometres in 15 seconds. The same simple calculation again produces 2,880 kilometres per hour. GEIPAN says that two witnesses prepared an illustrated report, although four military personnel reportedly observed the first sequence at the radar position.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://geipan.fr/en/node/46660?field_date_value=2004-04-23&amp;field_is_new_value=All&amp;field_is_revisited_value=All&amp;field_type_de_cas_target_id=All&amp;order=field_date_d_observation&amp;page=165&amp;sort=desc" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: geipan.fr">[Geipan]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Geipan[AERO CTL] COGNAC (16) 17.12.1957 &#124; GEIPAN...</span></span></span>

The surviving online record adds several details, but they are not always easy to reconcile. The witness entry for 17 December gives a local time of 16:01, equivalent to 15:01 Universal Time, and notes patches of fog. It also records an electronic or instrumental observation rather than a conventional visual sighting.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://geipan.fr/en/node/46658" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: geipan.fr">[Geipan]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Geipan[AERO CTL] COGNAC (16) 17.12.1957 T1 &#124; GEIPAN...</span></span></span>

The attached archive additionally includes a testimony entry dated 27 December 1957, ten days after the two tracks described in GEIPANs main narrative. Its metadata gives a local time of 14:30 and identifies a single phenomenon, while the indexed text of the military report refers to an unusual track detected by the base radar on that date. GEIPAN nevertheless presents the overall case under 17 December and summarises only the two returns at 15:01 and 15:45. This may reflect more than one episode preserved in the same file, a dating or cataloguing problem, or incomplete digitisation of the original paperwork. The public material does not explain the discrepancy.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://geipan.fr/en/node/46659" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: geipan.fr">[Geipan]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Geipan[AERO CTL] COGNAC (16) 27.12.1957 T2 &#124; GEIPAN...</span></span></span>

That archival uncertainty does not prove that the observations were mistaken. It does, however, make a precise reconstruction harder. A strong radar case normally depends on an internally consistent timeline, the radars operating characteristics, original plots or photographs, weather data, maintenance information and records of known air traffic. Cognacs public file provides only part of that chain.

## Why the calculated speed is not conclusive

The figure of 2,880 kilometres per hour is often the most memorable part of the story. In late 1957, such a speed would have seemed striking, especially on a French military radar screen. But the figure is a calculation based on reported range changes and estimated durations. It is not an independent measurement preserved in a complete radar recording.

Several uncertainties limit what can safely be inferred.

**The radar equipment is insufficiently described.** The published summary does not specify the radar model, wavelength, pulse settings, antenna rotation rate, range accuracy, display scale or the filtering used to suppress unwanted echoes. Without those details, it is impossible to estimate the likely measurement error or determine how confidently an operator could time a brief return.

**The original track data are not publicly available in a form suitable for modern analysis.** GEIPAN refers to drawings made by witnesses, but the accessible case summary does not contain a continuous electronic record showing successive positions, signal strength, altitude, transponder response or Doppler velocity. The speed is therefore reconstructed from a small number of reported range points rather than verified from raw data.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://geipan.fr/en/node/46660?field_date_value=2004-04-23&amp;field_is_new_value=All&amp;field_is_revisited_value=All&amp;field_type_de_cas_target_id=All&amp;order=field_date_d_observation&amp;page=165&amp;sort=desc" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: geipan.fr">[Geipan]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Geipan[AERO CTL] COGNAC (16) 17.12.1957 &#124; GEIPAN...</span></span></span>

**A change in displayed range is not automatically a complete flight path.** The calculation assumes that the echo represented one physical target moving continuously between the stated ranges during the stated period. If the displayed return shifted, faded, reappeared, merged with clutter or was mistakenly associated with another echo, the apparent movement would not describe the motion of a single object.

**The disappearance occurred among fixed echoes.** GEIPAN says the first return vanished at about 16 kilometres in the fixed echoes. Fixed or ground clutter consists of radar reflections from terrain, buildings and other stationary features. Such clutter can conceal a genuine aircraft return, but it can also make an uncertain target harder to follow or distinguish. The National Weather Service defines clutter as unwanted radar echoes that interfere with the observation of desired targets and notes that ground clutter may obscure other returns close to the radar.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://geipan.fr/en/node/46660?field_date_value=2004-04-23&amp;field_is_new_value=All&amp;field_is_revisited_value=All&amp;field_type_de_cas_target_id=All&amp;order=field_date_d_observation&amp;page=165&amp;sort=desc" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: geipan.fr">[Geipan]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Geipan[AERO CTL] COGNAC (16) 17.12.1957 &#124; GEIPAN...</span></span></span>

The neat repetition of exactly the same calculated speed for both tracks is intriguing, but it does not necessarily strengthen the case. It may reflect similar motion by two targets. It may also arise from rounded range readings and rounded observation times: 24 kilometres in 30 seconds and 12 kilometres in 15 seconds are mathematically identical ratios. With no raw timing record, it is unclear how precisely those intervals were measured.

The safe conclusion is therefore narrower than either a sensational or dismissive reading. Operators apparently saw short-lived moving echoes and considered them sufficiently unusual to document. The surviving information does not demonstrate that the radar accurately tracked solid craft travelling at 2,880 kilometres per hour.

## Radar echoes do not always represent aircraft

Radar is powerful precisely because it can detect objects that are difficult or impossible to see. It is not, however, an infallible camera. A display shows returned radio energy after it has interacted with the atmosphere, terrain, objects and the radar system itself. Operators must decide which returns correspond to aircraft and which are interference, clutter or propagation effects.

One established problem is anomalous propagation, sometimes called ducting. Under unusual temperature and humidity conditions, a radar beam can bend towards the ground instead of following its expected path. It may then reflect from distant terrain or structures and create unexpected blips. The US Federal Aviation Administration warns that anomalous propagation can produce numerous extraneous returns on an operators display.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.faa.gov/air_traffic/publications/atpubs/aip_html/part2_enr_section_1.1.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: faa.gov">[FAA]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">faa.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ENR 1.1: General RulesThe bending of radar pulses, often called anomalous propagation or ducting, may cause many extraneous blips to...</span></span></span>

Other possible sources of confusing radar returns include weather, birds, insects, interference from another transmitter, reflections outside the main radar beam and equipment faults. Modern systems use techniques such as Doppler processing, moving-target indication, transponder correlation and automated filtering to reduce these problems, although none removes every ambiguity. The Met Office notes that hills, buildings, birds and insects can all interfere with radar observations, while its technical material also identifies military chaff and interference from other radars as sources of non-meteorological or spurious echoes.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.metoffice.gov.uk/research/weather/observations-research/raindrops-and-radar" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: metoffice.gov.uk">[Met Office]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">metoffice.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Met Office Raindrops and radar</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Met Office Raindrops and radar</span></span></span>

These general possibilities should not be mistaken for a specific solution to Cognac. There is no surviving analysis demonstrating that anomalous propagation, birds, weather or interference caused the December 1957 tracks. The weather entry mentioning patches of fog is not enough by itself to establish unusual radio propagation. Such an assessment would require atmospheric profiles, local meteorological observations and the radars frequency and elevation settings.

The point is instead evidential: a radar return alone does not prove the presence, shape or performance of a physical vehicle. To discriminate between an aircraft and a false or misleading return, investigators need the technical information missing from Cognacs public file.

## No visual or airborne confirmation

The absence of a visual sighting is central to GEIPANs assessment. No ground observer is recorded as seeing a light or object corresponding to either radar track, even though the reported returns approached to within 16 and 25 kilometres of the radar site. GEIPAN explicitly cites the lack of visual observation when assigning category C.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://geipan.fr/en/node/46660?field_date_value=2004-04-23&amp;field_is_new_value=All&amp;field_is_revisited_value=All&amp;field_type_de_cas_target_id=All&amp;order=field_date_d_observation&amp;page=165&amp;sort=desc" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: geipan.fr">[Geipan]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Geipan[AERO CTL] COGNAC (16) 17.12.1957 &#124; GEIPAN...</span></span></span>

This absence is not decisive on its own. An aircraft might be hidden by cloud, mist, distance or poor viewing geometry, and the witness metadata mentions patches of fog. Radar is routinely used because visual identification is often impossible.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://geipan.fr/en/node/46658" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: geipan.fr">[Geipan]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Geipan[AERO CTL] COGNAC (16) 17.12.1957 T1 &#124; GEIPAN...</span></span></span>

Nevertheless, independent confirmation would have greatly strengthened the incident. A simultaneous sighting could have established direction, apparent form, lighting and angular motion. An airborne interception could have provided pilot testimony or aircraft radar data. Correlation with another ground radar could have shown that the return was not peculiar to one installation. None of these supporting strands appears in the published record.

There is also no publicly documented sonic boom, physical effect or operational response matching an object supposedly travelling at roughly 2.3 times the speed of sound near the base. That does not disprove the track: altitude, trajectory, atmospheric conditions and the accuracy of the speed estimate are all unknown. It does show why the numerical claim cannot be treated as a fully observed high-speed passage.

## How Cognacs military aviation role shapes the debate

The setting gives the report more weight than an anecdote from an untrained observer. Cognac was an active French military aviation site, and the people watching the display were military personnel using operational equipment. Their decision to record the event suggests that they did not immediately recognise the returns as routine traffic or familiar clutter.

The base was also closely connected with fast military aircraft and bombing operations during this period. Historical accounts place a bombardment wing at Cognac from 1957 to 1961, while the site had previously hosted jet reconnaissance aircraft. It was therefore an environment in which radar personnel and aircrew had practical reasons to distinguish military traffic from unexpected targets.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://fr.wikipedia.org/wiki/Base_a%C3%A9rienne_709_Cognac-Ch%C3%A2teaubernard" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Base arienne 709 Cognac-Chteaubernard</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Base arienne 709 Cognac-Chteaubernard</span></span></span>

This context supports the witnesses competence, but it does not automatically validate every interpretation made from the display. Experienced operators can recognise many common artefacts, yet unusual propagation and equipment behaviour can still mislead trained personnel. The FAAs operational guidance treats anomalous propagation as a normal radar phenomenon requiring technical mitigation, not as a mistake confined to inexperienced observers.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.faa.gov/air_traffic/publications/atpubs/aip_html/part2_enr_section_1.1.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: faa.gov">[FAA]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">faa.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ENR 1.1: General RulesThe bending of radar pulses, often called anomalous propagation or ducting, may cause many extraneous blips to...</span></span></span>

Military surroundings also introduce ordinary possibilities that may not be recoverable from a public UFO file. Training flights, classified movements, radar calibration exercises, electronic countermeasures or incomplete traffic records could all affect what appeared on a screen. No evidence currently identifies one of these as the Cognac explanation. Their relevance is that the airbase was not an empty background: it was a busy technical environment whose records would be essential to a proper investigation.

The military provenance therefore cuts both ways. It makes the report worthy of attention because trained personnel documented instrument returns. It also raises the evidential standard, because conclusions should depend on technical logs and operational records rather than the authority of the witnesses alone.

## Why GEIPAN leaves the case unresolved but weak

GEIPANs category C does not mean that the incident has been identified as a radar fault or conventional aircraft. It means there is insufficient reliable information to decide. The case is unresolved in the everyday sense, but it is not a category D case in which an adequately investigated phenomenon remains unidentified after available explanations have been tested.

The distinction prevents two common misreadings. The first is that an official unexplained file confirms extraordinary technology. Cognac does not carry that official assessment. The second is that missing evidence proves nothing happened. The military report indicates that radar operators did observe returns they considered unusual; what remains uncertain is the nature of those returns.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://geipan.fr/en/node/46660?field_date_value=2004-04-23&amp;field_is_new_value=All&amp;field_is_revisited_value=All&amp;field_type_de_cas_target_id=All&amp;order=field_date_d_observation&amp;page=165&amp;sort=desc" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: geipan.fr">[Geipan]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Geipan[AERO CTL] COGNAC (16) 17.12.1957 &#124; GEIPAN...</span></span></span>

It has preserved useful detailstimes, ranges, durations, witness numbers and sketchesbut has not produced the technical records needed to validate the claimed velocity. ## The fairest reading of the Cognac echoes

The Cognac incident deserves its prominent place in Charentes UFO history because it was recorded by military personnel and involved two apparently rapid radar tracks rather than a fleeting light seen by a lone witness. It is more technically suggestive than most local reports.

Its weakness is equally specific. The radar returns were not supported by visual observation, secondary radar, another station, an interception or preserved raw measurements. The published speed depends on rounded distances and times, and the surviving archive contains an unresolved date discrepancy. Known radar effects show that unexpected moving or shifting echoes cannot automatically be equated with high-performance vehicles, although no single artefact has been demonstrated as the cause here.

The most evidence-led verdict is therefore that Cognac recorded an unusual but inadequately documented radar event. Something appeared on the display; the public record cannot reliably say what generated it, how it moved or whether the figure of 2,880 kilometres per hour described a real object. That makes the case historically interesting, officially indeterminate and scientifically too weak to support the extraordinary claim often built around it.
