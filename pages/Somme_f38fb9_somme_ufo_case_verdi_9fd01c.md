## Introduction

[Somme]({{ 'why-sommes-ufo-record-still-resists-easy/' | relative_url }})’s official UFO record is less a catalogue of mysteries than a demonstration of how evidence changes a verdict. Some reports that sounded dramatic at first were later matched to Venus, airliners, advertising lasers, the International Space Station or floating lanterns. Other cases were placed in category C because photographs, timings or witness detail were too poor to support any conclusion. Only a small historical core, notably Harponville–Contay and Doullens in 1954, remains officially unexplained after review.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/fr/cas/1954-09-00008" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Le temps est...</span></span></span>

That distinction matters. In GEIPAN’s system, “unexplained” does not mean “extraterrestrial”, while “lacking an explanation” does not automatically mean a case is genuinely strange. A report can remain unidentified because it is strong enough to resist ordinary explanations, or because it is too incomplete to investigate. Somme contains clear examples of both outcomes, as well as cases in which later calculations substantially weakened the original interpretation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/fr/actualites/methodologie-classification-geipan" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-title">methodologie classification geipan</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">methodologie classification geipan</span></span></span>

## What the GEIPAN categories actually mean

GEIPAN, the French national space agency’s specialist unit for unidentified aerospace phenomena, classifies cases by combining two questions. First, how well does a known explanation account for the observation after investigation? Second, how consistent is the evidence, meaning how much reliable information is actually available? Consistency can be strengthened by prompt reporting, several independent witnesses, precise directions, photographs, radar records or detailed contemporary documents. It is weakened by long delays, vague times, uncertain locations, dependent witnesses and poor-quality images.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/fr/actualites/methodologie-classification-geipan" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-title">methodologie classification geipan</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">methodologie classification geipan</span></span></span>

The familiar letter grades therefore describe different evidential situations:

Some D cases are further divided into D1 and D2 according to their strangeness and evidential strength. D1 generally denotes an unusual but moderately supported report; D2 requires both high strangeness and much stronger consistency, such as independent testimony, recordings or physical traces. GEIPAN also stresses that classifications other than a firmly established A can change when new information or better analytical methods become available.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/fr/actualites/methodologie-classification-geipan" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-title">methodologie classification geipan</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">methodologie classification geipan</span></span></span>

This prevents two common misunderstandings. Category B is not simply a dismissive label: investigators may have reconstructed a highly convincing explanation but lack the data needed to identify the exact aircraft or lantern launch. Conversely, category C is not a reservoir of especially puzzling UFOs. It often contains the weakest reports in the archive.

## Unexplained is not the same as uninvestigable

The contrast between Somme’s category C and category D files is the clearest way to understand the department’s record. Both groups lack a final identification, but for almost opposite reasons.

A D case contains enough coherent material for investigators to recognise that something unusual was reported, yet no ordinary hypothesis adequately accounts for the whole description. A C case fails earlier in the process: investigators cannot establish the geometry, chronology or reliability required to compare the event properly with aircraft, astronomical objects or atmospheric effects. GEIPAN describes category C as an inability to conclude, not as evidence that the reported phenomenon defied explanation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/fr/actualites/methodologie-classification-geipan" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-title">methodologie classification geipan</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">methodologie classification geipan</span></span></span>

The 1996 Allery case is a straightforward Somme example. An amateur astronomer noticed unexplained dark traces on photographs taken while observing a comet. The images were too difficult to interpret, and there was no separate visual observation or dependable information establishing when and how the marks were produced. GEIPAN therefore classified the case C for lack of reliable evidence. The traces remained unidentified, but there was no strong basis for treating them as objects in the sky rather than photographic, processing or equipment-related artefacts.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cnes-geipan.fr/fr/cas/1996-05-01422?page=111&amp;undefined=" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on cnes-geipan.fr.</span></span></span>

Several reports from the [1954 wave]({{ '1954-wave-5cfde0/' | relative_url }}) have the same evidential problem. Cases at Le Crotoy, Hem-HardINVAL and Long were preserved because witnesses described unusual discs or coloured lights, but the surviving information was insufficient for a meaningful modern reconstruction. Missing angular measurements, incomplete timings and limited corroboration make it impossible to test many possible explanations. Their category C status says more about the condition of the historical files than about the physical nature of what was seen.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/fr/actualites/baisse-cas-d" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-title">baisse cas d</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">baisse cas d</span></span></span>

This is especially important with old newspaper reports. A vivid phrase such as “flying disc” can outlive the practical details needed to assess it: exact viewing direction, duration, cloud cover, whether the witness was moving and whether another observer saw the phenomenon independently. Once those details are lost, neither believers nor sceptics can reconstruct the event securely.

## Why Harponville–Contay stayed open

The Harponville–Contay encounter of 7 September 1954 remained unexplained because its surviving record is substantially richer than most category C reports. Two men cycling to work independently described a dark circular or cylindrical form in a field about 200 metres from the road. They approached to roughly 100 metres and said that it rose silently, first at an angle and then vertically, with grey-blue smoke apparently emerging from a tube. Their observation reportedly lasted four or five minutes, and they went promptly to the gendarmerie.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/fr/cas/1954-09-00008" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Le temps est...</span></span></span>

Those features give investigators more to work with than a single distant light. There were two direct witnesses, a daylight setting, an approximate distance, a prolonged observation and a contemporary police record. Yet the file contains no photograph, radar record or physical object. Investigators found no trace in the field, while estimates of size and distance still depended on human perception.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/fr/cas/1954-09-00008" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Le temps est...</span></span></span>

Ordinary possibilities can be proposed, but none has been demonstrated from the surviving evidence. Agricultural machinery, a balloon, an unusual aircraft or a misunderstanding of a more distant object might account for selected features, but each leaves difficulties, particularly the reported rise from the field, silence and apparent exhaust. The official D verdict therefore means that GEIPAN did not find an explanation with sufficient probability. It does not certify that a structured craft landed there, still less establish its origin.

The lack of ground traces is relevant but not decisive. It weakens any claim that a heavy object physically rested in the field, yet the witnesses’ account does not provide enough technical information to estimate its weight, contact area or exact position. The case remains one of Somme’s stronger historical reports because the testimony is comparatively detailed, not because it contains conclusive material evidence.

## Why Doullens remained difficult

The Doullens case of 19 October 1954 is open for different reasons. Several military personnel at a radar station saw a coloured luminous phenomenon beneath an estimated stratocumulus layer. The account describes rapid movement, an abrupt stop, several minutes of apparent hovering and repeated colour changes. More than one observer saw the stationary phase after the sentry alerted his superiors.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/fr/cas/1954-10-09217" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">GEIPANDOULLENS (80) 19.10.1954Le mardi 19 octobre 1954 entre 1h30 et 1h40 plusieurs militaires (dont un 2e classe, un caporal-chef et un...</span></span></span>

The witnesses’ military roles and the structured reporting add consistency, but they do not remove the case’s limitations. The station’s radar was not operating that night, so there was no instrumental track to confirm distance, altitude or speed. Estimates such as an 800-metre altitude depended on the assumed cloud base and the belief that the light was beneath it. A bright source farther away could produce very different physical dimensions and velocities.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/fr/cas/1954-10-09217" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">GEIPANDOULLENS (80) 19.10.1954Le mardi 19 octobre 1954 entre 1h30 et 1h40 plusieurs militaires (dont un 2e classe, un caporal-chef et un...</span></span></span>

Meteoric or astronomical explanations struggle with the reported stop and prolonged stationary phase. An aircraft might explain changing coloured lights and some apparent movement, but the witnesses described acceleration and manoeuvres that they did not associate with conventional aviation. Atmospheric distortion could alter the colour and apparent motion of a distant source, although it does not neatly reproduce every phase of the testimony.

The result is an unresolved case with respectable witnesses but no radar confirmation and no dependable range measurement. Doullens illustrates why occupational credibility cannot substitute for physical data. Trained personnel may describe an observation carefully, yet they still cannot derive an object’s true speed or size from angular movement alone when its distance is unknown.

## Venus could appear to follow a patrol car

Some of Somme’s most instructive resolved cases initially sounded more dramatic than the unexplained historical files. At Roye on 13 May 1980, two gendarmes patrolling the A1 motorway saw a bright object near the toll area. Other officers also confirmed a light in the sky. The main witnesses observed it for almost an hour and felt that it followed their vehicle as they drove north. Photographs were attempted, although the developed film yielded nothing useful.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cnes-geipan.fr/fr/cas/1980-05-00774?page=140" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on cnes-geipan.fr.</span></span></span>

The case was originally placed in category C, but a later review matched the phenomenon to Venus. Its position, brightness, shape, colour and long duration fitted the planet, which was present in the relevant part of the sky but was not identified by the witnesses. GEIPAN reclassified the report as category A.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cnes-geipan.fr/fr/cas/1980-05-00774?page=140" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on cnes-geipan.fr.</span></span></span>

The apparent pursuit is a familiar perceptual effect. A very distant light has almost no visible parallax when an observer moves over a modest distance. Nearby trees, signs and buildings sweep past, while the remote source stays in approximately the same direction. From a moving car, this can create the compelling impression that the light is keeping pace.

The reclassification did not require investigators to doubt the officers’ honesty or eyesight. GEIPAN explicitly separated perception from interpretation: the witnesses really saw a bright light, but their sense of its distance and behaviour was misleading. The case demonstrates why witness credibility and witness accuracy are related but not identical questions.

A more recent Somme report provides unusually clear confirmation of the same mechanism. At Quesnoy-le-Montant on 16 September 2023, a witness watched and filmed an intense white light for about 90 minutes before dawn. Astronomical reconstruction placed Venus at the reported direction and elevation, with exceptional brightness at the time. Smartphone zoom produced blurred circles and concentric patterns that looked more structured than the point seen by the naked eye. GEIPAN classified the case A in 2024.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cnes-geipan.fr/fr/cas/2023-09-51474" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on cnes-geipan.fr.</span></span></span>

Here, extensive video did not strengthen an extraordinary interpretation. It strengthened the ordinary one because investigators could compare the timing, direction and apparent movement with astronomical software. The strange shapes in the recording were consistent with defocus and diffraction from a camera not designed to resolve a bright point source at night.

## Aircraft can look stationary, triangular or silent

Aircraft explanations recur in Somme because viewing geometry can conceal the features by which people normally recognise a plane. An aircraft flying nearly towards an observer may appear almost motionless for a time. At night, its body and wings can disappear while its lights suggest a triangle or another solid outline. Engine noise may arrive late, be masked by traffic or wind, or remain faint when the aircraft is high and distant.

At Ailly-sur-Noye on 22 October 2013, a motorist reported three coloured lights defining a triangular form. It initially seemed stationary, then moved slowly and disappeared. Investigators found a regular Venice–London flight in the appropriate direction and time window. The reported lights were compatible with aircraft navigation lights, while temporary and final disappearances could be reproduced by the airliner passing behind broken cloud.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cnes-geipan.fr/fr/cas/2013-10-08598?field_classification_des_cas_target_id%5B0%5D=12&amp;order=title&amp;page=%2C157&amp;sort=asc" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on cnes-geipan.fr.</span></span></span>

GEIPAN classified the case B rather than A because the precise aircraft time and the witness’s reported light colours retained some uncertainty. This is a useful example of disciplined caution: the airliner explanation accounts for the principal features, but the evidence does not amount to an exact identification beyond reasonable doubt.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cnes-geipan.fr/fr/cas/2013-10-08598?field_classification_des_cas_target_id%5B0%5D=12&amp;order=title&amp;page=%2C157&amp;sort=asc" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on cnes-geipan.fr.</span></span></span>

A similar approach was applied to the Feuquières-en-Vimeu report of 10 November 2012, although GEIPAN ultimately favoured lanterns rather than an aircraft. The witness described a dark triangle marked by four orange lights, a right-angle turn and sudden high-speed departure. The investigation treated the apparent solid form and acceleration as interpretations that could arise when separate lights maintained a temporary formation and then extinguished or drifted into different wind layers.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cnes-geipan.fr/fr/cas/2012-11-09213" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on cnes-geipan.fr.</span></span></span>

These cases show why investigators do not take a witness’s description of a “triangle” as automatic evidence of one triangular object. The first task is to determine whether the outline was directly visible or mentally completed between several lights.

## Lanterns turned formations into probable identifications

Floating lanterns became a frequent source of modern UFO reports because they combine several apparently unusual qualities. They are orange, silent, capable of travelling in loose groups and bright enough to stand out against a dark sky. Their speed and direction follow the wind, while the extinction of the flame can resemble sudden disappearance or rapid recession.

At Bertangles in July 2010, a couple watched between 15 and 20 orange lights move from south to north over roughly 15 minutes. Photographs were taken and passed into the investigation. GEIPAN concluded that the images left little ambiguity that the lights were flying lanterns, although the published classification remained B, expressing a highly probable rather than absolutely demonstrated identification.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cnes-geipan.fr/fr/cas/2010-07-02617" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on cnes-geipan.fr.</span></span></span>

The Feuquières-en-Vimeu investigation shows how several clues are combined rather than relying on colour alone. The lights were yellow-orange, moved quietly at low apparent speed, appeared on a Saturday evening and lasted for less than a minute. Investigators considered the apparent acceleration to be compatible with extinction and loss of visual reference. A perceived 90-degree turn could have resulted from changes in wind direction at different heights.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cnes-geipan.fr/fr/cas/2012-11-09213" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on cnes-geipan.fr.</span></span></span>

Later Somme cases at Ham, Péronne and Contay were also judged probably to involve lanterns. The repetition is historically useful because it explains why groups of orange lights became less mysterious once investigators had accumulated comparison material. An unfamiliar sight in one year became a recognisable pattern after launch behaviour, photographs and wind-driven movement had been documented in many reports.

This does not mean that every silent orange light must be a lantern. A convincing identification still depends on matching direction, duration, number, wind and appearance. The Somme files nevertheless show that lanterns provide a strong explanation when several of those features occur together.

## A garage test settled the Abbeville laser case

The Abbeville-area case of 18 November 1995 demonstrates the value of reproducing an alleged phenomenon. A witness saw large luminous circles moving through the sky. Investigators discovered that a nearby Opel garage had been using a promotional laser projector during an advertising campaign.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cnes-geipan.fr/fr/cas/1995-11-01404" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on cnes-geipan.fr.</span></span></span>

Rather than stopping at a plausible coincidence, investigators arranged a test. When the projector was operated again, it reproduced the reported display. GEIPAN classified the case A.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cnes-geipan.fr/fr/cas/1995-11-01404" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on cnes-geipan.fr.</span></span></span>

This is stronger than a generic suggestion that “it may have been a searchlight”. The source was located, its operation corresponded with the sighting and an experimental repeat generated the same type of effect. Low cloud can act as a moving screen for beams or projected circles, creating apparent objects that seem to cross or rotate in the sky.

The case also illustrates why nearby commercial activity belongs in a UFO investigation. Nightclub spotlights, event lasers and dealership promotions may be omitted from aviation or astronomical databases, yet they can produce striking regional reports. Local checks sometimes resolve a case more decisively than complex technical modelling.

## Photographs help only when their origin is clear

Somme’s files caution against treating every photograph or video as independent confirmation. Images can be valuable when they preserve direction, timing and movement, as in the Quesnoy-le-Montant review. They can also reproduce camera artefacts more clearly than the actual celestial source.

Allery represents the opposite situation. The unexplained marks appeared on astronomical photographs but were not seen directly, and the images were too poor for reliable interpretation. Without a clear chain linking a mark to an external object, a photograph may record dust, processing damage, internal reflection, motion, defocus or another artefact.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cnes-geipan.fr/fr/cas/1996-05-01422?page=111&amp;undefined=" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on cnes-geipan.fr.</span></span></span>

The Fort-Mahon-Plage case of July 2010 followed a similar evidential pattern: unusual details were noticed only afterwards in a film rather than during the original scene. Such retrospective discoveries are difficult to reconstruct because the observer cannot provide a direct visual comparison, and the camera’s settings or movement may not be known.

The practical lesson is that image quantity is less important than image context. Investigators need the original file, exposure information, exact location, viewing direction, time, camera movement and ideally photographs of the same scene taken under controlled conditions. A blurry enlargement detached from those details may add less evidence than a precise written account made immediately after the event.

## Later reviews changed the meaning of older files

Roye shows that GEIPAN’s verdicts are snapshots of the available evidence, not permanent declarations. The case moved from C to A when a later review applied astronomical reconstruction and a more explicit analysis of perception.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cnes-geipan.fr/fr/cas/1980-05-00774?page=140" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on cnes-geipan.fr.</span></span></span>

GEIPAN has explained that its classification methods became more systematic after 2008, with greater emphasis on residual strangeness, consistency and the probability of competing hypotheses. Digital astronomy tools, satellite tracking, flight information and easier access to meteorological records have made it possible to revisit cases that earlier investigators could not resolve confidently.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/fr/actualites/methodologie-classification-geipan" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-title">methodologie classification geipan</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">methodologie classification geipan</span></span></span>

Reassessment can work in either direction. Better information may identify Venus or an aircraft, but it can also show that an apparently weak case contains several coherent, independent statements. Historical reviews remain constrained, however, because investigators cannot return to the scene with the original witnesses decades later. GEIPAN has acknowledged that old cases may sit close to the boundary between D and C when a modern field reconstruction is no longer possible.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cnes-geipan.fr/sites/default/files/Compte%20rendu%20enquete6.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Compte rendu enquete6</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Compte rendu enquete6</span></span></span>

This limitation is particularly relevant to Somme’s 1954 reports. Harponville–Contay and Doullens retain enough detail to remain officially unexplained, but their D classification is not equivalent to the evidential standard that a new case might reach after prompt interviews, measured sight lines, digital records and a modern site investigation.

## What the Somme verdicts really establish

Taken together, the Somme files support a graded rather than binary judgement. A few cases remain open because coherent testimony describes features that have not been matched convincingly to a known source. A larger number are unresolved only in the weaker sense that essential information is missing. Many modern reports became ordinary once investigators checked astronomy, flight paths, promotional lighting, wind and photographic behaviour.

The strongest explanations are those that reproduce several independent features at once. Venus at Roye matched direction, duration, brightness and apparent pursuit. The Ailly-sur-Noye airliner matched timing, azimuth, navigation lights, movement and cloud disappearance. The Abbeville laser was recreated experimentally. The Quesnoy-le-Montant analysis matched both the planet’s position and the camera artefacts visible in the recordings.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cnes-geipan.fr/fr/cas/1980-05-00774?page=140" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on cnes-geipan.fr.</span></span></span>

By contrast, Harponville–Contay and Doullens remain open because no single hypothesis has yet accounted for their central reported features without relying on unsupported assumptions. Their status preserves a genuine lack of explanation, but not proof of an extraordinary vehicle.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/fr/cas/1954-09-00008" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Le temps est...</span></span></span>

The most accurate reading of Somme’s UFO history is therefore neither that every case was debunked nor that official investigators confirmed unexplained craft. The archive shows a spectrum: secure identifications, probable identifications, unusable reports and a small residue of cases whose surviving evidence remains unusual but incomplete.
