## Introduction

France’s official GEIPAN archive supports the view that people in [Yonne]({{ 'why-yonnes-ufo-history-looks-stranger/' | relative_url }}) have repeatedly seen genuinely puzzling things in the sky. It does not, however, provide strong evidence that the department is a centre of extraordinary or technologically inexplicable activity. The clearest pattern is more ordinary: bright stars mistaken for hovering objects, satellites, meteors, balloons, atmospheric re-entry and floating lanterns, alongside reports too incomplete for investigators to decide what happened.



<img src="{{ "/assets/images/Yonne_77d4a6_yonne_geipan_record_ba8194-overview.webp" | relative_url }}" alt="Overview image for Official Record" loading="eager" decoding="sync" fetchpriority="high">

That distinction matters. GEIPAN, the unit within France’s CNES space agency that investigates unidentified aerospace phenomena, records what witnesses reported rather than treating every report as proof of an unknown craft. Its files therefore offer something local folklore cannot: a way to separate a memorable experience from the strength of the evidence behind it. In Yonne, the official record contains sincere and sometimes vivid testimony, but comparatively little that remains both well documented and unexplained after investigation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/en/actualites/methodologie-classification-geipan" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-title">GEIPANLa méthodologie de classification au GEIPAN &#124; GEIPAN</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">GEIPANLa méthodologie de classification au GEIPAN &#124; GEIPAN</span></span></span>

## How GEIPAN decides whether a case is mysterious

GEIPAN’s familiar A, B, C and D labels are not a simple scale running from “uninteresting” to “alien”. Investigators assess two separate qualities: the remaining strangeness of the observation after possible explanations have been tested, and the consistency of the evidence. Consistency includes the number and independence of witnesses, the detail and coherence of their accounts, photographs, accurate directions and timings, and other information that can be checked.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/en/actualites/methodologie-classification-geipan" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-title">GEIPANLa méthodologie de classification au GEIPAN &#124; GEIPAN</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">GEIPANLa méthodologie de classification au GEIPAN &#124; GEIPAN</span></span></span>

The resulting categories are best read as follows:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* **A:** the phenomenon has been identified with high confidence.
* **B:** an ordinary explanation is considered probable, although not completely demonstrated.
* **C:** the information is too incomplete or unreliable to support a conclusion.
* **D:** the case remains unexplained after investigation and has enough consistency to justify that status.

</div>

Category C is the one most easily misunderstood. It does not mean that investigators have confirmed a baffling phenomenon. It means that they cannot make a sound judgement. A distant light reported without a precise time, direction, duration or angular movement may remain unidentified simply because the necessary information was never obtained. GEIPAN explicitly describes C cases as non-exploitable: neither an explanation nor genuine residual strangeness can be established.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/en/actualites/methodologie-classification-geipan" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-title">GEIPANLa méthodologie de classification au GEIPAN &#124; GEIPAN</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">GEIPANLa méthodologie de classification au GEIPAN &#124; GEIPAN</span></span></span>

This method explains why the Yonne files can contain striking witness language without producing an equally striking official conclusion. The archive asks a different question from local legend. It does not ask whether the experience felt extraordinary; it asks whether the recorded details exclude ordinary causes.

## The Yonne record is widespread, but not concentrated

Reports indexed under Yonne are associated with places across the department, including Auxerre, Avallon, Brion, Joigny, Monéteau, Pont-sur-Yonne, Saint-Julien-du-Sault, Sens and smaller rural communes. The distribution does not reveal an obvious official hotspot centred on a single airfield, military site or narrow geographical corridor. Instead, it resembles a broad collection of observations made from homes, roads, villages and open countryside.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.geipan.fr/fr/recherche/cas/tab?field_agregation_index_value=&amp;field_classification_des_cas_target_id%5B0%5D=13&amp;field_classification_des_cas_target_id%5B1%5D=14&amp;field_date_d_observation_value%5Bmax%5D=&amp;field_date_d_observation_value%5Bmin%5D=&amp;field_departement_target_id=&amp;field_document_existe_ou_pas_value=All&amp;field_latitude_value%5Bmax%5D=&amp;field_latitude_value%5Bmin%5D=&amp;field_longitude_value%5Bmax%5D=&amp;field_longitude_value%5Bmin%5D=&amp;field_phenomene_target_id=&amp;field_type_de_cas_target_id=All&amp;order=field_departement_textuel&amp;page=53&amp;select-category-export=nothing&amp;sort=asc" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: geipan.fr">[Geipan]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Recherche de casListe des cas; [N77] DE NEUVY-SAUTOUR (89) VERS SAINT-FLORENTIN (89) 11.05.2023, 11/05/2023, C · Yonne, Manque d&#x27;i...</span></span></span>

Nor does the archive show one distinctive “Yonne phenomenon”. Witnesses have reported stationary points, triangular arrangements of lights, fast luminous bodies, orange objects, silent formations and distant shapes. Yet similar descriptions can arise from very different causes. A triangle may be three separate lights rather than one solid object. An apparently stationary object may be a star. A brilliant green body with a trail may be a meteor. A silent orange light may be a paper lantern moving with the wind.

The public index also spans several decades. Entries include cases from the 1970s and early 1980s, later observations around Joigny and Pont-sur-Yonne, lantern reports in the 2010s, identified objects in 2019 and newer files from the 2020s. This long time range demonstrates continued reporting, not necessarily continued anomalous activity. Changes in available evidence, reporting routes, public awareness, photography and investigative practice make raw comparisons between decades unreliable.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.geipan.fr/fr/recherche/cas/tab?field_classification_des_cas_target_id%5B0%5D=12&amp;order=field_date_d_observation_textuel&amp;page=9&amp;sort=desc" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: geipan.fr">[Geipan]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">YONNE (89) 30.09.1977 &#124; 26/09/1977 &#124; B &#124; Yonne &#124; Phénomène identifié non indiqué &#124; 27/09/2010 &#124; 1977-09-26... ## Stars and cameras can create convincing “craft”

The Auxerre observation of 17 September 2019 is one of the most useful Yonne files because the witness supplied more information than is available in many brief reports. At about 5.45 am, a bright white phenomenon appeared stationary in the early-morning sky. It seemed to change intensity and was described as having fainter lines that produced a V or triangular form. The object remained visible for more than an hour, and photographs appeared to show three lights arranged in a V.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/fr/cas/2019-09-50826" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on cnes-geipan.fr.</span></span></span>

GEIPAN reconstructed the viewing direction and compared it with the sky at the stated time. Sirius, the brightest star in the night sky, occupied the corresponding area and followed a movement consistent with the observation. Its atmospheric scintillation explained the apparent changes in brightness. The supposed V-shape in the photographs was attributed chiefly to camera movement: enlarging an unsteady image had turned a point source into an apparently structured pattern. The case was classified A.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/fr/cas/2019-09-50826" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on cnes-geipan.fr.</span></span></span>

The importance of the Auxerre file goes beyond identifying one star. It illustrates why photographs do not automatically make a case stronger. A blurred or hand-held exposure can introduce streaks, duplicated points and geometric forms that the eye did not clearly see. In this instance, the supporting material allowed investigators to explain both the original light and the more dramatic feature that appeared in the images.

The case also shows that witness credibility and mistaken identification are not opposites. GEIPAN considered the testimony reasonably consistent and detailed. The conclusion was not that the witness invented the event, but that a real astronomical object, atmospheric effects and photographic artefacts combined to produce an unfamiliar impression.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/fr/cas/2019-09-50826" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on cnes-geipan.fr.</span></span></span>

## Lanterns explain several silent orange lights

Floating lanterns recur in Yonne’s official files because they reproduce several features often associated with UFO reports: silence, warm orange or reddish light, slow or uncertain movement and apparently coordinated formations.

At Brion on 3 July 2010, a witness described a flame-red object that initially appeared stationary before moving silently southwards. GEIPAN noted the summer weekend timing, the proximity of a venue used for celebrations and weddings, the flame-like colour and the object’s behaviour. Even the reported helical appearance could be reproduced by seeing the structure of a lantern from below. The evidence was limited to one witness and a relatively sparse gendarmerie statement, so the lantern explanation was judged probable rather than certain and the case was classified B.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.geipan.fr/fr/cas/2010-07-02603" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: geipan.fr">[Geipan]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on geipan.fr.</span></span></span>

A similar interpretation was reached at Monéteau on 26 August 2016. A couple saw three orange lights passing silently in a triangular arrangement before trees blocked the view. GEIPAN compared their direction of travel with local wind information and found it compatible with low-altitude lanterns. The observation lasted only seconds, leaving room for errors in estimated speed and distance, but the colour, grouping, silence and summer-evening setting fitted the lantern hypothesis. The case was also classified B.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.geipan.fr/fr/cas/2016-08-09538" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: geipan.fr">[Geipan]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on geipan.fr.</span></span></span>

These examples are especially relevant to claims of triangular objects. Human perception naturally links lights moving together and interprets the empty space between them as a surface. Three independent lanterns can therefore be remembered as the corners of one large object. The official files do not prove that every triangular sighting in Yonne has this cause, but they show that geometry alone is weak evidence for a solid craft.

## Meteors and space debris can look close and controlled

The Joigny case of 30 March 2009 involved a very different type of observation. A family travelling by car saw a fast, silent, pale-green oval moving in a straight line with a silver trail. It then disappeared abruptly. Those details closely match a bright meteor, or bolide: high speed, intense colour, a luminous trail and a brief passage ending suddenly. GEIPAN classified the case B because no independent reports were available to confirm the reconstruction.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://geipan.fr/fr/cas/2009-03-02448?field_agregation_index_value=&amp;field_date_d_observation_value%5Bmax%5D=&amp;field_date_d_observation_value%5Bmin%5D=&amp;field_date_value=&amp;field_departement_target_id=&amp;field_document_existe_ou_pas_value=All&amp;field_is_new_value=All&amp;field_is_revisited_value=All&amp;field_latitude_value%5Bmax%5D=&amp;field_latitude_value%5Bmin%5D=&amp;field_longitude_value%5Bmax%5D=&amp;field_longitude_value%5Bmin%5D=&amp;field_phenomene_target_id=&amp;field_type_de_cas_target_id=116&amp;order=field_departement_textuel&amp;page=160&amp;select-category-export=nothing&amp;sort=asc" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: geipan.fr">[Geipan]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on geipan.fr.</span></span></span>

The case demonstrates why a probable explanation may remain below category A. A description can fit a known phenomenon extremely well while still lacking corroborating data such as another witness position, an astronomical detection or a precise trajectory. “Probably explained” is therefore not a dismissive label; it records both the strength of the hypothesis and the surviving evidential gap.

The most dramatic example affecting Yonne belongs to the national wave of 5 November 1990. Witnesses across France, including around Vézelay, reported a procession of lights often perceived as an immense dark or triangular object flying at relatively low altitude. The event generated calls to gendarmerie units and CNES and was investigated through civil and military aviation authorities, meteorological services, orbital calculations and information from NASA.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://geipan.fr/fr/cas/1990-11-01225" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: geipan.fr">[Geipan]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on geipan.fr.</span></span></span>

The matched event was the atmospheric re-entry of debris associated with the third stage of a Soviet Proton rocket. The debris travelled across France at very high altitude, with separate incandescent fragments and trails extending over a broad area. Because observers had no reliable distance reference at night, the brilliant lights could appear much nearer than they were. Their common movement also encouraged the visual system to group them into the outline of one dark object. GEIPAN classifies the national event A.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://geipan.fr/fr/cas/1990-11-01225" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: geipan.fr">[Geipan]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on geipan.fr.</span></span></span>

This episode is central to interpreting Yonne’s record because it combined large numbers of sincere witnesses, apparently structured forms and a powerful sense of proximity. Independent orbital information nevertheless supplied a conventional explanation. Multiple testimony strengthened the evidence that something spectacular crossed the sky; it did not, by itself, establish the witnesses’ estimates of size, distance or physical structure.

## Satellites, balloons and the Moon broaden the pattern

Other indexed Yonne cases reinforce the same lesson. The Saint-Julien-du-Sault observation of 8 July 2012 is listed as a category B satellite case. At Gigny on 14 September 2019, the reported phenomenon was classified A and identified as a crewed gas balloon. More recent indexed material includes a September 2025 report at Vinneuf classified A as the Moon.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://geipan.fr/fr/recherche/cas/tab?field_agregation_index_value=&amp;field_date_d_observation_value%5Bmax%5D=&amp;field_date_d_observation_value%5Bmin%5D=&amp;field_date_value=2007-03-01&amp;field_departement_target_id=&amp;field_document_existe_ou_pas_value=All&amp;field_is_new_value=All&amp;field_is_revisited_value=All&amp;field_latitude_value%5Bmax%5D=&amp;field_latitude_value%5Bmin%5D=&amp;field_longitude_value%5Bmax%5D=&amp;field_longitude_value%5Bmin%5D=&amp;field_phenomene_target_id=&amp;field_type_de_cas_target_id=All&amp;order=field_departement_textuel&amp;page=3%2C33&amp;sort=desc" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: geipan.fr">[Geipan]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on geipan.fr.</span></span></span>

These explanations may sound obvious in hindsight, but they are not always obvious during an observation. Satellites can brighten suddenly when sunlight reflects from their surfaces. Balloons may appear motionless, change apparent direction relative to a moving observer or present an unfamiliar silhouette. The Moon, especially when seen through cloud, haze, trees or a vehicle window, can seem enlarged, distorted or oddly mobile.

The diversity of identified causes is more significant than any single explanation. It suggests that Yonne reports are not generated by one recurring hidden source. They arise from encounters with many unrelated objects and visual conditions that share only one quality: at the time, the witness could not recognise them.

## Incomplete cases reveal the archive’s limits

Some Yonne entries remain in category C. The Sens report dated 28 July 1981, for example, is catalogued as lacking reliable information. Pont-sur-Yonne’s 4 November 2008 case is similarly listed as C. A more recent report made during a journey from Neuvy-Sautour towards Saint-Florentin on 11 May 2023 also appears in the public index as a C case.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://geipan.fr/fr/cas/1981-07-00878" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: geipan.fr">[Geipan]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">SENS (89) 1981SENS (89) 1981. Date d&#x27;observation. 28/07/1981. Région. Bourgogne. Département. Yonne. Classification. C. Date de mis...</span></span></span>

Such files are tempting to describe as “unsolved”, but that wording can be misleading. They may remain open to several possibilities precisely because the archive lacks the material needed to test any of them. Missing directions, uncertain dates, delayed testimony, no independent witnesses or insufficient detail can prevent investigators from comparing a report with aircraft, astronomical objects, weather or satellite data.

A category C entry therefore contributes little to a claim that something exceptional occurred. It establishes that a report was received and could not be resolved from the available record. It does not establish that conventional explanations were excluded.

This is also where the official archive understates as well as restrains the local story. A weak file does not prove that the witness saw nothing unusual, only that the event cannot now be reconstructed confidently. GEIPAN’s caution protects against turning missing information into positive evidence, but it also means that potentially interesting observations may remain permanently unassessable.

## Does the official record support Yonne’s UFO folklore?

GEIPAN’s files support a limited version of the folklore. Yonne has a real history of unusual aerial reports. Witnesses have described impressive lights, apparent formations and objects that seemed silent, structured or close. Several observations were serious enough to produce questionnaires, gendarmerie statements, photographs, weather comparisons or astronomical reconstruction.

What the archive does not support is a strong claim that Yonne contains an exceptional concentration of well-evidenced unexplained phenomena. The most informative cases tend to move towards identification or a probable explanation because their times, directions, photographs and environmental conditions can be checked. The least informative cases remain unclassified in practical terms because the evidence is insufficient. The mystery often survives most strongly where the record is weakest.

The official picture is therefore neither “nothing happened” nor “the files prove extraordinary craft”. Something happened in each case at the level that matters to the witness: a light was seen, an unfamiliar shape was perceived or a striking event crossed the sky. But the better-documented Yonne examples repeatedly show how stars, camera movement, lanterns, meteors, satellites, balloons, the Moon and space debris can create experiences that are vivid, sincere and initially difficult to explain.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/fr/cas/2019-09-50826" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[Geipan]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on cnes-geipan.fr.</span></span></span>

For Yonne’s wider UFO history, that is the archive’s most valuable finding. It shifts attention away from the raw number or dramatic wording of reports and towards the quality of each case. A convincing departmental mystery would require accurate timings and locations, independent witnesses, reliable imagery or instrument data, and an investigation that eliminated plausible ordinary causes. In the publicly accessible Yonne material, those conditions rarely come together while the phenomenon remains unexplained.
