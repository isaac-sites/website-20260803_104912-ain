## Introduction

Most reported UFOs in [Hauts-de-Seine]({{ 'why-one-hauts-de-seine-ufo-case-still/' | relative_url }}) have turned out to be familiar objects or lighting effects seen under unfamiliar conditions. The department’s official files repeatedly identify aircraft, balloons, lanterns, satellites, drones, astronomical objects and beams reflected from low cloud. These explanations matter because the original observations were often genuinely puzzling: ordinary objects can appear silent, stationary, enormous or strangely shaped when viewed at night without reliable clues to their distance.



<img src="{{ "/assets/images/Hauts-de-Seine_2ccee8_explained_urban_sigh_1ab6e6-overview.webp" | relative_url }}" alt="Overview image for Explained Cases" loading="eager" decoding="sync" fetchpriority="high">

France’s official investigation service, GEIPAN, distinguishes between fully identified cases, probable identifications, reports that lack enough information and sightings that remain unexplained after investigation. Across its national archive, CNES currently reports 24.6 per cent as firmly identified and 39.7 per cent as probably identified, compared with 3.3 per cent left unexplained after investigation. The Hauts-de-Seine record broadly reflects that pattern, with its strongest modern evidence usually supporting mundane explanations rather than exotic craft.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/fr/missions-methodes-et-resultats" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-title">GEIPANMission &amp; Geipan &#124; GEIPAN</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">GEIPANMission &amp; Geipan &#124; GEIPAN</span></span></span>

## The objects most often mistaken for UFOs

### Aircraft approaching Paris

Aircraft are among the easiest objects to misread in the western Paris suburbs. An airliner approaching almost directly towards an observer may show powerful landing lights while its red, green and flashing navigation lights remain difficult to see. Because there is little sideways movement, it can appear to hover. When it turns, the previously “stationary” light may suddenly move away or vanish behind buildings.

A clear example occurred at Courbevoie on 12 March 2015. A witness filmed two lights that initially seemed fixed before moving in succession and disappearing behind the urban skyline. GEIPAN matched them with airliners descending towards an airport and classified the report as fully explained. Its investigation noted that forward-facing landing lights can remain visible from more than 20 kilometres away and can overpower the aircraft’s smaller navigation lights.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cnes-geipan.fr/fr/cas/2015-03-09130" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">On rappelle qu&#x27;un avion vu se préparant à atterrir, donc vu de face, peut être vu à plus de 20 km grâce à ses feux d&#x27;atterrissage, lesque...</span></span></span>

### Balloons, drifting debris and lanterns

Small windborne objects account for many reports that seem especially difficult to judge. A balloon has no fixed scale in the observer’s field of view, so its distance, altitude and speed cannot be calculated by eye. A small object a few hundred metres away can easily be interpreted as something large and distant.

At Sceaux on 4 June 2023, a witness saw and filmed a dark, rounded object apparently edged by flame-like features. GEIPAN judged it to be a metallic foil balloon. The shifting appearance was consistent with a reflective, irregular object rotating as it drifted through changing sunlight. The case was classified as fully identified.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cnes-geipan.fr/fr/node/60573" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on cnes-geipan.fr.</span></span></span>

Other local files show how broad this category can be. Three dark circular objects over Issy-les-Moulineaux in March 2008 were judged probably to be balloons of unknown origin, while a large dark form over Courbevoie in July 2012 was attributed to airborne debris carried at a constant speed by the wind. Reports of small silver objects at Clamart and yellow-orange forms at Châtillon were also assessed as probable balloons. The important clue in such cases is not merely that the object drifts, but that its direction and speed agree with measured winds.

Illuminated paper lanterns create a different but equally persuasive illusion. Their warm flame produces a steady orange or reddish glow; their bodies and supporting frames are usually invisible at night. They therefore appear as self-luminous spheres moving silently in formation. A lantern may brighten as it turns, fade in thin cloud or seem to disappear instantly when its flame goes out.

Four red lights seen from Montrouge shortly after midnight on 21 July 2009 displayed this familiar combination: low apparent altitude, silent movement, reddish colour and sudden extinction. GEIPAN classified the incident as a probable lantern release. Similar reports were made at nearby Bagneux the following night, at Clamart in December 2011 and at Asnières-sur-Seine in June 2014, where approximately 20 orange lights were seen on a Friday night in circumstances suggestive of a celebratory launch.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cnes-geipan.fr/fr/cas/2009-07-02349" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on cnes-geipan.fr.</span></span></span>

The number of lights does not make such an observation less conventional. A cluster often strengthens the lantern explanation because releases commonly occur at weddings, parties and public celebrations.

### Satellites and the International Space Station

Satellites are increasingly prominent in UFO reports because they are bright, silent and apparently unconnected to any visible structure. They cross the sky in smooth straight lines without the flashing lights expected from aircraft. They may also disappear abruptly when they enter the Earth’s shadow, creating the impression that a luminous object has switched off or accelerated away.

A particularly instructive Hauts-de-Seine case occurred at Châtillon during the night of 16–17 July 2022. Two witnesses saw a bright “sphere” travelling rapidly above La Défense and recorded it on a telephone. GEIPAN compared the observation time, direction and elevation with orbital data and found a close match with a pass of the International Space Station. The case was classified as fully identified.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cnes-geipan.fr/fr/cas/2022-07-51370" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on cnes-geipan.fr.</span></span></span>

The video initially appeared to support the idea of a round object because magnified frames showed two concentric circles. Investigators found that these were produced when the telephone failed to focus on a distant point of light. In better-focused sections, the supposed sphere returned to a simple luminous point. This is an important warning: enlarging a blurred image can turn a camera artefact into an apparent physical structure.

## How urban conditions distort distance and movement

The Hauts-de-Seine skyline gives observers plenty of foreground objects but surprisingly few useful references for judging something in the open sky. Buildings may establish direction, yet they do not reveal whether a light is 200 metres away or 20 kilometres away. Once distance is unknown, estimates of size, altitude and speed become largely speculative.

GEIPAN identifies several recurring perception effects. A lone light can appear to wander because of the **autokinetic effect**, a visual illusion in which a stationary point seems to move when there is no stable background. An aircraft can appear motionless when it approaches the observer directly. Conversely, an object moving with the witness may seem to pivot around a fixed point in the landscape. Observers may then construct an apparent flight path from several impressions that were never measured.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/fr/missions-methodes-et-resultats" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-title">GEIPANMission &amp; Geipan &#124; GEIPAN</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">GEIPANMission &amp; Geipan &#124; GEIPAN</span></span></span>

Urban surroundings amplify these problems in several ways.

**Interrupted viewing changes the apparent trajectory.** A light passing behind a tower, roofline or tree is not continuously tracked. When it reappears, the mind may connect the two visible positions with a turn or burst of speed that did not occur.

**Street lighting reduces night vision.** Bright windows, vehicle headlights and illuminated signs make faint details harder to see. The result may be an intense central light with no visible wings, balloon envelope or supporting structure.

**Noise is an unreliable distance test.** Traffic, ventilation equipment and enclosed courtyards can mask quiet or distant aircraft. A witness may therefore describe a phenomenon as completely silent even when its sound never stood out from the background.

**Cloud creates a false surface in the sky.** A low layer can receive light from below and make the illumination appear to be an object within or above the cloud. Because the beam itself may be invisible, witnesses see moving patches without an obvious source.

The last effect appears repeatedly in the department’s files. At La Garenne-Colombes on 21 August 2010, three witnesses watched three luminous balls move rapidly across a cloudy sky and disappear together. GEIPAN considered a multi-beam entertainment lighting system the most probable cause. The moving patches were not airborne objects but the illuminated points where ground-based beams struck the cloud base.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cnes-geipan.fr/fr/cas/2010-08-02632" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on cnes-geipan.fr.</span></span></span>

A more thoroughly documented example came from Chaville on 13 July 2023. A witness photographed organised white-blue lights that formed lines, a crown, a whirl and a ring before shrinking to a small halo. Weather information placed the first cloud layer at roughly 1,800 to 2,300 metres. GEIPAN concluded that powerful single-beam projectors, possibly being tested before the 14 July celebrations, were producing moving impact patches on the cloud. Such projectors can remain visible from more than ten kilometres away, so their actual source need not be close to the witness.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cnes-geipan.fr/fr/cas/2023-07-51479" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on cnes-geipan.fr.</span></span></span>

These cases explain why urban lighting reports often sound mechanically controlled. The light really is being directed and programmed, but the machinery is on the ground rather than flying in the sky.

## Why drones now resemble classic UFO formations

Drones have added a genuinely new element to the local night sky. A single drone may resemble a hovering aircraft light, while a coordinated display can form triangles, circles and symmetrical patterns that earlier generations of witnesses would have found extremely unusual.

On 11 December 2023, a witness in Boulogne-Billancourt saw five coloured lights performing geometric and apparently irregular movements above the direction of the Parc de Saint-Cloud. The display was silent from her position and lasted intermittently for about ten minutes. Video evidence made the observation reasonably well documented, but it did not make it mysterious.

Investigators discovered that a 1,000-drone performance took place at the park the following day. The organising company examined the witness material and confirmed that the lights belonged to a rehearsal for that event. GEIPAN classified the case as fully identified.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cnes-geipan.fr/fr/cas/2023-12-51502" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on cnes-geipan.fr.</span></span></span>

The case is valuable because the explanation was supported by more than visual resemblance. It combined:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--phrase-stack" markdown="1">

* the correct location and direction;
* movement typical of coordinated drones;
* simultaneous colour changes and switching;
* a documented public performance;
* and direct confirmation from the operator.

</div>

This is much stronger than simply saying that a sighting “looked like drones”. It demonstrates the standard investigators should seek: an explanation that fits the time, position, behaviour and appearance, ideally with independent confirmation.

Drone explanations should nevertheless not be applied automatically. An unverified claim that anything manoeuvrable must be a drone is no better than assuming it is an exotic craft. Flight restrictions, battery endurance, weather, visibility and the number of objects all need to be considered. The Boulogne-Billancourt case is persuasive precisely because those wider circumstances were checked.

## What photographs, videos and flight data can reveal

Modern recordings can transform an investigation, but only when treated as measurements rather than as dramatic pictures. A photograph may preserve the position of a light relative to a roof or tree. A video may establish duration, angular speed and changes in brightness. File timestamps can narrow the relevant flight, satellite and weather searches.

The most useful evidence usually comes from combining several independent sources:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--step-flow" markdown="1">

1. **Exact time and viewing direction** allow investigators to compare the report with aircraft tracks, satellite passes and astronomical positions.
2. **Weather data** show wind direction, cloud height, visibility and the likelihood of optical effects.
3. **Unedited original files** preserve metadata and reveal whether image processing, digital zoom or compression has altered the appearance.
4. **A daytime photograph from the same position** helps reconstruct the line of sight.
5. **Known events nearby** may identify fireworks, drone rehearsals, searchlights or balloon releases.

</div>

The Courbevoie aircraft case and Châtillon space-station case both became explainable because the reported paths could be compared with external movement data. The Chaville investigation depended heavily on cloud height, while the Boulogne-Billancourt drone case was settled through confirmation from the display operator.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cnes-geipan.fr/fr/cas/2015-03-09130" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">On rappelle qu&#x27;un avion vu se préparant à atterrir, donc vu de face, peut être vu à plus de 20 km grâce à ses feux d&#x27;atterrissage, lesque...</span></span></span>

Recordings can also introduce false detail. Digital zoom does not recover information that the lens failed to capture. Autofocus hunting can make a point expand and contract. Internal reflections may create secondary coloured lights, while camera movement makes a stationary source appear to dart across the frame.

The Sceaux case of 10 November 2013 illustrates both useful and misleading photographic evidence. A witness photographed an intense light near sunset and later noticed a white and red luminous feature above the horizon. GEIPAN identified the main phenomenon as a sundog, an optical effect produced by sunlight passing through ice crystals at an angle of about 22 degrees. Green marks in the photographs were separately identified as internal lens reflections rather than additional objects.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cnes-geipan.fr/fr/cas/2013-11-08607" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on cnes-geipan.fr.</span></span></span>

A photograph therefore does not automatically make a claim stronger. It may support an explanation by preserving geometry and timing, or weaken an extraordinary interpretation by revealing defocus, flare, motion blur or an ordinary object.

## Explained does not mean imagined

The explained cases of Hauts-de-Seine are not evidence that witnesses were dishonest or foolish. In most instances, they accurately noticed something striking but lacked the information needed to identify it in real time. GEIPAN itself stresses that the witness begins with an extraordinary personal experience even when the later explanation proves ordinary. Its investigators treat testimony as valuable but fragile, because observation includes both what reached the eye and how the mind interpreted an unfamiliar scene.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/fr/missions-methodes-et-resultats" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-title">GEIPANMission &amp; Geipan &#124; GEIPAN</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">GEIPANMission &amp; Geipan &#124; GEIPAN</span></span></span>

Nor should every weak report be labelled “debunked”. GEIPAN’s categories make an important distinction:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* **Category A** means the phenomenon has been identified with a high level of confidence.
* **Category B** means one explanation is considered very probable.
* **Category C** means there is not enough reliable information for a sound conclusion.
* **Category D** means the case remains unexplained despite sufficient material for investigation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/fr/missions-methodes-et-resultats" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-title">GEIPANMission &amp; Geipan &#124; GEIPAN</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">GEIPANMission &amp; Geipan &#124; GEIPAN</span></span></span>

</div>

That distinction is especially relevant to the department’s older files. A probable aircraft or lantern is not as firmly established as the confirmed Boulogne-Billancourt drone rehearsal. Conversely, a report lacking direction, time or photographs does not become an unexplained mystery merely because investigators cannot reconstruct it.

The practical lesson from Hauts-de-Seine is that the strangest-looking features often arise from the interaction between an ordinary source and an urban viewing environment. Landing lights create hovering objects; lanterns become silent orange spheres; a defocused space station becomes a circular craft; projectors paint coordinated forms on cloud; and rehearsing drones reproduce the geometry once associated with speculative aircraft. The more accurately investigators can recover time, direction, weather and movement, the more often the extraordinary appearance gives way to a specific, testable explanation.
