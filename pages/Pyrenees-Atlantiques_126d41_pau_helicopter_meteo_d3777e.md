## Introduction

On the evening of 14 December 2022, a French Civil Security helicopter crew returning towards Pau reported four fast, luminous trails apparently travelling almost horizontally near the aircraft. An air-traffic controller at Pau-Pyrénées Airport then saw a similar brief light. The witnesses were experienced aviation professionals, the crew were using night-vision binoculars, and radar and military-traffic checks found no matching aircraft.

Even so, France’s official UFO investigation unit, GEIPAN, concluded that the most likely explanation was natural: an initial grazing fireball followed by three meteors associated with the Geminid shower. The finding was not presented as absolute identification. It was classified B, meaning “probably identified”, because no photograph, camera detection or independently reconstructed meteor trajectory confirmed the explanation. The case matters within Pyrénées-Atlantiques because it shows how a well-observed event can remain visually baffling while its timing, direction, duration and appearance point towards an ordinary astronomical cause.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/fr/cas/2022-12-51414" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">GEIPAN[AERO] DE BEDOUS (64) VERS PAU (64) 14.12.2022 &#124; GEIPAN...</span></span></span>

## What the helicopter crew and controller reported

The helicopter left the Bedous area in the Aspe valley after a mountain training exercise and headed back towards Pau. At about 18:35, shortly after passing Oloron-Sainte-Marie, it was flying at roughly 2,000 feet. ## Why experienced witnesses still found the lights extraordinary

The witnesses’ professional backgrounds make the report valuable, but they do not eliminate the central problem of night observation: a light against the sky usually provides no reliable scale or distance. Without a known object beside it, apparent altitude, size and proximity are largely inferred rather than directly perceived.

That limitation explains the most dramatic element of the pilot’s account. A form judged “larger than an airliner” need not have been a large nearby object. A meteor hundreds of kilometres away can subtend a conspicuous angle in the sky, while a bright head, wake, flare or contrast effect may be interpreted as a solid body. The pilot later estimated the first phenomenon’s apparent length as five to ten centimetres when measured at arm’s length, but that angular measure could not by itself reveal its distance or physical size.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/sites/default/files/Compte%20rendu%20enquete697.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Compte rendu enquete697</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">GEIPANModèle de document par défaut CNES version 2.0 Janvier 2010...</span></span></span>

Night-vision binoculars added sensitivity but also removed useful visual cues. The pilot did not see the first four events with the naked eye. His equipment rendered the scene in monochrome shades ranging from pale green to near black, meaning that a bright meteor head, an uneven trail or local image saturation could produce contrasts unlike ordinary unaided vision. GEIPAN treated the reported dark oblong head as the main feature that did not fit the meteor explanation neatly.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/sites/default/files/Compte%20rendu%20enquete697.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Compte rendu enquete697</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">GEIPANModèle de document par défaut CNES version 2.0 Janvier 2010...</span></span></span>

Experience itself can also create a misleading comparison. Both crew members were familiar with brief, steeply sloping shooting stars. They therefore regarded the slower, near-horizontal tracks as abnormal. GEIPAN’s response was that meteor appearance varies with speed and viewing geometry: a meteor travelling nearly across the observer’s line of sight, with its radiant close to the horizon, may follow a long, apparently level path rather than dropping visibly downwards.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/sites/default/files/Compte%20rendu%20enquete697.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Compte rendu enquete697</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">GEIPANModèle de document par défaut CNES version 2.0 Janvier 2010...</span></span></span>

## How radar, geometry and the Geminids were checked

The investigation considered an aviation explanation before settling on meteors. The Gendarmerie des Transports Aériens obtained information from the National Air Operations Centre and examined available radar material. According to the official report, recorded aircraft tracks did not correlate with the sightings. Radio records also show that another control station suggested at the time that the light looked like a meteor or shooting star.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/sites/default/files/Compte%20rendu%20enquete697.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Compte rendu enquete697</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">GEIPANModèle de document par défaut CNES version 2.0 Janvier 2010...</span></span></span>

The absence of a radar return did not prove that the lights were meteors. Many objects or atmospheric effects may not appear on conventional air-traffic radar. It did, however, weaken the crew’s initial hypothesis of a fast aircraft operating near Pau, especially when combined with the duration of only two to five seconds and the lack of navigation lights, engine noise or a continuing flight path.

The astronomical timing was unusually favourable to the meteor hypothesis. The observation occurred on 14 December, the date of the 2022 Geminid maximum. The International Meteor Organization’s calendar placed the shower’s predicted peak at about 13:00 UTC that day, only several hours before the Pau sightings. It listed a theoretical zenithal hourly rate of up to 150 under ideal conditions. This figure is not the number a particular observer should expect to see; it is a standardised rate for a perfectly dark sky with the meteor radiant overhead.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.imo.net/files/meteor-shower/cal2022.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: imo.net">[imo.net]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">imo.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">International Meteor Organization2022 Meteor Shower CalendarJuly 19, 2021 — Geminid (004 GEM) shower peak night December 13/14 has a wani...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 19, 2021</span></span></span>

For Pau at about 18:35 local time, GEIPAN calculated that the Geminid radiant—the point from which shower members appear to diverge—was in the north-east at an elevation of only about 1.15 degrees. That low position was crucial. Three of the reported trails began in the north-eastern sector and travelled towards the south-west, consistent with meteors radiating from that point. Seen side-on, their tracks could look almost horizontal and close to the local horizon.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/sites/default/files/Compte%20rendu%20enquete697.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Compte rendu enquete697</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">GEIPANModèle de document par défaut CNES version 2.0 Janvier 2010...</span></span></span>

The first east-to-west event did not align as well with the Geminid radiant. GEIPAN therefore proposed a two-part sequence rather than forcing every observation into one shower: the first was probably a larger, independent grazing meteor or fireball, while the following three were probably Geminids. This distinction is important because the official explanation did not claim that four identical Geminids followed precisely the same course.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/sites/default/files/Compte%20rendu%20enquete697.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Compte rendu enquete697</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">GEIPANModèle de document par défaut CNES version 2.0 Janvier 2010...</span></span></span>

## The speed calculation supported—but did not prove—the theory

GEIPAN attempted a rough speed estimate for the first and most striking event. The calculation used the pilot’s recalled angular elevation, the apparent trail length measured at arm’s length and a duration of about four seconds. Depending on whether the event was modelled as an ordinary meteor or a lower-penetrating fireball, the resulting speed was about 10.6 or 20 kilometres per second.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/sites/default/files/Compte%20rendu%20enquete697.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Compte rendu enquete697</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">GEIPANModèle de document par défaut CNES version 2.0 Janvier 2010...</span></span></span>

Those values fall within the broad range expected for atmospheric meteors, whose minimum entry speed is around 11 kilometres per second. The higher estimate also matched the pilot’s impression of something faster than an aircraft yet slower than many familiar shooting stars. Geminid meteors enter at roughly 35 kilometres per second, slower than showers such as the Perseids, which helps explain why an experienced observer might perceive them as unusually deliberate or prolonged.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/sites/default/files/Compte%20rendu%20enquete697.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Compte rendu enquete697</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">GEIPANModèle de document par défaut CNES version 2.0 Janvier 2010...</span></span></span>

The calculation should not be mistaken for a measured trajectory. GEIPAN explicitly warned that it was highly sensitive to uncertain inputs. Changing the assumed duration from four seconds to three raised the fireball estimate from about 20 to 27 kilometres per second. The assumed altitude, viewing angle and apparent length were also reconstructed from memory rather than derived from calibrated imagery. The exercise therefore showed compatibility with a meteor; it did not independently establish one.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/sites/default/files/Compte%20rendu%20enquete697.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Compte rendu enquete697</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">GEIPANModèle de document par défaut CNES version 2.0 Janvier 2010...</span></span></span>

## What evidence was missing

No photograph or video was taken from the helicopter or tower. GEIPAN also searched the FRIPON fireball-camera network, the BOAM amateur meteor database and the International Meteor Organization’s visual-report archive without finding a matching record. This is the strongest limitation on the official conclusion. There was no instrumentally timed meteor track that could be triangulated and compared directly with the witnesses’ bearings.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/sites/default/files/Compte%20rendu%20enquete697.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Compte rendu enquete697</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">GEIPANModèle de document par défaut CNES version 2.0 Janvier 2010...</span></span></span>

GEIPAN argued that non-detection was plausible because the event occurred during the transition between nautical and astronomical twilight, when the western sky was not fully dark. The pilot also described the light as dimmer than a city street lamp, and night-vision equipment could reveal objects too faint to trigger distant automated cameras. Those points explain how a meteor might go unrecorded, but they remain explanations for missing evidence rather than positive confirmation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/sites/default/files/Compte%20rendu%20enquete697.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Compte rendu enquete697</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">GEIPANModèle de document par défaut CNES version 2.0 Janvier 2010...</span></span></span>

There were further uncertainties:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--metric" markdown="1">

* Only the pilot saw all four helicopter events.
* The mechanic saw one, and the controller saw another that the crew missed.
* The controller’s naked-eye description was less detailed than the crew’s night-vision account.
* The first event’s dark oblong “body” was not independently confirmed.
* The reported directions and durations were reconstructed after brief observations made from a moving aircraft.
* GEIPAN received some of the fuller gendarmerie material months later, limiting its own opportunity to retrieve time-sensitive aviation data.

</div>

These weaknesses do not discredit the witnesses. They restrict how precisely the events can be reconstructed and explain why GEIPAN stopped at “probable” rather than “certain”.

## Why GEIPAN chose a probable meteor explanation

GEIPAN’s conclusion rested on several features working together, rather than on any single decisive match. Each light lasted only a few seconds; the paths were straight; the witnesses described trails, combustion-like luminosity and spark-like material; three tracks were geometrically compatible with a low north-eastern Geminid radiant; and the sightings occurred only hours after the shower’s predicted maximum. The rough speed calculation was also physically plausible for a meteor or fireball.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/fr/cas/2022-12-51414" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">GEIPAN[AERO] DE BEDOUS (64) VERS PAU (64) 14.12.2022 &#124; GEIPAN...</span></span></span>

The alternative aircraft hypothesis was weaker. No matching military activity or radar track was established, and an aircraft would be unlikely to appear repeatedly for only two to five seconds as an elongated, sparkling trail before fading without a continuing light or course. The meteor hypothesis also explained why apparently similar tracks could recur within minutes: they were separate particles from an active shower, not one craft making repeated passes.

GEIPAN assigned the case a strangeness score of about 0.38 and a consistency score of about 0.62. In its system, consistency reflects the amount and reliability of information, while strangeness measures how poorly the event fits known phenomena after investigation. The resulting category B means that the phenomenon was probably identified, not proved beyond doubt.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/sites/default/files/Compte%20rendu%20enquete697.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Compte rendu enquete697</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">GEIPANModèle de document par défaut CNES version 2.0 Janvier 2010...</span></span></span>

That wording is the fairest overall judgement. The Geminid explanation accounts well for the timing, repeated short trails, low apparent elevation and three later directions. The first event is less secure because of its different path and the pilot’s perception of a large dark body. With no camera record or triangulation, it remains possible that some details were reconstructed incorrectly or that the controller did not see exactly the same type of phenomenon as the crew.

## What the Pau case adds to Pyrénées-Atlantiques UFO history

The Pau helicopter report is stronger than most local light sightings because it produced prompt professional testimony, aviation radio exchanges, a gendarmerie inquiry, radar checks and a detailed public GEIPAN report. Its importance does not depend on remaining unexplained. On the contrary, it is valuable because investigators could compare an impressive account with a specific astronomical event occurring at the same time.

It also cautions against two opposite errors. The first is assuming that trained pilots and controllers cannot misidentify natural phenomena. Their expertise improves reporting of headings, timing and aviation context, but it cannot supply distance to an isolated light. The second is dismissing the experience as a trivial “shooting star”. The geometry was unusual, the observations occurred through night-vision equipment, and the first trail may have been a grazing fireball—precisely the kind of rare presentation that could look unlike meteors the crew had previously seen.

Later investigation therefore weakened the claim that the lights represented unknown aircraft, while strengthening a coherent meteor interpretation. It did not turn every reported detail into certainty. Within the department’s wider UFO record, the case stands as a particularly clear example of a credible sighting becoming probably explained through timing, geometry and astronomical context rather than through doubts about the witnesses themselves.
