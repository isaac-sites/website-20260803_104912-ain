## Introduction

The Évreux case of 23 April 1978 is arguably the strongest officially documented UFO incident in Eure, but “strongest” does not mean conclusive. Personnel at Base aérienne 105 saw a red point of light north-west of the airfield, and a military radar then displayed a faint return in roughly the same sector for seven minutes. That combination is more interesting than an unsupported eyewitness story because it appears to join human observation with an instrument reading. Yet the radar trace was weak, was not preserved, and was not confirmed by another surveillance station.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/fr/cas/1978-04-01879" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">GEIPANFAUVILLE (27) 23.04.1978 &#124; GEIPAN...</span></span></span>

The case therefore occupies an awkward middle ground. France’s official investigators rejected some straightforward explanations and still classify the phenomenon as unidentified, but they also warn that a false radar echo cannot be excluded. Rather than proving that an unusual craft crossed Eure’s skies, Évreux shows how apparently corroborating evidence can strengthen a report without making it secure.

## What air-base personnel saw and recorded

The incident began on Sunday evening, 23 April 1978, at approximately 20:35 Universal Time, or 22:35 local time. According to the surviving official summary, a telephone call prompted the operational duty officer at Évreux air base to look towards an estimated bearing of 300 degrees, broadly north-west. He saw a single red light between about 10 and 15 degrees above the horizon. Personnel in the control tower also observed it, and its brightness reportedly diminished during the sighting.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/fr/cas/1978-04-01879" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">GEIPANFAUVILLE (27) 23.04.1978 &#124; GEIPAN...</span></span></span>

The published witness data describe the phenomenon only as a point-like red object, with an apparent size compared to a planet. There was no reported shape, structure, beam, exhaust or close approach. No reliable distance was established, so estimates of its actual size and altitude are impossible. The visual component was therefore unusual mainly because of its apparent movement and duration, not because witnesses saw a detailed machine.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/fr/temoignage/2096" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">GEIPANFAUVILLE (27) 23.04.1978 &#124; GEIPAN...</span></span></span>

The duty officer asked for radar assistance. At 20:44, the radar operator reported an echo at a bearing of about 300 degrees and a range of 18 nautical miles, roughly 33 kilometres. It was said to move towards 330 degrees at an estimated 30 to 40 knots, or approximately 56 to 74 kilometres per hour. Both the visual observation and radar watch ended at about 20:51, giving the radar episode a duration of seven minutes.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/fr/cas/1978-04-01879" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">GEIPANFAUVILLE (27) 23.04.1978 &#124; GEIPAN...</span></span></span>

This sequence is the core reason the case matters. The radar was not consulted days later in an attempt to find supporting data; it was brought into the observation while the light was still visible. The reported bearing also placed the return in the same general part of the sky. That is genuine evidential value, even though it falls short of demonstrating that the radar and the red light represented the same physical object.

The officer wrote his report on 24 April. A separate radar observation report apparently gave the observation date as the 24th rather than the 23rd, but the official review treats this as a probable typing error. The matching times and sequence make contemporaneous radar involvement plausible, although the dating inconsistency is another reminder that the surviving case file is not a flawless instrumental record.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/fr/cas/1978-04-01879" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">GEIPANFAUVILLE (27) 23.04.1978 &#124; GEIPAN...</span></span></span>

## Why the radar return remains important

Radar can provide information that eyesight cannot, particularly an apparent range, direction and pattern of movement. In the Évreux case, the reported return was not merely a momentary flash on the screen. It persisted for seven minutes and appeared to change bearing. That duration makes it harder to dismiss as a single random pulse or an operator glancing at an isolated speck.

The return also appeared during the visual observation. In a well-documented radar-visual case, agreement between two independent sensing methods can reduce the chance that a witness simply mistook a star or planet. Modern scientific proposals for studying unidentified aerial phenomena similarly emphasise simultaneous measurements from different sensors because corroboration helps distinguish real targets from visual misperceptions and instrument artefacts.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.18566" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: arxiv.org">[arXiv]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">arxiv.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Scientific Investigation of Unidentified Aerial Phenomena (UAP) Using Multimodal Ground-Based ObservatoriesMay 29, 2023...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 29, 2023</span></span></span>

Évreux nevertheless lacked the information needed to make that principle decisive. The original radar recording is not available in the public file. Readers cannot inspect the raw signal, successive plots, antenna scans or any automatic track history. The published account relies on the operator’s written report and the later interpretation of investigators.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/fr/cas/1978-04-01879" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">GEIPANFAUVILLE (27) 23.04.1978 &#124; GEIPAN...</span></span></span>

The return’s intensity was rated only one out of five. Such a weak signal may come from a small or poorly reflecting target, but it is also more vulnerable to noise, clutter, propagation effects and indirect reflections. Without the recording, it is impossible to test how consistently the echo appeared on successive rotations or whether its reported movement reflected a true track rather than shifting patches of interference.

There was also no confirmation from the military surveillance centre at Cinq-Mars-la-Pile. That absence does not automatically disprove the Évreux detection: different radar systems can have different coverage, sensitivity, filtering and viewing geometry. However, a second station registering a corresponding target would have made the case substantially stronger. GEIPAN specifically cites the lack of confirmation as a reason for caution.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/fr/cas/1978-04-01879" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">GEIPANFAUVILLE (27) 23.04.1978 &#124; GEIPAN...</span></span></span>

The fairest description is therefore **limited instrumental corroboration**. The radar report raises the case above an ordinary light-in-the-sky sighting, but it does not supply a preserved, independently verified trajectory.

## False echoes, Venus and the limits of the evidence

### Could Venus explain the red light?

Venus was conspicuous in the western sky that evening and lay near the initial reported bearing. GEIPAN calculated its approximate azimuth as 296 to 299 degrees, close to the officer’s first estimate of 300 degrees. The planet was low, however, descending from roughly four degrees to about 1.5 degrees above the horizon. The unexplained light was estimated much higher, at 10 to 15 degrees, and was later associated with movement towards a bearing of 330 degrees.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/fr/cas/1978-04-01879" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">GEIPANFAUVILLE (27) 23.04.1978 &#124; GEIPAN...</span></span></span>

GEIPAN therefore regards a simple identification with Venus as extremely unlikely. That judgement is reasonable if the recorded angular positions are dependable. A difference of six degrees or more in elevation is large enough to matter, and a planet cannot physically travel 30 degrees northwards in a few minutes.

Even so, the Venus issue cannot be dismissed entirely. It is surprising that trained personnel did not mention such a bright nearby reference point, particularly when judging the position of another point-like light. Human estimates of elevation and bearing can be inaccurate when an object is distant, the horizon is poorly defined or the observer has no measuring instrument. Venus might have contributed to confusion without explaining every reported feature.

The description of decreasing brightness also has ordinary possibilities. Thin or broken cloud can alter a celestial light’s intensity, while atmospheric extinction becomes stronger near the horizon. Yet those effects would not naturally explain the stated change of bearing or the reported radar track. Venus is therefore a relevant complication rather than a complete solution.

### Could the radar have shown a false target?

Radar does not display only aircraft. Depending on the equipment and conditions, returns can be produced by terrain, weather, birds, atmospheric refraction, equipment effects or signals reaching the receiver by an indirect path. Aviation guidance recognises that abnormal bending of radar pulses can generate extraneous targets, while reflections may create apparent objects at misleading positions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.faa.gov/documentLibrary/media/Order/6310.6.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: faa.gov">[Federal Aviation Administration]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">faa.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Federal Aviation AdministrationOrder 6310.620 Jul 1976 — false targets, reflections, propagation effects, potential sources of reflected...</span></span></span>

Such effects are particularly relevant when a return is weak and unconfirmed. GEIPAN mentions a secondary reflection as one possible explanation for the Évreux echo. In this scenario, the signal need not have come directly from an unknown object at the plotted position; radar energy could have followed a more complicated path involving another reflecting surface or target.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/fr/cas/1978-04-01879" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">GEIPANFAUVILLE (27) 23.04.1978 &#124; GEIPAN...</span></span></span>

The reported speed of 30 to 40 knots does not settle the matter. It is slow for a conventional fixed-wing aircraft but compatible with a helicopter or some balloons. It could also represent the apparent displacement of a false echo rather than the movement of a solid target. Radar artefacts are not always perfectly stationary: atmospheric conditions or changing reflection geometry can make anomalous returns shift or appear to possess misleading velocity characteristics.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.weather.gov/media/wrh/online_publications/TAs/ta9507.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: weather.gov">[National Weather Service]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">weather.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Weather Service Western Region Technical Attachment No</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">95-07 March 14...January 31, 2013 — 14 Mar 1995 — Obviously, anomalous propagation of the radar beam was occurring since echo tops of 37...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: January 31, 2013</span></span></span>

Investigators considered and rejected helicopter and balloon explanations. The public summary does not provide enough detail to reconstruct those eliminations fully, however. It does not identify all known air traffic, balloon-launch records, wind profiles or the precise radar characteristics used in the comparison. The rejection should therefore be treated as the official investigative conclusion, not as proof that every aircraft or lighter-than-air possibility was exhaustively excluded.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/fr/cas/1978-04-01879" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">GEIPANFAUVILLE (27) 23.04.1978 &#124; GEIPAN...</span></span></span>

A further complication is that the visual and radar observations may have been coincidental. A low celestial or airborne light could have attracted attention while the radar displayed an unrelated weak echo in the same broad sector. Bearings around 300 degrees would sound impressively similar in a brief summary, but the visual object had no measured range and the radar return’s elevation is not given. The file does not establish that both sensors were following one target in three-dimensional space.

## How strong is the case today?

GEIPAN retains the incident as a D1 case: unidentified after investigation, moderately strange and supported by evidence of reasonable consistency. The classification is significant because it was not assigned merely through later UFO folklore. GEPAN, the original CNES investigation group founded in 1977, received a prompt military report and examined possible explanations. GEIPAN continues to publish the case as unresolved, including in an update dated 16 June 2026.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/fr/cas/1978-04-01879" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">GEIPANFAUVILLE (27) 23.04.1978 &#124; GEIPAN...</span></span></span>

The classification does not mean that France’s space agency considers the event an extraterrestrial craft. CNES distinguishes between cases that remain unidentified because information is inadequate and a much smaller group that remain unidentified after investigation. Even in that latter category, “unidentified” describes the result of the inquiry, not the origin of the phenomenon.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cnes.fr/en/projects/geipan" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes.fr">[CNES]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-title">GEIPAN &#124; CNES</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">GEIPAN &#124; CNES...</span></span></span>

The case’s strongest features are clear:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* the report was made promptly by military personnel performing operational duties;
* more than one person reportedly saw the light;
* radar was consulted during the visual observation;
* the echo persisted for several minutes rather than appearing only once;
* investigators did not find a satisfactory helicopter, balloon or astronomical explanation.

</div>

Its weaknesses are equally important:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--metric" markdown="1">

* only one formal witness testimony is listed in the accessible file;
* the object remained a distant, point-like light;
* no photograph, film or physical trace exists;
* the raw radar data were not preserved or released;
* the return was extremely faint;
* another radar station did not confirm it;
* the radar and visual positions were not matched through a complete measured track;
* ordinary radar artefacts cannot be excluded.

</div>

Later reporting has neither solved nor dramatically strengthened the case. The modern GEIPAN summary clarifies the timing, positions, Venus comparison and radar limitations, but it does not add a recovered recording, new independent witness statement or second sensor detection. The passage of time has therefore improved public access to the official assessment rather than the underlying evidence.

## Did radar make the Évreux sighting stronger?

Yes, but only to a point. The radar report makes Évreux 1978 more substantial than most UFO accounts from Eure because it supplies an apparently contemporaneous instrument response and an estimated range and speed. It also helps explain why the event remains a landmark case in the department’s official UFO history.

The same radar evidence prevents a confident conclusion. A one-out-of-five return, missing raw data and no confirmation from Cinq-Mars-la-Pile are serious limitations, not minor technicalities. They leave open the possibilities of a weak reflection from an ordinary target, abnormal propagation, an indirect echo or a radar event unrelated to the observed light.

The most defensible verdict is that military personnel encountered something they could not identify and documented a weak radar return in the same general direction. That combination deserves attention, but it does not establish a single extraordinary craft. Évreux remains unresolved because the evidence is suggestive yet incomplete: stronger than a bare anecdote, weaker than a securely correlated radar-visual track.
