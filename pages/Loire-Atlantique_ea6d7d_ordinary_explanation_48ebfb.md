## Introduction

Some of [Loire-Atlantique]({{ 'which-loire-atlantique-ufo-cases-still/' | relative_url }})’s most striking UFO reports did not fade because witnesses withdrew their stories. They became less mysterious because investigators reconstructed where people were standing, what direction they were looking, how clouds and darkness altered the scene, and which ordinary lights were actually present.



<img src="{{ "/assets/images/Loire-Atlantique_ea6d7d_ordinary_explanation_48ebfb-overview.webp" | relative_url }}" alt="Overview image for Likely Explanations" loading="eager" decoding="sync" fetchpriority="high">

A supposed object following a car near Le Pellerin was eventually judged likely to have been an illuminated model aircraft. A triangular formation over Nantes matched the Hyades star cluster. A huge red-orange form over the coast proved consistent with the Moon rising through cloud. Other reports were traced to Thai lanterns or powerful lights on a working ship. These cases matter because they show how a witness can accurately describe a vivid experience while misjudging its distance, scale or movement. They also demonstrate why an “unexplained” classification is not permanent: later mapping tools, astronomical software, weather data and traffic records can turn an extraordinary-looking report into an ordinary object.

## The Le Pellerin object that seemed to follow a car

The department’s clearest example of apparent pursuit occurred near Le Pellerin on 11 June 1994. At about 11.45 p.m., a woman driving along a small country road noticed something moving beside her at what appeared to be very low altitude. She described a lemon-shaped object covered in flashing, multicoloured lights, with what looked like a cable or projection on top. It disappeared from view, reappeared and seemed to keep pace with her car before vanishing rapidly. The original case was classified as unexplained.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/fr/cas/1994-06-01358" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">GEIPANPELLERIN (LE) (44) 11.06.1994 &#124; GEIPAN...</span></span></span>

That description sounds more elaborate than a distant point of light. Yet the later GEIPAN review found that the observation was directed mainly towards an authorised flying area belonging to a nearby model-aircraft club. The object’s low apparent elevation suited model-aircraft operations, while its coloured flashing lights broadly resembled aviation navigation and anti-collision lighting. The witness was also viewing it at night from inside a moving car with the windows closed, circumstances that made both its outline and any engine noise difficult to recognise.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/fr/cas/1994-06-01358" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">GEIPANPELLERIN (LE) (44) 11.06.1994 &#124; GEIPAN...</span></span></span>

The investigation did not establish the identity of a particular model or pilot. GEIPAN therefore stopped short of calling the identification certain. Instead, it reclassified the case as category B: probably explained by a model aircraft or model helicopter. That distinction is important. The conclusion rests on a close fit between the location, lighting, apparent altitude and flight behaviour, rather than on a recovered object or a flight log.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/fr/cas/1994-06-01358" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">GEIPANPELLERIN (LE) (44) 11.06.1994 &#124; GEIPAN...</span></span></span>

Several apparently exotic details could also be reproduced without invoking an unusual craft. The “cable” may have been part of the model’s tail or structure briefly illuminated by its lights. Its apparent splitting into two forms could have resulted from a change in orientation, with a dimmer section of the fuselage becoming visible, or from partial obstruction by vegetation. A sudden disappearance behind trees could easily look like instant acceleration into the distance.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/fr/cas/1994-06-01358" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">GEIPANPELLERIN (LE) (44) 11.06.1994 &#124; GEIPAN...</span></span></span>

Most significant was the impression that the object deliberately followed the car. GEIPAN linked this to a familiar “following light” effect. A distant light whose direction changes little relative to the observer can seem to travel alongside a moving vehicle. Because its true distance is unknown, the witness may interpret changes in brightness, orientation or visibility as forward movement, retreat or evasive manoeuvres. In the Le Pellerin case, the nearby road, hedges and trees supplied an apparent foreground trajectory even though there was no reliable way to place the light among those features.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/fr/cas/1994-06-01358" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">GEIPANPELLERIN (LE) (44) 11.06.1994 &#124; GEIPAN...</span></span></span>

## How darkness creates speed, size and pursuit

Night observations are difficult not because witnesses are necessarily inattentive, but because many of the cues used to judge distance disappear. A small bright object nearby and a powerful light many kilometres away can occupy the same angle in the observer’s vision. Unless the object passes in front of a feature at a known distance, its physical size and speed cannot be calculated reliably.

GEIPAN explicitly separates what was perceived from the interpretation added to it. Investigators consider short-term visual effects, uncertain reconstruction of paths, assumptions about distance and speed, emotional response and later memory. The service notes that an ordinary stimulus can acquire additional strangeness through apparent motion, cloud movement or the absence of a recognisable shape.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/en/missions-methodes-et-resultats" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-title">GEIPANMission &amp; Geipan &#124; GEIPAN</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">GEIPANMission &amp; Geipan &#124; GEIPAN</span></span></span>

Several mechanisms recur in the Loire-Atlantique files:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* **Relative motion:** a distant light can appear to accompany a moving car because its bearing changes slowly.
* **Loss of brightness:** a lantern, light or partly obscured astronomical object may seem to shoot away when it dims or disappears.
* **Moving clouds:** stars or the Moon can appear to move when clouds drift across them.
* **Poor angular estimates:** witnesses commonly report metres, kilometres or aircraft-like dimensions when only an apparent size in the sky was actually available.
* **Foreground association:** a light beyond trees, buildings, turbines or a coastline can be mistakenly placed among them rather than far behind them.

</div>

These are not claims that observers imagined the light itself. They explain how a real light can acquire a false trajectory, structure or sense of intention.

## Stars that formed a triangular object over Nantes

Shortly after midnight on 20 October 2013, two people walking in Nantes noticed a group of evenly spaced lights forming what looked like a triangle. The arrangement suggested a structured object rather than random stars, and cloud movement may have strengthened the impression that the formation itself was moving.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/fr/cas/2013-10-08585" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">GEIPANNANTES (44) 20.10.2013 &#124; GEIPAN...</span></span></span>

GEIPAN reconstructed the viewing direction and found that the pattern matched the Hyades, the prominent V-shaped star cluster near Aldebaran. The lights occupied the expected positions, while the single orange-toned point corresponded to the appearance of Aldebaran beside the cluster. The case was classified A, meaning that GEIPAN considered the astronomical identification certain.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/fr/cas/2013-10-08585" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">GEIPANNANTES (44) 20.10.2013 &#124; GEIPAN...</span></span></span>

This case illustrates how several independent lights can be perceived as one object. Human vision naturally groups points into shapes, particularly when they have roughly regular spacing. Once the lights are understood as the corners or illuminated parts of a dark body, the empty sky between them becomes the imagined surface of the object. Clouds then appear to pass behind or across that supposed body, although the stars themselves remain fixed.

GEIPAN did not describe the witnesses as having seen the stars incorrectly. Its conclusion was that they interpreted a genuine celestial pattern under conditions of surprise, tiredness and uncertain cloud movement. That distinction preserves the value of the testimony while explaining why its initial meaning was misleading.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/fr/cas/2013-10-08585" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">GEIPANNANTES (44) 20.10.2013 &#124; GEIPAN...</span></span></span>

## When the Moon looked enormous and moved along the coast

An older coastal case shows how strongly atmosphere and geography can transform a familiar object. On 16 July 1979, two witnesses at Le Pouliguen reported a very large red-orange and yellow luminous form above Pornichet. It appeared stationary at first, then seemed to move towards Saint-Nazaire while its surrounding halo gradually diminished. The report was originally treated as unexplained.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/fr/cas/1979-07-00641" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">GEIPANPOULIGUEN (LE) (44) 16.07.1979 &#124; GEIPAN...</span></span></span>

A later review placed the Moon low above Pornichet in precisely the relevant direction. At an elevation of only about 4.6 degrees, it was being viewed through a long stretch of atmosphere and intermittent cloud or mist. Those conditions could account for its reddish colouring, its halo and the changing outlines described by the witnesses. A gap in the clouds could produce a defined central “dome”, while surrounding moisture scattered the light into a much larger luminous area.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/fr/cas/1979-07-00641" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">GEIPANPOULIGUEN (LE) (44) 16.07.1979 &#124; GEIPAN...</span></span></span>

The apparent movement towards Saint-Nazaire was reconstructed as an illusion caused by the halo shrinking and the light fading as cloud closed over the Moon. Without a known distance, decreasing angular size and brightness were interpreted as physical retreat. The local geography made that perceived retreat appear to follow the coastline in the direction of Saint-Nazaire. ## Lanterns that accelerated or changed course

Thai or Chinese sky lanterns have generated a separate family of Loire-Atlantique reports. Their orange-red colour, silence, low apparent altitude and tendency to travel in loose groups can make them resemble a formation of controlled objects.

On 16 April 2011, a motorist and passenger travelling from Treillières towards Nantes saw about ten orange lights moving horizontally and silently before gradually disappearing. Their direction matched the prevailing north-easterly wind, while the time—early on a weekend morning—was compatible with lanterns released after a private celebration. GEIPAN classified the event as probably caused by Thai lanterns.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/fr/cas/2011-04-02849" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-title">GEIPAN[N537] DE TREILLIERES (44) VERS NANTES (44) 16.04</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">16 Apr 2011 — Probable observations du passage de lanternes thailandaises. ## A “light in the sky” that was actually at sea

The coastal setting produces another recurring error: a light on the sea can be placed in the sky. A recent Batz-sur-Mer case demonstrates how modern traffic records can resolve that ambiguity.

## Why later reviews overturn unexplained classifications

An unexplained label describes the state of an investigation, not the permanent nature of the event. GEIPAN’s categories distinguish between identified cases, probably identified cases, reports lacking enough reliable information and cases that remain unexplained after investigation. The service also allows old cases to be revisited when better evidence or new analytical methods become available.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/en/missions-methodes-et-resultats" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-title">GEIPANMission &amp; Geipan &#124; GEIPAN</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">GEIPANMission &amp; Geipan &#124; GEIPAN</span></span></span>

Several improvements have directly affected Loire-Atlantique cases:

**Astronomical reconstruction** can now test the precise position of stars, planets and the Moon for a stated location and time. This resolved the Nantes Hyades case and helped overturn the old Le Pouliguen classification.

**Digital mapping** allows investigators to compare sightlines with roads, club flying areas, coastlines, vegetation, wind turbines and other landmarks. In Le Pellerin, geography connected the reported path with the model-aircraft zone; at Batz-sur-Mer, turbines in the video anchored the line of sight.

**Weather records** help determine whether cloud, haze and wind support an explanation. Wind direction is particularly valuable for lantern reports, while cloud cover can explain changing shape, colour and apparent motion around an astronomical source.

**Photographic and traffic analysis** can test colour, angular size and the presence of aircraft or vessels. The Batz-sur-Mer conclusion required maritime tracking and confirmation from the ship operator, evidence that was unavailable from the witness’s impressions alone.

Reclassification does not mean that investigators proved every detail of the reconstructed scenario. The Le Pellerin model aircraft was not individually identified, and the 2020 Nantes lantern release was never located. Those cases remained category B because the ordinary explanation was strongly supported but not conclusively demonstrated. By contrast, exact astronomical matches, confirming imagery or matching operational records can justify category A.

## What the ordinary explanations reveal

The explained cases do not show that every Loire-Atlantique report has an easy answer. Some files remain unresolved, while others contain too little information to support any reliable conclusion. They do show, however, that the most dramatic element in a report is often not the original light but the motion, shape or distance attributed to it.

A star cluster becomes a triangular craft when separate lights are visually grouped. A low Moon becomes an enormous object when cloud supplies a changing boundary and halo. A lantern accelerates when its flame goes out. A model aircraft follows a motorist because a distant bearing remains nearly constant. A ship’s work light hangs in the sky because the sea and vessel disappear into darkness.

That pattern is central to understanding Loire-Atlantique’s UFO history. Ordinary explanations did not require treating witnesses as dishonest or foolish. They emerged by separating careful observations—colour, direction, duration, sequence—from uncertain inferences about size, altitude, speed and intention. In several cases, that separation was enough to turn an apparently structured or pursuing object back into the Moon, stars, lanterns, a model aircraft or a working vessel.
