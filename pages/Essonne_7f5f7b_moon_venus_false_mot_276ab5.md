## Introduction

Several striking [Essonne]({{ 'why-essonnes-ufo-cases-often-defy-first/' | relative_url }}) UFO reports became much less mysterious once investigators compared the witnesses’ viewing direction, time and apparent movement with the positions of the Moon or Venus. The clearest examples are a “structured” light seen through binoculars at Épinay-sous-Sénart in 1980, a yellow-orange sphere apparently accompanying a car between Briis-sous-Forges and Limours in 2026, and a second Moon-like object reported at Gif-sur-Yvette in 2003.



<img src="{{ "/assets/images/Essonne_7f5f7b_moon_venus_false_mot_276ab5-overview.webp" | relative_url }}" alt="Overview image for Sky Illusions" loading="eager" decoding="sync" fetchpriority="high">

These cases matter because they show that an ordinary astronomical source need not look ordinary. Near the horizon, the Moon can appear enlarged, orange, distorted and surprisingly close. A remote light can seem to move when the observer or surrounding landscape moves. Venus, dazzling against a dark sky, can acquire false edges or “structures” when viewed through imperfect optics or window glass. In Essonne, these mechanisms have produced sincere reports whose unusual appearance was real to the observer, even though the source itself was familiar.

## The structured Venus at Épinay-sous-Sénart

At about 3.30 am on 23 August 1980, a woman living on the fourth floor of a block of flats at Épinay-sous-Sénart noticed an exceptionally bright, stationary light in the east. It was visible through her curtains and resembled a very large, brilliant star. She looked again at roughly the same time two mornings later and found the object in almost the same place. On the second occasion she watched it for around two hours and examined it with binoculars.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/sites/default/files/Notes%20d%27enquete50.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on cnes-geipan.fr.</span></span></span>

The binocular view transformed a point of light into something apparently constructed. The witness drew a dark oval base, two luminous sloping lines, a dark upper portion and what appeared to be antenna-like projections. The object gradually faded and disappeared at about 5.30 am. After reading a newspaper report of unusual lights elsewhere in the Paris region, she contacted the gendarmerie. Officers interviewed her and inspected the location, but found no additional witness.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/sites/default/files/Notes%20d%27enquete50.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on cnes-geipan.fr.</span></span></span>

The original GEPAN investigators already considered Venus a likely explanation, although the file was initially placed in category C because the available evidence was limited. A later GEIPAN re-examination reconstructed the sky for Épinay-sous-Sénart. Venus was low in the east at the stated time, at an azimuth of about 63 degrees and an elevation of roughly three degrees. Its position on 25 August was nearly identical to its position two days earlier, matching the witness’s observation that the light reappeared in the same area.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/sites/default/files/Notes%20d%27enquete50.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on cnes-geipan.fr.</span></span></span>

The disappearance also fitted an astronomical source. The light faded as dawn approached, when the brightening sky would steadily reduce the contrast of Venus. GEIPAN found that an earlier astronomical document had been misread because its times were given in Universal Time rather than local time; correcting that error strengthened rather than weakened the Venus identification. The case is now classified A, meaning that GEIPAN regards the identification as established.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/sites/default/files/Notes%20d%27enquete50.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on cnes-geipan.fr.</span></span></span>

### Why Venus acquired “antennae”

The witness’s detailed sketch does not by itself show that the source possessed a detailed physical structure. Venus is far too small to be resolved as a shaped object with ordinary binoculars. What the viewer receives is an intense point of light, altered by the optical system, atmospheric turbulence and any glass between the observer and the sky.

GEIPAN noted that the witness apparently used binoculars from behind the apartment window and did not report opening it. Reflections between glass surfaces, dirt or moisture on the window, poor focusing, misaligned binoculars and optical flare can spread a brilliant point into lines, lobes or dark-edged forms. Once those irregularities resemble parts of a familiar object, the brain may organise them into a stable structure.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/sites/default/files/Notes%20d%27enquete50.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on cnes-geipan.fr.</span></span></span>

The strongest evidence for Venus is therefore not a vague resemblance. It is the combination of several independent matches: the correct compass direction, the correct low elevation, exceptional brightness, immobility, repetition at the same time on two mornings and gradual loss of visibility during dawn. The complex binocular appearance is the least reliable part of the report because it depended most heavily on viewing conditions and interpretation.

The case also illustrates why witness sincerity and mistaken identification are not opposites. GEIPAN explicitly stated that the woman’s honesty and credibility were not in doubt. Its conclusion was that she accurately reported what she perceived but assigned an artificial structure to a distorted view of Venus under nocturnal, tiring conditions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cnes-geipan.fr/fr/cas/1980-08-01688" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">GEIPANEPINAY-SOUS-SENART (91) 23.08.1980 &#124; GEIPAN...</span></span></span>

## Why the Moon seemed to follow a moving car

A particularly clear Essonne example occurred between Briis-sous-Forges and Limours shortly before dawn on 1 January 2026. Between about 6.35 and 6.40 am, a motorist saw a diffuse yellow-orange sphere ahead of the vehicle. Its outline appeared slightly irregular, it seemed to be moving at low altitude, and it disappeared rapidly near the horizon. The observation lasted less than a minute.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cnes-geipan.fr/fr/cas/2026-01-51730" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">GEIPAN[R] DE BRIIS-SOUS-FORGES (91) VERS LIMOURS (91) 01.01.2026 &#124; GEIPAN...</span></span></span>

The most unsettling feature was the apparent relationship between the light and the car. As the witness drove, the object seemed to move with the vehicle or maintain its place ahead. This behaviour can suggest deliberate pursuit because nearby objects normally change position when an observer travels past them.

GEIPAN reconstructed the journey from the witness’s questionnaire, drawings and estimated directions. The reported position closely matched the Moon, which was setting very low in the north-west at an azimuth of approximately 309 degrees. Clouds in that direction could account for its quick disappearance, while the low elevation and intervening atmosphere explained its orange colour and softened outline. The case was classified A as an observation of the setting Moon.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cnes-geipan.fr/fr/cas/2026-01-51730" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">GEIPAN[R] DE BRIIS-SOUS-FORGES (91) VERS LIMOURS (91) 01.01.2026 &#124; GEIPAN...</span></span></span>

The illusion of accompaniment follows from simple perspective. A tree, house or road sign is close enough for the observer’s changing position to produce a large shift against the background. The Moon is so distant that travelling a few hundred metres makes almost no visible difference to its direction. The landscape therefore sweeps past while the Moon remains nearly fixed, creating the impression that the distant light is keeping pace with the car.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cnes-geipan.fr/fr/cas/2026-01-51730" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">GEIPAN[R] DE BRIIS-SOUS-FORGES (91) VERS LIMOURS (91) 01.01.2026 &#124; GEIPAN...</span></span></span>

This effect becomes especially persuasive on a winding or undulating road. Trees may repeatedly hide and reveal the light; bends place it alternately ahead or to one side; slopes make it seem to rise or descend. The observer experiences a sequence of changing views and may naturally attribute those changes to the object rather than to the car, road and foreground.

The Moon’s appearance near the horizon adds to the misidentification. NASA notes that a low Moon commonly looks more yellow or orange than one high in the sky and may also appear unusually large, although the apparent enlargement is principally a perceptual illusion rather than a physical magnification by the atmosphere. Atmospheric refraction can also flatten or distort its outline close to the horizon.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://science.nasa.gov/solar-system/moon/the-moon-illusion-why-does-the-moon-look-so-big-sometimes/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: science.nasa.gov">[NASA Science]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">science.nasa.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Science The Moon Illusion: Why Does the Moon Look So Big Sometimes?</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">NASA ScienceJanuary 24, 2023 — * The Moon DOES look more yellow near the horizon... There&#x27;s one notable way in which the Moon&#x27;s appearan...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: January 24, 2023</span></span></span>

The Briis–Limours report is unusually useful because it preserves more than a label such as “probably the Moon”. GEIPAN had a reconstructed route, estimated bearings, drawings and astronomical data. Although there was only one witness and no usable recording, the directional agreement was strong enough for investigators to reject a separate moving object.

## Moon dogs, memory and delayed reports

The Gif-sur-Yvette case of 5 December 2003 involved neither a spacecraft-like form nor an apparently pursuing light. Instead, the witness saw what looked like a second Moon.

Between 8.00 and 8.30 pm, while standing by a window and speaking on the telephone, the observer noticed a stationary white oval close to the real Moon. It appeared almost identical in size, colour and brightness and vanished after roughly 15 to 20 seconds. No photograph or second witness was obtained. More importantly, the account was not submitted until June 2014, more than ten years after the event.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cnes-geipan.fr/fr/cas/2003-12-08708?field_is_revisited_value=All&amp;field_type_de_cas_target_id=All&amp;order=field_date_d_observation&amp;page=59&amp;sort=asc" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">GEIPANGIF-SUR-YVETTE (91) 05.12.2003 &#124; GEIPAN...</span></span></span>

GEIPAN classified the case B: probably a paraselene, commonly called a Moon dog. This is the lunar equivalent of a sun dog, produced when moonlight is refracted through suitably aligned hexagonal ice crystals in the atmosphere. Such a patch can resemble a separate luminous object positioned beside the Moon. The delay is crucial. A witness may retain a vivid central image—“a second white Moon”—while losing dependable information about compass direction, cloud cover, angular separation or the exact date and time. Memory does not normally preserve an event as an untouched recording. Each later recollection can retain the striking feature while simplifying or shifting its surroundings. GEIPAN therefore treated errors in the remembered star field and lunar position as likely consequences of the decade-long interval.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cnes-geipan.fr/fr/cas/2003-12-08708?field_is_revisited_value=All&amp;field_type_de_cas_target_id=All&amp;order=field_date_d_observation&amp;page=59&amp;sort=asc" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">GEIPANGIF-SUR-YVETTE (91) 05.12.2003 &#124; GEIPAN...</span></span></span>

This is why the Gif-sur-Yvette report did not receive the same classification as the Épinay Venus case or the Briis–Limours Moon case. Category A requires a firmer identification. At Gif-sur-Yvette, a paraselene best explains the reported appearance, but there were too few contemporary measurements to demonstrate that explanation conclusively.

## False motion is not one single illusion

The Essonne cases involve several related but distinct ways in which a stationary or predictable object can appear active.

**Movement caused by the observer.** In the Briis–Limours case, the car and foreground were moving while the Moon’s bearing changed very little. The contrast produced an apparent following motion.

**Movement caused by partial obstruction.** Thin cloud, branches, rooftops or terrain can hide and reveal an astronomical body. A light that repeatedly appears in different gaps may seem to jump, descend or manoeuvre.

**Apparent motion in a dark, reference-poor sky.** A small isolated light can seem to wander when there are few fixed visual references. This perceptual effect, known as autokinesis, is most persuasive when the observer stares at a bright point against darkness. It should not be used as a universal explanation, but it can amplify uncertainty in reports of stars or planets that appear to sway or drift.

**Slow genuine celestial motion misjudged as local movement.** The Moon, Venus and stars do move across the sky because of Earth’s rotation and their own changing positions. Over a long observation, that gradual motion can be noticed without providing useful clues to distance. A witness may correctly see displacement but incorrectly infer a nearby object travelling through the lower atmosphere.

**Optical deformation mistaken for mechanical change.** Atmospheric turbulence, defocus, binocular shake and window reflections can make a point source expand, split, pulse or develop spikes. The resulting form may appear to rotate or reconfigure even though the astronomical source remains unchanged.

Investigators therefore need to separate three questions that witnesses naturally experience as one: what emitted or reflected the light, what changed in the image reaching the eye, and what movement was inferred from that change.

## What made the explanations convincing

Calling a report “the Moon” or “Venus” is not sufficient unless the proposed object was actually visible where and when the witness looked. The strongest Essonne identifications relied on several converging checks.

At Épinay-sous-Sénart, Venus matched the eastern direction, low angular height, exceptional brightness, repeated timing and dawn disappearance. At Briis–Limours, the setting Moon matched the route-facing bearing, low position, orange colour and apparent accompaniment of the car. At Gif-sur-Yvette, the paraselene interpretation matched the resemblance to the adjacent Moon, but the delayed and incomplete account prevented an equally secure conclusion.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/sites/default/files/Notes%20d%27enquete50.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on cnes-geipan.fr.</span></span></span>

This comparison also shows why dramatic detail does not necessarily make a case stronger. The Épinay witness supplied antenna-like features, yet those details were produced under the least controlled part of the observation: binocular viewing through glass. The Briis–Limours witness described a moving orange sphere, but a reconstructed route and astronomical bearing explained the movement. The plainer Gif description—a second Moon—was highly compatible with a recognised atmospheric effect, yet the ten-year delay left important facts unrecoverable.

For Essonne’s wider UFO history, these are not trivial “mistakes” to be discarded. They demonstrate how investigators can move from an unusual narrative to a testable reconstruction. They also establish a useful standard for less readily explained reports: precise time, direction, elevation, route, duration, weather and contemporary documentation matter more than the vividness of the object’s apparent shape.

## The lasting lesson from Essonne’s sky illusions


<div class="content-callout content-callout--key content-callout--takeaway" markdown="1">

The astronomical cases in Essonne weaken the claim that every structured, mobile or low-looking light represents an unknown vehicle. They do not imply that witnesses invented their experiences. Rather, they show that perception combines a real stimulus with incomplete information about scale, distance and movement.

</div>

Venus can look engineered when intense light is distorted by binoculars, windows and expectations. The Moon can seem to fly alongside a car because distant objects do not shift like the nearby landscape. Ice crystals can produce a second Moon-like patch whose brief appearance is genuinely unusual. In each instance, the decisive evidence comes from matching the report with the sky and viewing geometry, not from dismissing the witness.

The three cases also deserve different levels of confidence. Épinay-sous-Sénart and Briis–Limours are strong identifications supported by positional reconstruction and are classified A by GEIPAN. Gif-sur-Yvette remains a probable atmospheric-optics case, classified B, because memory delay and missing contemporary evidence prevent certainty. That distinction—securely explained, probably explained and merely unresolved—is central to reading Essonne’s UFO record fairly.
