## Introduction

Official investigation changed [Vienne]({{ 'vienne/' | relative_url }})’s UFO story by replacing a loose collection of unusual testimonies with a graded body of evidence. The department’s files no longer support a simple choice between “explained” and “mysterious”. They show several very different outcomes: bright planets mistaken for hovering lights, lanterns interpreted as silent formations, satellites and spacecraft seen without recognition, meteors reconstructed from camera data, and reports that remain unclassified only because the evidence is too weak.



<img src="{{ "/assets/images/Vienne_f0511b_vienne_official_ufo_b304fa-overview.webp" | relative_url }}" alt="Overview image for Official Files" loading="eager" decoding="sync" fetchpriority="high">

That distinction matters most in the cases labelled C. In GEIPAN’s system, a C case is not a phenomenon that resisted a complete investigation; it is one that could not be identified because reliable information was missing. Vienne’s best-known example, the 2009 Chasseneuil-du-Poitou report, therefore occupies a much weaker evidential position than its unusual description might suggest. Across the department, the official files have generally weakened extraordinary interpretations while preserving a smaller residue of uncertainty.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://geipan.fr/fr/recherche/cas/tab?field_date_value=2007-03-01&amp;field_is_new_value=All&amp;field_is_revisited_value=All&amp;field_type_de_cas_target_id=All&amp;order=field_departement_textuel&amp;page=6&amp;sort=desc" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: geipan.fr">[Geipan]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Recherche de cas &#124; GEIPAN</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Recherche de cas &#124; GEIPAN...</span></span></span>

## How reports enter the French investigation system

France has maintained an official system for examining unusual aerial reports since 1977. The present body, GEIPAN, operates within the national space agency CNES and continues the work of its predecessors GEPAN and SEPRA. Its role is to collect testimony, analyse it, investigate where possible, archive the material and publish anonymised conclusions. It does not set out to prove alien visitation, and it deliberately uses the wider term “unidentified aerospace phenomenon” because witnesses do not always observe a clearly defined physical object.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cnes.fr/en/projects/geipan" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes.fr">[CNES]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">GEIPAN7 Jul 2025 — GEIPAN, the French UAP research and information group created by CNES in 1977, collects, analyses and archives inf...</span></span></span>

A modern report normally begins with a detailed technical questionnaire completed by a direct witness. Investigators seek the exact time, location, direction of view, angular movement, duration, weather, visibility and circumstances of the observation. Drawings, photographs, video and other records may be added. A case can then be assigned to a trained investigator, who may contact witnesses, visit the location or reconstruct lines of sight. GEIPAN also works with the gendarmerie, police, civil aviation, the armed forces, Météo-France and specialists in fields such as astronomy, meteorology, imaging, plasma physics and psychology.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/en/what-did-i-see/step-1" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-title">GEIPANUnderstanding a Phenomenon &#124; GEIPAN</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">GEIPANUnderstanding a Phenomenon &#124; GEIPAN</span></span></span>

This procedure changes the value of a sighting in two ways. First, it turns an impression such as “a light moved strangely” into claims that can be checked against aircraft routes, astronomical positions, satellite passes, wind direction and other external data. Secondly, it records how much of that checking is actually possible. An intriguing account with no secure time, direction or independent witness may remain unidentified, but it does not carry the same weight as an unexplained event supported by radar, photographs or multiple independent observations.

That is why the official archive looks very different from newspaper and UFO-catalogue accounts. Press stories naturally foreground the most striking description. GEIPAN foregrounds whether the description can be tested.

## What the GEIPAN categories really mean

GEIPAN classifies reports according to both their remaining strangeness and their consistency. “Strangeness” concerns how poorly the observation matches known phenomena after investigation. “Consistency” concerns the quantity, reliability and objectivity of the available information. A remarkable story can therefore receive a low evidential rating if it comes from one uncertain account, while a spectacular fireball may be classified with confidence because cameras and independent reports establish what happened.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/en/node/58788" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-title">GEIPANMethodology &#124; GEIPAN</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">GEIPANMethodology &#124; GEIPAN</span></span></span>

The four main categories are easily misunderstood:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* **A:** the phenomenon has been identified with high confidence.
* **B:** an ordinary explanation is considered probable, although the evidence is not complete enough for category A.
* **C:** no identification can be made because information is missing or unreliable.
* **D:** the phenomenon remains unidentified after an investigation supported by sufficiently substantial evidence.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/en/node/412" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">cation D: Phenomenon not identified after investigation...</span></span></span>

</div>

For readers of Vienne’s files, the crucial distinction is between C and D. Both may be casually described as “unexplained”, but they mean different things. A D classification says investigators had enough material to test the main conventional hypotheses and still failed to identify the phenomenon. A C classification says they could not perform that level of testing.

The official Vienne list is notable for what it does **not** contain: the published cases visible in the department’s current archive are predominantly A, B and C, rather than a strong group of well-supported D cases. The database includes identifications involving Venus, Jupiter, aircraft, satellites, the International Space Station and Space Shuttle, lanterns, a meteor and, most recently, projected light reflected from clouds. Its C entries are generally marked by missing reliable information rather than by verified extraordinary behaviour.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://geipan.fr/fr/recherche/cas/tab?field_date_value=2007-03-01&amp;field_is_new_value=All&amp;field_is_revisited_value=All&amp;field_type_de_cas_target_id=All&amp;order=field_departement_textuel&amp;page=6&amp;sort=desc" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: geipan.fr">[Geipan]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Recherche de cas &#124; GEIPAN</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Recherche de cas &#124; GEIPAN...</span></span></span>

Categories are also not intended to be permanent monuments. GEIPAN states that C and D cases can be revisited when new information becomes available. Nationally, the organisation has re-examined older cases using improved digital tools and stricter criteria; it reported that 50 older D files were reinvestigated and explained in 2017. This history is a warning against treating any archive label as a final scientific verdict.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/en/missions-methodes-et-resultats" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-title">GEIPANMission &amp; Geipan &#124; GEIPAN</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">GEIPANMission &amp; Geipan &#124; GEIPAN</span></span></span>

## Explained Vienne cases changed the pattern

The most important contribution of the official files is cumulative. Any single explanation may seem unremarkable, but together they reveal the recurring sources of Vienne’s apparent UFOs.

### Lanterns over Poitiers

Shortly after midnight on 5 September 2009, two witnesses in Poitiers watched four intense orange lights move north to south for about five minutes. The lights made no sound, appeared to rise and then disappeared towards the west. Those features can sound impressive when presented without context: several lights, a formation, silence and an unusual trajectory.

GEIPAN classified the case B, concluding that it probably involved Thai-style sky lanterns. Their colour, slow wind-driven movement, apparent ascent and silence matched the account. Two closely dated reports elsewhere in Vienne — another Poitiers observation on 6 September and a Nieuil-l’Espoir case on 3 October — received the same probable explanation. Rather than three independent mysteries, the archive points to a recognisable type of misidentification recurring across the department.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/fr/cas/2009-09-02439" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">GEIPANPOITIERS (86) 05.09.2009 &#124; GEIPAN...</span></span></span>

The importance of these cases lies less in proving the precise source of each individual light than in showing how easily lanterns generate classic UFO characteristics. At night, distance is hard to judge, wind is not visible and separate lights may appear to maintain an organised formation. A B classification preserves some uncertainty while making clear that an exotic explanation is unnecessary.

### Spacecraft mistaken for a strange conjunction

At Saint-Martin-l’Ars on 17 March 2009, a report was eventually classified A as the International Space Station and the Space Shuttle. This was a period when the two vehicles could appear as conspicuous moving lights following related paths. Without an orbital check, such a pairing could be remembered as structured or coordinated objects. With accurate timing and satellite data, the sighting became a straightforward aerospace identification.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://geipan.fr/fr/recherche/cas/tab?field_date_value=2007-03-01&amp;field_is_new_value=All&amp;field_is_revisited_value=All&amp;field_type_de_cas_target_id=All&amp;order=field_departement_textuel&amp;page=6&amp;sort=desc" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: geipan.fr">[Geipan]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Recherche de cas &#124; GEIPAN</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Recherche de cas &#124; GEIPAN...</span></span></span>

Other Vienne reports show the same value of astronomical and orbital comparison. A 2010 case at Civray was classified B as Jupiter. Reports from Latillé in 2016 and La Villedieu-du-Clain in 2015 were attributed probably to satellites. Montmorillon’s 2015 observation was classified A as Venus. These files undermine the impression that repeated sightings necessarily indicate a local “flap”; several occurred because familiar objects were seen under unfamiliar conditions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://geipan.fr/fr/recherche/cas/tab?field_date_value=2007-03-01&amp;field_is_new_value=All&amp;field_is_revisited_value=All&amp;field_type_de_cas_target_id=All&amp;order=field_departement_textuel&amp;page=6&amp;sort=desc" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: geipan.fr">[Geipan]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Recherche de cas &#124; GEIPAN</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Recherche de cas &#124; GEIPAN...</span></span></span>

### The Bonnes fireball was reconstructed, not merely guessed

The 30 September 2015 observation near Bonnes provides one of the department’s clearest examples of strong positive identification. Witnesses across a wide area of France reported an exceptionally bright object crossing the sky at about 22:53. The Vienne report was not assessed from testimony alone. Automatic cameras operated by GEIPAN and observatories at the Pic du Midi and Rochechouart recorded the event, allowing investigators to model its trajectory in three dimensions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/fr/cas/2015-09-09328" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on cnes-geipan.fr.</span></span></span>

The reconstruction placed the luminous path at roughly 60 kilometres altitude. Its short duration implied an initial speed above eight kilometres per second, supporting the conclusion that it was a natural meteoroid entering the atmosphere. Investigators also checked whether a catalogued piece of space debris had re-entered over France and found no matching event. GEIPAN therefore classified the report A: a fireball of natural origin.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/fr/cas/2015-09-09328" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on cnes-geipan.fr.</span></span></span>

This case demonstrates what a high-quality official explanation looks like. It combines multiple witnesses, independent cameras, geometrical reconstruction, speed estimation and the elimination of a competing aerospace hypothesis. The object was genuinely dramatic; the explanation did not depend on dismissing the witnesses or claiming they had imagined it.

### Antran shows how local geography can create a UFO effect

The Antran case of 29 August 2024 is a recent illustration of the same method applied to a less familiar source. Two witnesses saw pairs of luminous points moving rapidly across a lightly clouded night sky. The paths appeared parallel and seemed to converge near the horizon. With no visible beams joining the lights to the ground, the display could appear detached from any obvious human activity.

GEIPAN’s investigation connected the lights to the Futuroscope night show near Poitiers. Projectors and lasers were striking a layer of cloud, while clear air below the cloud made the beams themselves difficult or impossible to see from Antran. The witnesses therefore saw moving patches of reflected light without the visual cue that would reveal their source. The case was published in 2026 as category A.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cnes-geipan.fr/fr/cas/2024-08-51565" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on cnes-geipan.fr.</span></span></span>

This explanation is especially relevant to Vienne because it is rooted in a distinctive local feature. Futuroscope’s large-scale night displays can create luminous effects visible beyond the immediate park, depending on cloud height and atmospheric conditions. Future reports of moving lights around northern Poitiers therefore need to be checked against event schedules and weather before they are treated as evidence of a separate aerial object.

## Why Chasseneuil remains uncertain but weak

The 24 September 2009 Chasseneuil-du-Poitou case is the most striking C file in Vienne’s official archive. According to the account, a seven-year-old child saw a white rectangular or box-like object following an aircraft. It allegedly tilted, descended sharply, stopped below a power line, moved towards a neighbouring television aerial, rose at very high speed, stopped again and then vanished vertically. The episode reportedly lasted 20 to 30 seconds.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/fr/cas/2009-09-02430" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">GEIPAN(CAN) CHASSENEUIL-DU-POITOU (86) 24.09.2009 &#124; GEIPAN...</span></span></span>

Taken literally, the described manoeuvres do not fit an obvious aircraft, balloon or astronomical object. GEIPAN noted that the object appeared to travel against the measured wind and that no ordinary hypothesis accounted for all the reported movements. Yet the agency did not classify the case D. It classified it C because the evidential foundation was too weak.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/fr/cas/2009-09-02430" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">GEIPAN(CAN) CHASSENEUIL-DU-POITOU (86) 24.09.2009 &#124; GEIPAN...</span></span></span>

The observation was made by a young child and relayed to investigators through his father. There was no second witness, photograph, video, radar record or opportunity for a sufficiently detailed direct interview. GEIPAN also noted a potentially important contextual factor: the father had recently reported a different observation that he later recognised as sky lanterns. Investigators did not accuse either parent or child of dishonesty, but considered that family discussion of the earlier event might have influenced how an ambiguous sight was noticed, remembered or described.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/fr/cas/2009-09-02430" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">GEIPAN(CAN) CHASSENEUIL-DU-POITOU (86) 24.09.2009 &#124; GEIPAN...</span></span></span>

The resulting classification is cautious rather than evasive. It leaves open the possibility that the child saw something real and unusual, while refusing to treat the reported trajectory as established fact. In evidential terms, “no conventional explanation fits the account” is not the same as “a conventional object has been ruled out”. The account itself may contain errors in perceived distance, direction, shape or sequence.

This is the central lesson of Vienne’s official record. Its most extraordinary-sounding modern case is not its strongest case. Bonnes has stronger evidence but an ordinary explanation. Chasseneuil has a stranger narrative but much weaker evidence.

## Where official investigation has limits

Official status does not make every classification infallible. French sceptical researchers have criticised earlier GEPAN and SEPRA work for leaving plausible astronomical, aviation and re-entry explanations insufficiently tested, and for allowing some weak cases to remain in the unexplained category. Their 2009 review argued that older classifications could reflect uneven methods, delayed investigations and an excessive willingness to associate uncertain physical traces with reported objects.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://skepticalinquirer.org/wp-content/uploads/sites/29/2009/01/p47.pdf?ref=thegalacticmind.com" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: skepticalinquirer.org">[Skeptical Inquirer]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">skepticalinquirer.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Skeptical Inquirer</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Skeptical Inquirer</span></span></span>

GEIPAN’s own later practice partly acknowledges these weaknesses. It describes testimony as fragile, recognises that old cases often lacked today’s digital resources, applies stricter standards to D classifications and permits files to be revisited. The fall in the proportion of recently unexplained cases is therefore not necessarily evidence that the sky has become less mysterious. It may reflect better satellite databases, improved astronomical software, easier access to weather and radar information, more cameras, and a more demanding threshold for calling a case genuinely unexplained.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/en/missions-methodes-et-resultats" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-title">GEIPANMission &amp; Geipan &#124; GEIPAN</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">GEIPANMission &amp; Geipan &#124; GEIPAN</span></span></span>

There are also practical limits. GEIPAN is a small unit supported by partner organisations and volunteer investigators. Only a minority of reports lead to site visits, and a detailed investigation can consume substantial time. Reports submitted late, second-hand accounts and cases without a firm time or location may never become testable, however sincere the witness.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/en/what-did-i-see/step-1" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-title">GEIPANUnderstanding a Phenomenon &#124; GEIPAN</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">GEIPANUnderstanding a Phenomenon &#124; GEIPAN</span></span></span>

For Vienne’s historical cases from 1954 and the Moulière reports of 1976, this creates an important divide. Many survive chiefly through press archives or later catalogues rather than through complete contemporary files built to modern standards. They remain part of the department’s UFO history, but they cannot simply be placed on the same evidential level as a recent case supported by questionnaires, weather records, orbital calculations or camera triangulation.

## What Vienne’s official files actually show

The official archive has not erased Vienne’s UFO story. It has made it more precise. Reports still begin with genuine surprise, and some descriptions remain difficult to explain. What has changed is the meaning attached to that difficulty.

Most published Vienne cases show how ordinary sources become extraordinary experiences when distance, scale and context are missing. Venus and Jupiter can look like persistent hovering lights. Satellites and crewed spacecraft can appear coordinated. Lanterns can form silent orange groups. Aircraft can seem stationary or oddly shaped. Meteors can produce spectacular movement over a vast area. Projected light can appear to move independently across cloud.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://geipan.fr/fr/recherche/cas/tab?field_date_value=2007-03-01&amp;field_is_new_value=All&amp;field_is_revisited_value=All&amp;field_type_de_cas_target_id=All&amp;order=field_departement_textuel&amp;page=6&amp;sort=desc" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Recherche de cas &#124; GEIPAN</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Recherche de cas &#124; GEIPAN...</span></span></span>

The remaining C cases do not form a secure core of unexplained craft. They chiefly mark the boundary beyond which the available data cannot support a conclusion. Chasseneuil-du-Poitou remains interesting because its description is unusual, but its indirect child testimony and lack of corroboration weaken it sharply. Jaunay-Clan, Vouillé and La Villedieu-du-Clain are likewise listed as cases lacking reliable information, not as demonstrations of unknown technology.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://geipan.fr/fr/recherche/cas/tab?field_date_value=2007-03-01&amp;field_is_new_value=All&amp;field_is_revisited_value=All&amp;field_type_de_cas_target_id=All&amp;order=field_departement_textuel&amp;page=6&amp;sort=desc" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: geipan.fr">[Geipan]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Recherche de cas &#124; GEIPAN</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Recherche de cas &#124; GEIPAN...</span></span></span>

The most defensible reading is therefore neither that every Vienne sighting has been solved nor that official records confirm a hidden extraordinary phenomenon. The files show a spectrum: well-explained observations, probable identifications, reports too weak to assess and a continuing need to reassess older material. Official investigation changed the department’s story by moving attention away from how strange a witness’s words sound and towards the harder question of how much reliable evidence survives behind them.
