## Introduction

On the evening of 5 November 1990, people across [Ille-et-Vilaine]({{ 'which-ille-et-vilaine-ufo-cases-still/' | relative_url }}) saw groups of brilliant lights moving across a clear sky. Some described a luminous procession; others perceived an enormous dark triangle or elongated craft carrying several lights. Similar reports arrived almost simultaneously from much of France, creating one of the country’s largest and most memorable UFO waves.



<img src="{{ "/assets/images/Ille-et-Vilaine_a0a684_1990_rocket_reentry_5c81de-overview.webp" | relative_url }}" alt="Overview image for 1990 Wave" loading="eager" decoding="sync" fetchpriority="high">

The central event is now well explained. France’s space agency, CNES, identified the phenomenon as the atmospheric re-entry and fragmentation of a Soviet Proton rocket stage associated with the launch of the Gorizont 21 communications satellite. Orbital calculations matched the timing, direction and national spread of the observations. GEIPAN, the CNES unit responsible for investigating unusual aerospace reports, classifies the event as category A: identified without significant ambiguity.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/fr/node/51509" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">GEIPANRENTREE ATMOSPHERIQUE 5 NOVEMBRE 1990 &#124; GEIPAN...</span></span></span>

Its importance to Ille-et-Vilaine’s UFO history lies not in an unresolved mystery, but in what it reveals about eyewitness evidence. A real and extraordinary event occurred, yet its height, distance and structure were widely misperceived. Separate burning fragments moving together could convincingly resemble the lights of one vast, low-flying craft.

## What witnesses across Ille-et-Vilaine saw

The main display began at about 7 pm. CNES later reported receiving information from more than a thousand witnesses nationwide, many through the National Gendarmerie. The phenomenon crossed the sky over several minutes rather than flashing past like a conventional meteor, giving observers time to watch it, interpret it and sometimes alert other people.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/fr/node/51509" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">GEIPANRENTREE ATMOSPHERIQUE 5 NOVEMBRE 1990 &#124; GEIPAN...</span></span></span>

GEIPAN’s national case file contains statements from numerous towns and departments. The Ille-et-Vilaine material includes reports associated with Sougéal, Saint-Broladre, Saint-Senoux, Châteaugiron and Nouvoitou. These locations are spread from the department’s northern edge near Mont-Saint-Michel Bay to the country south-east of Rennes, showing that the local sightings were not confined to one village or one viewing position.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/fr/node/51509" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">GEIPANRENTREE ATMOSPHERIQUE 5 NOVEMBRE 1990 &#124; GEIPAN...</span></span></span>

The descriptions varied, but several recurring elements also appeared elsewhere in France:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* numerous white, yellow, orange or reddish lights;
* a broad formation travelling generally from west-south-west towards east-north-east;
* glowing trails or smaller lights apparently following larger ones;
* slow, smooth and often silent movement;
* an impression that the phenomenon was at aircraft height;
* a dark triangular, rectangular or cigar-shaped body seemingly connecting the lights.

Those reports should not be dismissed as inventions. Witnesses were responding to an unusually large, bright and complex spectacle. Even CNES astronaut Jean-Pierre Haigneré observed the event and regarded it as striking, while technicians at the CNES balloon facility at Aire-sur-l’Adour delayed a launch to watch it. The experience was genuinely dramatic even though its cause was conventional.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/fr/node/51509" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">GEIPANRENTREE ATMOSPHERIQUE 5 NOVEMBRE 1990 &#124; GEIPAN...</span></span></span>

The geographical pattern is crucial. An individual report of a slow triangular object over Ille-et-Vilaine might initially invite comparison with aircraft, helicopters or the unresolved Noyal-sur-Vilaine sighting of 26 October 1990. On 5 November, however, comparable observations appeared along a broad corridor across France at the same time. That large-scale coherence points away from a local craft and towards a distant phenomenon visible over hundreds of kilometres.

## How the Soviet rocket stage was identified

The object responsible for the wave was catalogued as 20925, with the international designation 1990-094C. It was connected with the Soviet Gorizont 21 mission, launched aboard a Proton-K rocket from Baikonur on 3 November 1990, two days before the sightings. Gorizont 21 itself was a communications satellite intended for geostationary orbit; the object that re-entered was discarded launch hardware rather than the working satellite.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/fr/node/51509" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[cnes-geipan.fr]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">GEIPANRENTREE ATMOSPHERIQUE 5 NOVEMBRE 1990 &#124; GEIPAN...</span></span></span>

CNES did not announce the answer instantly. Its security services were overwhelmed by calls on the evening of the event, and SEPRA—the predecessor of GEIPAN—began checking with civil and military aviation authorities, meteorological services and organisations tracking objects in orbit. NASA supplied confirmation on 8 November that object 1990-094C had re-entered on 5 November. CNES orbital specialists then confirmed a track crossing France from the Bay of Biscay towards Alsace, and the finding was released to news agencies on 9 November.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/fr/node/51509" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">GEIPANRENTREE ATMOSPHERIQUE 5 NOVEMBRE 1990 &#124; GEIPAN...</span></span></span>

A member of an experienced observing network had already alerted SEPRA on 6 November that the phenomenon was probably the re-entry of 1990-094C. According to the GEIPAN account, this observer calculated that the object passed roughly 103 kilometres above the Bay of Biscay and left eastern France at about 83 kilometres altitude. The early identification matters because it was based on orbital information, not an explanation invented later merely to fit the publicity.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/fr/node/51509" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">GEIPANRENTREE ATMOSPHERIQUE 5 NOVEMBRE 1990 &#124; GEIPAN...</span></span></span>

The case was also reported in the contemporary national press. By 10 November, *Le Monde* described the UFO as identified with the disintegration of parts of a Soviet rocket during atmospheric re-entry. A follow-up account noted that the debris had spent a short period in orbit before entering the denser atmosphere on the evening of 5 November.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.lemonde.fr/archives/article/1990/11/10/l-ovni-du-5-novembre-est-identifie_3978101_1819218.html?srsltid=AfmBOorQs9vJA_kC3lylIlLOFaB4nZQtJ7VnL4KPED_f42E4iRYYADOC" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: lemonde.fr">[Le Monde.fr]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">lemonde.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-title">l ovni du 5 novembre est identifie 3978101 1819218</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Le Monde.frL&#x27;OVNI du 5 novembre est... identifié10 Nov 1990 — Il s&#x27;agit de la désintégration, lors de la rentrée dans l&#x27;atmosphère, de mo...</span></span></span>

### The reconstructed path

GEIPAN later modelled the event using CNES software designed to simulate the atmospheric braking of debris with different shapes and densities. The simplified model represented the rocket stage with a cylindrical body and two spherical tank-like components. In reality, fragmentation probably produced tens or hundreds of pieces with different masses, forms and rates of deceleration.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/fr/node/53" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">GEIPANNouvelle simulation de la rentrée atmosphérique du 5 novembre 1990 &#124; GEIPAN...</span></span></span>

The reconstruction placed the beginning of major fragmentation at approximately 94 kilometres altitude over the Bay of Biscay. GEIPAN acknowledged uncertainty about exactly how far the debris cloud stretched along its direction of travel, because the number and physical properties of the pieces were unknown. The cross-track position—the corridor followed across France—was considerably less uncertain.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/fr/node/53" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">GEIPANNouvelle simulation de la rentrée atmosphérique du 5 novembre 1990 &#124; GEIPAN...</span></span></span>

During the visible phase, the fragments descended through roughly 90 to 50 kilometres altitude. Their speed fell from around 7 kilometres per second to about 2 kilometres per second, equivalent to approximately 25,000 to 7,200 kilometres per hour. Those figures are incompatible with the slow aircraft-height object perceived by many witnesses, but they are consistent with orbital debris losing speed as it broke apart in the upper atmosphere.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/fr/node/51509" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">GEIPANRENTREE ATMOSPHERIQUE 5 NOVEMBRE 1990 &#124; GEIPAN...</span></span></span>

## Why separate lights looked like solid craft

The hardest part of the rocket explanation is not establishing that a re-entry occurred. The orbital identification is strong. The harder question is why sincere observers—including experienced aircrew—described an object that seemed structured, low and sometimes enormous.

The answer begins with the night sky’s lack of reliable distance cues. When an unfamiliar light is seen against darkness, the observer generally has no known size, surface detail or background reference from which to calculate its distance. Brightness can create an unconscious impression of proximity. Once a witness assumes that the lights are only a few hundred metres away, their apparent angular separation implies a craft hundreds of metres or even kilometres long. Place the same lights tens of kilometres away, and no giant nearby vehicle is required.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/fr/node/51509" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">GEIPANRENTREE ATMOSPHERIQUE 5 NOVEMBRE 1990 &#124; GEIPAN...</span></span></span>

The re-entry was unusually easy to misjudge because the fragments were not concentrated into a single point. Pieces of the rocket stage separated according to their mass, shape and density, producing a long formation of glowing objects and trails extending over many kilometres. From the ground, the formation moved as a coordinated group. The eye and brain naturally organise lights sharing the same direction and speed into one pattern.

This perceptual tendency is sometimes called an illusory contour: observers perceive a boundary or surface even when only separate points are visible. Four or five lights can therefore become the corners and centre of a dark triangle. A row of lights can become windows or engines along a cigar-shaped vehicle. Areas of unlit sky between the fragments may be interpreted as a solid black body blocking the stars, although no connecting structure has been demonstrated. GEIPAN specifically cites this grouping effect when explaining reports of a dark craft carrying multiple lights.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/fr/node/51509" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">GEIPANRENTREE ATMOSPHERIQUE 5 NOVEMBRE 1990 &#124; GEIPAN...</span></span></span>

The phenomenon’s apparent slowness reinforced the illusion. A very distant object travelling at orbital speed can cross the observer’s field of view more gradually than a nearby aircraft. The debris also decelerated and spread out during re-entry, remaining visible for several minutes over France. Some witnesses therefore concluded that it was hovering or cruising gently, when in fact it was travelling thousands of kilometres per hour high above the atmosphere’s lower layers.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/fr/node/53" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">GEIPANNouvelle simulation de la rentrée atmosphérique du 5 novembre 1990 &#124; GEIPAN...</span></span></span>

Another useful clue was the limited change in apparent size during the passage. A truly nearby formation covering hundreds of metres should change noticeably as it approached and receded. GEIPAN argues that the relatively stable angular size described by many witnesses indicates a very remote phenomenon travelling across a broad section of the country rather than a low object passing directly over each observer.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/fr/node/51509" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">GEIPANRENTREE ATMOSPHERIQUE 5 NOVEMBRE 1990 &#124; GEIPAN...</span></span></span>

## Why the explanation remained disputed

The rocket interpretation was accepted by CNES but did not immediately end the argument. Some witnesses remained certain that the phenomenon had passed just above houses, roads or trees. Others reported beams of light, sudden changes of direction, stationary phases or objects seen outside the main time and trajectory. UFO investigators later collected hundreds of accounts and argued that the re-entry might explain only part of the evening’s activity.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/fr/node/53" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">GEIPANNouvelle simulation de la rentrée atmosphérique du 5 novembre 1990 &#124; GEIPAN...</span></span></span>

Several factors encouraged continuing doubt.

**The initial official uncertainty mattered.** SEPRA was itself surprised by the scale and diversity of the calls. Confirmation from overseas tracking records took several days, leaving a period in which television, radio and newspapers could present the event primarily as an unexplained national UFO wave. GEIPAN’s later director Xavier Passot acknowledged that the initial hesitation, the delay in obtaining NASA information and emphatic media treatment helped preserve the mystery.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/fr/node/53" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">GEIPANNouvelle simulation de la rentrée atmosphérique du 5 novembre 1990 &#124; GEIPAN...</span></span></span>

**The explanation contradicted lived experience.** A person who believed that a huge object had crossed a nearby road was being told that the lights were tens of kilometres high. That is a much greater psychological adjustment than accepting that an unknown light was a distant aircraft. The strength of the witness’s impression was real, but confidence in perceived distance is not the same as measured distance.

**The reports were highly variable.** Critics treated unusual descriptions—vertical movement, hovering or separate trajectories—as evidence for additional objects. GEIPAN’s response is that variation is expected in a large eyewitness population watching a complex event from different positions. When hundreds of accounts are collected, a minority will contain extreme estimates, timing errors or interpretations that depart from the central pattern. Selecting only those outliers can make a broadly coherent event appear to consist of several unrelated phenomena.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/fr/node/53" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">GEIPANNouvelle simulation de la rentrée atmosphérique du 5 novembre 1990 &#124; GEIPAN...</span></span></span>

That does not mean every claim made anywhere in France at any time on 5 November must automatically be assigned to the rocket. Reports significantly earlier or later than the main passage, or on incompatible trajectories, need to be considered individually. A mass sighting can coincide with aircraft, stars, planets or unrelated reports. The evidential question is whether such exceptions possess independent documentation strong enough to establish a second extraordinary event.

For the main wave, the answer remains no. The matched date, orbital object, direction, timing, duration, fragmentation pattern and enormous viewing area form a much stronger explanation than the hypothesis that multiple large craft crossed France alongside an independently confirmed rocket re-entry.

## What the case means for Ille-et-Vilaine

Within Ille-et-Vilaine, 5 November 1990 should be treated as an identified regional episode within a national event, not as a collection of separate unresolved local encounters. Sougéal, Saint-Broladre, Saint-Senoux, Châteaugiron and Nouvoitou appear in the same official archive as reports along the wider west-to-east corridor. Their value comes from helping to reconstruct the scale and appearance of the re-entry.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/fr/node/51509" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">GEIPANRENTREE ATMOSPHERIQUE 5 NOVEMBRE 1990 &#124; GEIPAN...</span></span></span>

The event also provides an important safeguard when reading other departmental cases. It shows that multiple witnesses, geographical spread and detailed descriptions do not by themselves prove the presence of an unknown vehicle. A known aerospace event can produce reports of shapes, dimensions and altitudes that bear little resemblance to its physical reality.

At the same time, the rocket explanation should not be used indiscriminately. The unresolved triangular sighting near Noyal-sur-Vilaine occurred on 26 October—ten days earlier—and is a separate case with a different time and evidential record. Its proximity in the calendar may have made the November wave feel like part of a continuing flap, but the identification of the 5 November event neither explains nor validates the earlier report. GEIPAN continues to list the two cases differently: the national re-entry as category A and Noyal-sur-Vilaine as category D, unexplained on the available information.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/en/recherche/cas/tab?customGetLattitude=46.124763699209396&amp;customGetLongitude=2.4169921874999996&amp;customGetZoom=6&amp;field_agregation_index_value=&amp;field_classification_des_cas_target_id=All&amp;field_date_d_observation_value%5Bmax%5D=&amp;field_date_d_observation_value%5Bmin%5D=&amp;field_date_value=&amp;field_departement_target_id=&amp;field_document_existe_ou_pas_value=All&amp;field_is_new_value=All&amp;field_is_revisited_value=All&amp;field_latitude_value%5Bmax%5D=50.52739681329302&amp;field_latitude_value%5Bmin%5D=41.72213058512578&amp;field_longitude_value%5Bmax%5D=9.8876953125&amp;field_longitude_value%5Bmin%5D=-5.053710937500001&amp;field_phenomene_target_id=&amp;field_type_de_cas_target_id=All&amp;order=field_date_d_observation&amp;page=38%2C0&amp;sort=asc" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-title">GEIPANRecherche de cas &#124; GEIPANRecherche de cas</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">NOYAL-SUR-VILAINE (35) 26.10.1990 &#124; 26/10/1990 &#124; D &#124; Ille-et-Vilaine &#124; Phénomène étrange à très étrange/ de consistance moyenne à forte &#124;...</span></span></span>

The lasting lesson is therefore more precise than “the witnesses were wrong”. They correctly observed an exceptional procession of lights and accurately recognised that it was unlike normal air traffic. The main errors concerned depth, scale and the assumption that lights moving together must be attached to one object. Once orbital tracking supplied an independently known rocket stage and a matching trajectory, those perceptual errors became understandable rather than mysterious.

## Assessment

The 5 November 1990 wave is one of the strongest identified cases in French UFO history. The explanation does not depend solely on resemblance to a meteor or on a general claim that witnesses are unreliable. It rests on a catalogued Soviet rocket component known to have re-entered that day, an orbit reconstructed across France, a direction matching the reports, and fragmentation behaviour capable of producing the observed procession of lights. CNES subsequently modelled the debris cloud and retained the event in GEIPAN’s highest-confidence identified category.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/fr/node/51509" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">GEIPANRENTREE ATMOSPHERIQUE 5 NOVEMBRE 1990 &#124; GEIPAN...</span></span></span>

Some peripheral statements remain difficult to test, particularly those collected long after the event or lacking precise times and viewing directions. They do not overturn the central identification. Later investigation strengthened the rocket explanation by adding trajectory modelling and a clearer account of the perceptual effects that made high-altitude fragments look like low, solid craft.

For Ille-et-Vilaine, the wave remains a landmark precisely because it began as an extraordinary local experience but became intelligible only when reports from the entire country and data from space tracking were examined together.
