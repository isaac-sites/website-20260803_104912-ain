## Introduction

Nièvre’s strangest official sighting reports remain unresolved for a less dramatic reason than the word “unidentified” suggests: the surviving evidence is not strong enough to decide what happened. The key files from 1976, 1979 and 2011 contain vivid descriptions of coloured lights, large apparent forms and abrupt disappearances, but they lack dependable measurements, timely radar checks, photographs or fully consistent testimony. GEIPAN therefore classifies them as category C—unidentifiable because reliable information is missing—not category D, meaning unexplained after a sufficiently substantial investigation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/fr/actualites/missions-methodes-et-resultat" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-title">missions methodes et resultat</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">GEIPANMISSIONS, METHODES ET RESULTATS25 Feb 2021 — Sinon l&#x27;observation est déclarée non exploitable (Cas C) par manque de données fiables...</span></span></span>

This distinction is central to Nièvre’s UFO history. A category-C report can sound extraordinary while remaining evidentially weak. It may conceal an unusual event, but it may equally preserve a distorted view of an aircraft, meteor, astronomical object, balloon or projected light. The official record does not establish which interpretation is correct. It establishes that the available material cannot support a confident conclusion.

## The 1976, 1979 and 2011 case records

### Châteauneuf-Val-de-Bargis, July 1976

At about 3 am on 19 July 1976, a 50-year-old witness at Châteauneuf-Val-de-Bargis reported a single green phenomenon in a clear night sky. The GEIPAN database describes it as roughly ten times the apparent size of a star, initially stationary and then moving rapidly, without any reported sound. A gendarmerie statement survives, but the case is classified C for lack of reliable information.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://geipan.fr/en/node/46915" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: geipan.fr">[Geipan]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">CHATEAUNEUF-VAL-DE-BARGIS (58) 19.07.1976 &#124; GEIPAN...</span></span></span>

The description is memorable, but most of the details needed for reconstruction are absent or too uncertain. “Ten times a star” is not an angular measurement: stars have no visible disc to the unaided eye, so such comparisons usually express brightness, visual impact or an indistinct halo rather than physical size. Nor does the public summary provide a firmly measured elevation, an accurately plotted direction throughout the event or a duration detailed enough to test possible astronomical and aviation explanations.

The reported colour also carries less diagnostic value than it first appears. Green can be produced or perceived in several circumstances, including navigation lighting, meteors, fireworks, optical distortion and bright point sources viewed through haze or imperfect eyesight. The sudden rapid departure could represent genuine movement, but it could also reflect the observer losing sight of a light, a changing line of sight or a brief object reaching the end of its visible path. With one witness, no photograph and no independent instrument record, those possibilities cannot now be separated.

The most defensible conclusion is therefore deliberately narrow. A witness reported a striking green light, but the archive does not preserve enough geometry or corroboration to establish its source. The case remains open in an administrative sense, not strong in an evidential one.

### Decize, December 1979

The Decize file is more detailed and involved several witnesses. At around 5.40 pm on 5 December 1979, witnesses described a ball with orange reflections, approximately comparable in apparent size to an orange or football. It reportedly moved vertically, sometimes stopped, then departed towards the north-east at high speed. The event was said to last about fifteen minutes. A similar phenomenon was reportedly seen again the following evening at approximately the same place and time, although the descriptions of its apparent size and behaviour varied.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.geipan.fr/fr/temoignage/2786" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: geipan.fr">[Geipan]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">DECIZE (58) 05.12.1979 T2 &#124; GEIPAN...</span></span></span>

Multiple witnesses and a repeated observation make Decize more interesting than a fleeting single-person light report. They do not, however, automatically make it reliable enough for a category-D classification. The gendarmerie record contains ordinary-language estimates rather than a calibrated reconstruction. Descriptions such as “the size of a football” are especially problematic without knowing whether the comparison means held at arm’s length, seen at a presumed distance or simply experienced as visually prominent.

The record also leaves major uncertainties about weather, exact bearings and the relationship between the witnesses’ accounts. It is unclear whether each observer independently described the phenomenon before discussing it with the others. That matters because witnesses watching together can influence one another’s estimates of colour, motion and shape without intending to do so.

The apparent stops and changes in brightness are intriguing but not uniquely extraordinary. A distant light moving towards or away from an observer may appear nearly stationary. Thin cloud, haze or intervening trees can change its brightness. A low astronomical object may seem to move against foreground features, while an aircraft viewed nearly head-on can remain in roughly the same part of the sky before turning and apparently accelerating away. Conversely, the reported duration would be long for a normal meteor. No one conventional explanation fits the surviving summary cleanly—but the data are too incomplete to test the alternatives properly.

Decize is consequently unresolved because too many plausible branches remain open. It is not a case in which conventional explanations were comprehensively excluded.

### The D33 road sighting, November 2011

The 9 November 2011 report between Cosne-Cours-sur-Loire and Saint-Père is Nièvre’s most extensively investigated category-C case. Three people travelling by car reported unusual coloured light effects at about 9.05 or 9.10 pm. One witness first saw blue flashes or a blue-lit section of sky. Another described two superimposed, differently coloured discs that seemed to rotate. A later observation was described as a huge, stationary orange wheel that disappeared in a fraction of a second. The principal observations lasted only about five to ten seconds.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/sites/default/files/Compte%20rendu%20enquete95.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Compte rendu enquete95</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">GEIPANModéle de document par défaut CNES version 1.5 mars 1999...</span></span></span>

Unlike the older cases, this report prompted an on-site investigation. A GEIPAN field investigator met the witnesses, revisited the observation points, recorded interviews, took photographs and compared drawings. The inquiry identified nearby high-voltage lines, the A77 motorway, the Belleville nuclear power station and two nightclubs in broadly relevant directions. It also checked the Moon’s position and obtained weather information from the Avord station.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/sites/default/files/Compte%20rendu%20enquete95.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Compte rendu enquete95</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">GEIPANModéle de document par défaut CNES version 1.5 mars 1999...</span></span></span>

The investigators regarded a powerful nightclub projection system, or “sky tracker”, as the most plausible conventional lead. One nearby venue possessed such equipment, a local resident said projected lights were often visible in the reported direction, and one witness recalled fog or low cloud that could have made a beam unusually conspicuous. However, the club manager said the system was normally used on Friday and Saturday nights, later in the evening, whereas the sighting occurred on a Wednesday at about 9.10 pm. No operating log existed that could confirm or rule out a brief test or accidental activation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/sites/default/files/Compte%20rendu%20enquete95.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Compte rendu enquete95</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">GEIPANModéle de document par défaut CNES version 1.5 mars 1999...</span></span></span>

The testimony also contained important contradictions. The main witness said the sky was clear and insisted that a solid mass was visible between the orange lights. Another witness recalled grey, cloudy or foggy conditions in the direction of the phenomenon. The nearest cited weather station, some 45 kilometres away, reported heavy cloud, but that could not prove the exact conditions along the road. The witnesses also did not all see the same form at the same moment.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/sites/default/files/Compte%20rendu%20enquete95.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Compte rendu enquete95</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">GEIPANModéle de document par défaut CNES version 1.5 mars 1999...</span></span></span>

A radar check might have helped test an aircraft hypothesis, but GEIPAN recorded that relevant radar traces were not requested in time. Nor could the witnesses’ drawings establish real size or distance. A light that seems enormous may be small and nearby, or large and distant; without triangulation or a known background reference, apparent diameter cannot be converted into physical dimensions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/sites/default/files/Compte%20rendu%20enquete95.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Compte rendu enquete95</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">GEIPANModéle de document par défaut CNES version 1.5 mars 1999...</span></span></span>

The final classification reflects that stalemate. Projected light remained plausible but unconfirmed; the witnesses rejected it; meteorological recollections conflicted; radar data were unavailable; and the observation was extremely brief. GEIPAN therefore placed the case in category C rather than claiming either a proven explanation or a robust unexplained object.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/sites/default/files/Compte%20rendu%20enquete95.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Compte rendu enquete95</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">GEIPANModéle de document par défaut CNES version 1.5 mars 1999...</span></span></span>

## What category C really means

Category C is often misunderstood as a halfway form of “officially unexplained”. GEIPAN defines it more cautiously: the phenomenon cannot be identified because data or information are lacking. A category-D case, by contrast, remains unidentified after investigation and must have enough reliable substance to support its unusual character. GEIPAN’s method explicitly links “consistency” to the quantity and reliability of the evidence. When that consistency is too low, even an apparently strange account becomes unusable for a firm conclusion.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/fr/actualites/missions-methodes-et-resultat" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-title">missions methodes et resultat</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">GEIPANMISSIONS, METHODES ET RESULTATS25 Feb 2021 — Sinon l&#x27;observation est déclarée non exploitable (Cas C) par manque de données fiables...</span></span></span>

That produces three important distinctions in the Nièvre record:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--benefit" markdown="1">

* **Unresolved is not the same as inexplicable.** A case may be unresolved simply because investigators cannot test the available possibilities.
* **Strangeness is not the same as strength.** A huge silent wheel sounds stranger than a distant point of light, but its value depends on duration, viewing conditions, independent witnesses and measurable position.
* **Several witnesses are not automatically independent evidence.** People in the same car share the same viewing conditions and may discuss the event before giving formal accounts.

</div>

Category C also leaves room for revision. GEIPAN states that C and D cases can be reassessed when new information arrives. In practice, however, the prospects decline sharply with time. ## How missing details weaken extraordinary claims

The Nièvre files show why sighting investigation depends less on dramatic description than on basic observational discipline. GEIPAN’s technical questionnaire asks for precise start time, duration, witness location, direction, trajectory, weather, astronomical conditions, instruments used, environmental noise and sketches tied to maps or photographs. Those questions are designed to reconstruct what the observer could actually see and compare it with known phenomena.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/sites/default/files/Questionnaire%20technique-R281.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Questionnaire technique R281</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Questionnaire technique R281</span></span></span>

Several recurring gaps weaken the department’s unresolved reports.

**No reliable angular measurements.** Comparisons with stars, plates, oranges, footballs or fairground wheels do not establish true size. Even apparent size remains uncertain unless the comparison is made consistently at arm’s length.

**Uncertain distance.** Witnesses often describe a light as being above a field, behind trees or near a building, but a bright source with no visible surface detail may be much farther away. Without two separated viewing positions or a clearly observed passage behind a known object, distance is usually an assumption.

**Inexact timing and direction.** A difference of only a few minutes or several degrees can decide whether a planet, satellite, aircraft route or meteor event is relevant. The 2011 case had a broadly known time, but the older files lack the precision expected from a modern technical submission.

**No durable instrument record.** None of the central category-C cases has a useful original photograph, video, radar plot or other recording that fixes the phenomenon’s position and behaviour. The 2011 radar opportunity was specifically lost through delay.

**Conflicting recollections.** In the D33 case, clear weather and fog were both reported. Such disagreement does not prove dishonesty; brief, surprising events are remembered imperfectly. It does mean that explanations involving projected light and low cloud cannot be confidently accepted or rejected.

**Weak control over alternative sources.** The nightclub explanation in 2011 illustrates the problem. Investigators found appropriate equipment in the relevant area, but no log settled whether it operated at the crucial moment. The hypothesis remained plausible rather than demonstrated.

These are not minor technical objections applied after the event to dismiss witnesses. They determine whether a claim can be tested at all. Without them, a researcher cannot calculate speed, estimate altitude, reproduce a sightline or distinguish an object from a lighting effect.

## What would have changed the verdict?

The older Châteauneuf-Val-de-Bargis and Decize reports would have been much stronger with immediate mapped bearings, separate statements from every witness, precise durations and checks against aircraft, astronomical and meteorological records. A photograph containing identifiable foreground features might also have supplied scale and direction, even if the light itself appeared only as a small blur.

For the 2011 road case, timely preservation of air-traffic radar data would have tested whether an aircraft or helicopter was present. A verified operational record from the nightclub could have strengthened or eliminated the sky-tracker hypothesis. Simultaneous photographs from separated points might have shown whether the apparent form was a distant projection on cloud, a nearby light source or something with a measurable trajectory.

Independent reports from the same time and direction would also have mattered. The investigation noted another reported luminous event farther north that evening, followed several minutes later by a boom, but the account was indirect and described different movement and lighting. It therefore could not reliably corroborate the stationary wheel seen from the D33.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/sites/default/files/Compte%20rendu%20enquete95.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Compte rendu enquete95</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">GEIPANModéle de document par défaut CNES version 1.5 mars 1999...</span></span></span>

Absent such material, later retelling cannot repair the files. Repetition may make a story culturally familiar, but it does not restore measurements that were never taken.

## The honest status of Nièvre’s unresolved cases

Nièvre has no published category-D case in the official group reviewed here. Its most unusual records instead occupy the evidentially awkward category C: too incomplete for identification, but also too incomplete to support a strong extraordinary interpretation. The 1976 case is a sparse single-witness light report. Decize has multiple witnesses and a repeated observation but insufficient reconstruction. The 2011 D33 case received serious field investigation, yet ended with contradictory testimony, a plausible unverified lighting hypothesis and missing radar data.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://geipan.fr/en/node/46915" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: geipan.fr">[Geipan]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">CHATEAUNEUF-VAL-DE-BARGIS (58) 19.07.1976 &#124; GEIPAN...</span></span></span>

That does not mean the witnesses saw nothing, nor that every report has been debunked. It means the surviving record cannot show what they saw with acceptable confidence. In Nièvre’s UFO history, the principal unresolved mystery is therefore not evidence of a hidden craft. It is the permanent gap between a vivid human experience and the limited information preserved well enough to test it.
