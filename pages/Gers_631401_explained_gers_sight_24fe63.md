## Introduction

Some of Gers’s most convincing UFO reports were solved not by questioning the honesty of the witnesses, but by reconstructing exactly where they were, when they looked and what should have been visible in that part of the sky. At Saint-Lary in 1991, three witnesses — including a gendarme who watched through binoculars for nearly an hour — mistook the bright star Capella for an unusual coloured light. Near Gaujan in 2024, a helicopter crew and medical passengers saw points of light that appeared and vanished without corresponding aircraft or radar contacts. Orbital calculations instead placed numerous Starlink satellites in the precise region where sunlight could make them flare.



<img src="{{ "/assets/images/Gers_631401_explained_gers_sight_24fe63-overview.webp" | relative_url }}" alt="Overview image for Solved Cases" loading="eager" decoding="sync" fetchpriority="high">

These cases matter because they show what “explained” should mean in the Gers record. An identification is strongest when it accounts for the reported direction, timing, appearance and behaviour together. Credible testimony remains valuable, but it describes an experience; astronomical and satellite reconstruction can reveal what produced it.

## Saint-Lary and the star that looked alive

Shortly after midnight on 6 August 1991, a couple in Saint-Lary contacted the gendarmerie at Jegun about a strange light in the sky. The duty gendarme could also see it from about 00:35 and observed it carefully with binoculars until 01:29. The resulting file was unusually substantial for a simple light report: three witnesses, a prolonged observation and a relatively precise description. GEIPAN, the French space agency unit that investigates unidentified aerospace phenomena, states that the witnesses’ sincerity and credibility were never in doubt.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.geipan.fr/fr/cas/1991-08-01242" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: geipan.fr">[Geipan]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">SAINT-LARY (32) 06.08.1991SAINT-LARY (32) 06.08.1991; Date d&#x27;observation. 06/08/1991; Région. Midi-Pyrénées; Département. Gers...</span></span></span>

The object appeared as a luminous point whose colour changed. Watching it for so long, and confirming it through binoculars, made an ordinary mistake seem less likely. Yet those details ultimately supported the conventional explanation. The light remained in broadly the same area for more than an hour, behaved like a distant point source and displayed colours consistent with a bright star seen through disturbed air. When the old file was reassessed using improved astronomical software, Capella occupied the relevant part of the sky. GEIPAN changed the case from its earlier inconclusive status to category A, meaning identified.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.geipan.fr/fr/cas/1991-08-01242" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: geipan.fr">[Geipan]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">SAINT-LARY (32) 06.08.1991SAINT-LARY (32) 06.08.1991; Date d&#x27;observation. 06/08/1991; Région. ### Why Capella can appear extraordinary

Capella is one of the brightest stars visible from the Northern Hemisphere. When a bright star is comparatively low in the sky, its light passes through a longer and more turbulent path in the atmosphere. Small changes in air density continually bend the incoming light, altering its apparent brightness, colour and position. This effect, called scintillation, is stronger near the horizon and can create rapid red, green, blue or white flashes.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cloudatlas.wmo.int/scintillation.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cloudatlas.wmo.int">[International Cloud Atlas]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cloudatlas.wmo.int</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Other factors being equal, scintillation is more pronounced for longer paths traversed by the light through the atmosphere...</span></span></span>

Binoculars do not necessarily remove the illusion. They make the coloured fluctuations easier to see, while offering few distance clues because a star remains an unresolved point. A witness may therefore perceive a pulsing or rotating object rather than a fixed astronomical source. Slight involuntary eye movements and the absence of nearby reference points can also make a stationary light seem to wander.

The Saint-Lary solution did not depend merely on saying that the object “looked like a star”. Several parts of the account converged:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* **Sky position:** Capella was in the reported viewing area.
* **Duration:** a star could remain visible throughout the prolonged observation.
* **Appearance:** scintillation accounted for the changing colours and brightness.
* **Angular form:** the phenomenon remained essentially a luminous point rather than showing a reliably observed structure.
* **Behaviour over time:** it did not depart, approach or cross the sky as an aircraft would.

</div>

GEIPAN’s conclusion explicitly separated perception from interpretation. The witnesses really did see a striking light; the mistake lay in assigning unfamiliar behaviour to it. Night-time surprise, fatigue and sustained attention made the ordinary source appear exceptional.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.geipan.fr/fr/cas/1991-08-01242" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: geipan.fr">[Geipan]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">SAINT-LARY (32) 06.08.1991SAINT-LARY (32) 06.08.1991; Date d&#x27;observation. 06/08/1991; Région. Midi-Pyrénées; Département. Gers...</span></span></span>

The identification is also supported by a later Gers case. A report from Solomiac on 16 August 2022 was likewise classified as Capella, illustrating that the same bright star can generate apparently unusual observations decades apart when its position and atmospheric conditions are favourable.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cnes-geipan.fr/fr/recherche/cas/tab?field_is_revisited_value=All&amp;field_type_de_cas_target_id=All&amp;order=field_departement_textuel&amp;page=49&amp;sort=asc" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">GEIPANRecherche de casListe des cas revisités; BOULAUR (32) 24.03.1984, 24/03/1984, C · Gers, Manque d&#x27;informations fiables; SAINT-LARY...</span></span></span>

## Gaujan and the Starlink flashes beyond radar

The Gaujan case of 26 November 2024 initially appeared more difficult to dismiss. During an early-morning helicopter flight from Lourdes towards Toulouse, the pilot and flight assistant saw two or three luminous points appearing and disappearing in an apparently irregular fashion. The observation continued for roughly five minutes. A doctor and nurse travelling aboard the medical flight later saw the lights after the crew pointed them out.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cnes-geipan.fr/en/node/61241?field_date_value=2024-07-23&amp;field_is_new_value=1&amp;order=title&amp;page=%2C34&amp;sort=desc" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">GEIPAN[AERO] GAUJAN (32) 26.11.2024Observation depuis un hélicoptère d&#x27;apparition et disparition de plusieurs points lumineux dans le cie...</span></span></span>

The crew treated the sighting as a possible aviation matter. The assistant checked Flightradar and Automatic Dependent Surveillance–Broadcast data, while air-traffic control at Toulouse-Blagnac confirmed that no corresponding radar target was present. Photographs and video were also supplied. Those checks strengthened the report as evidence that something visible was present, but they did not establish that the lights were flying within controlled airspace. Satellites would not appear as nearby aircraft on the systems being consulted.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cnes-geipan.fr/en/node/61241?field_date_value=2024-07-23&amp;field_is_new_value=1&amp;order=title&amp;page=%2C34&amp;sort=desc" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">GEIPAN[AERO] GAUJAN (32) 26.11.2024Observation depuis un hélicoptère d&#x27;apparition et disparition de plusieurs points lumineux dans le cie...</span></span></span>

GEIPAN reconstructed the scene with Stellarium astronomy software and satellite orbital data. The lights were seen at about ten degrees above the horizon, vertically aligned with the Sun’s position beneath it. At the time, the Sun was approximately 38 degrees below the horizon. Numerous Starlink satellites were passing through the same restricted area of sky. That combination matched a recognised phenomenon known as Starlink cluster flares. GEIPAN classified the sighting as category A.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cnes-geipan.fr/en/node/61241?field_date_value=2024-07-23&amp;field_is_new_value=1&amp;order=title&amp;page=%2C34&amp;sort=desc" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">GEIPAN[AERO] GAUJAN (32) 26.11.2024Observation depuis un hélicoptère d&#x27;apparition et disparition de plusieurs points lumineux dans le cie...</span></span></span>

### Why the satellites seemed to appear from nowhere

A Starlink flare is not the same thing as the familiar “train” of newly launched satellites moving in a line. Satellites already distributed through their operational orbits can reflect sunlight towards an observer for only a few moments. As different spacecraft pass through the favourable reflection geometry, one point brightens, fades and is replaced by another nearby. To an observer, the lights may seem to jump, circle, reverse direction or repeatedly materialise in one small patch of otherwise dark sky.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.geipan.fr/fr/node/60652" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: geipan.fr">[Geipan]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on geipan.fr.</span></span></span>

The geometry is counter-intuitive because the observer may be in full darkness while satellites hundreds of kilometres above Earth remain illuminated by the Sun. Research into unusually bright Starlink flares has shown that reflections from the spacecraft structure can become bright enough to attract airline crews’ attention and be reported as unidentified phenomena. The effect is particularly associated with observations near the horizon and in the direction of the Sun below it.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.13091" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: arxiv.org">[arXiv]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">arxiv.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">arXiv Extreme Flaring of Starlink Satellites</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">arXiv Extreme Flaring of Starlink Satellites</span></span></span>

GEIPAN had begun receiving numerous reports of such lights from long- and medium-haul pilots from autumn 2022 onwards. Witnesses commonly described between one and four white points appearing briefly within a limited region of sky, sometimes recurring for an extended period as successive satellites crossed the reflection zone. That accumulated case pattern gave investigators a comparison set that would not have existed during earlier decades of UFO investigation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.geipan.fr/fr/node/60652" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: geipan.fr">[Geipan]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on geipan.fr.</span></span></span>

Four elements made the Gaujan identification particularly strong:

1. The lights occupied the narrow area predicted for satellite flaring relative to the hidden Sun.
2. The calculated solar depression was suitable for illuminating satellites while the lower sky appeared dark.
3. Orbital reconstruction placed many Starlink satellites in the relevant zone at the relevant time.
4. The brief, repeated appearances resembled other investigated pilot reports already attributed to cluster flares.

</div>

The absence of an aircraft radar return therefore ceased to be mysterious. It was not evidence that a craft possessed unusual concealment or performance; it was expected if the luminous sources were objects in orbit.

## Why trained and credible witnesses still misread the sky

Saint-Lary and Gaujan involved observers whose occupations or actions might normally inspire confidence. A gendarme watched methodically through binoculars. A helicopter crew checked flight-tracking data and contacted air-traffic control. The lesson is not that such witnesses are unreliable. It is that credibility and identification expertise are different things.

A trained observer can report time, direction, colour and apparent motion accurately while still choosing the wrong category of object. Pilots are highly skilled at recognising aircraft and assessing hazards within an aviation setting. They are not automatically familiar with every reflection produced by a rapidly growing satellite constellation. Likewise, a police officer’s careful attention does not prevent atmospheric scintillation from making a bright star appear active or mobile.

Several features of night viewing increase the risk of misinterpretation:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--decision" markdown="1">

* **Distance is difficult to judge.** A point of light may be a nearby lamp, a distant aircraft, a high-altitude satellite or a star.
* **The horizon supplies poor reference points.** Apparent motion may come from the observer, the aircraft or involuntary eye movement.
* **Colour changes suggest machinery.** Atmospheric distortion can resemble rotating beacons or pulsing illumination.
* **Intermittent visibility suggests manoeuvring.** Satellite reflections can switch on and off without the satellite changing course.
* **Technical checks can be applied too narrowly.** No aircraft on radar rules out an ordinary tracked aircraft, not an astronomical or orbital source.

</div>

Multiple witnesses improve confidence that a phenomenon was genuinely visible, but they do not make each observation independent. Once one person points out an unfamiliar light and suggests that it is behaving strangely, others naturally focus on the same features. In Gaujan, the doctor and nurse saw the lights after the flight crew drew their attention to them; this corroborated visibility but did not provide a separate identification. ## What turns a plausible explanation into a solution

Not every Gers report can be resolved simply by naming a familiar object. The decisive difference is whether the proposed source reproduces the observation under the actual conditions. Saint-Lary was solved because Capella matched the location, persistence, point-like form and colours. Gaujan was solved because solar geometry, satellite positions and the pattern of repeated flashes all aligned.

This distinction protects against two opposite errors. One is treating every unexplained light as evidence of an extraordinary craft. The other is attaching a convenient conventional label without demonstrating the match. A robust identification should normally answer four questions:

**Was the candidate present?** Astronomical software, flight records or satellite ephemerides should place it in the relevant direction at the stated time.

**Could it look that way?** The explanation must account for colour, brightness, duration, apparent movement and disappearance rather than merely sharing one superficial feature.

**Does the geometry work?** For satellite flares, the positions of observer, spacecraft and Sun are essential. For stars, altitude above the horizon affects the strength of scintillation.

**Does it fit comparable reports?** A repeated pattern across independent cases strengthens an explanation, especially when the same viewing conditions produce the same unusual appearance.

Where those elements are missing, the proper result may remain “insufficient information” rather than either “alien” or “debunked”. GEIPAN’s Gers listings contain both identified cases and category-C files that cannot be determined because dependable details are lacking. ## The lasting lesson from the solved Gers cases

The two cases capture a change in the sources of UFO reports. Saint-Lary represents an old and persistent problem: bright stars distorted by the atmosphere and misread in a dark, reference-poor sky. Gaujan represents a newer one: large satellite constellations producing unfamiliar reflections that can surprise even professional aircrew.

Both were solved by treating the witnesses seriously while testing the interpretation. The original testimony supplied the times, directions and visual details needed for reconstruction. Investigators then compared those details with an independently calculable sky. Later analysis weakened the extraordinary reading of each incident, but it did not show that the witnesses fabricated or imagined the lights.

That is the most useful distinction in the UFO history of Gers. A sighting can be sincere, vivid, shared and initially resistant to explanation without remaining mysterious once the sky is reconstructed.
