## Introduction

An unsolved UFO report in Gers does not automatically point to an extraordinary craft. In many cases, it means that the evidence was never strong enough to test ordinary explanations properly. The Mirande sighting of 26 November 2010 is the clearest local example: two witnesses described several yellow-orange lights moving rapidly around a stationary light, yet GEIPAN ultimately placed the case in category C — unidentified because reliable information was insufficient.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://geipan.fr/fr/cas/2010-11-02743?field_date_value=2007-03-01&amp;field_is_new_value=All&amp;field_is_revisited_value=All&amp;field_type_de_cas_target_id=All&amp;order=field_classification_des_cas&amp;page=148&amp;sort=asc" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: geipan.fr">[Geipan]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">MIRANDE (32) 26.11.2010 &#124; GEIPAN...</span></span></span>

That distinction matters. A category-C file records a limit of knowledge, not a finding that aircraft, balloons, lanterns, astronomical objects or human activity have been eliminated. Mirande remains open because the report reached investigators too late for effective local enquiries, not because an exhaustive investigation uncovered evidence of exotic technology. Read carefully, the case is less a mystery solved halfway than an investigation that could never properly begin.

## What witnesses reported over Mirande

Between about 11.30 pm and midnight on 26 November 2010, two witnesses in Mirande reported seeing five or six points of yellow-orange light. They described the lights as similar in apparent size, silent and moving in fast, irregular curves around another light that appeared fixed. The moving lights reportedly went out abruptly, while the central light faded more gradually.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://geipan.fr/fr/cas/2010-11-02743?field_date_value=2007-03-01&amp;field_is_new_value=All&amp;field_is_revisited_value=All&amp;field_type_de_cas_target_id=All&amp;order=field_classification_des_cas&amp;page=148&amp;sort=asc" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: geipan.fr">[Geipan]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">MIRANDE (32) 26.11.2010 &#124; GEIPAN...</span></span></span>

The account is striking because it describes an organised-looking arrangement rather than a lone point crossing the sky. Apparent movement around a fixed centre can suggest coordination, especially when the observer has no visible horizon, known distance or reference object against which to judge the scene. Yet the published record contains no instrumental measurement of the lights’ altitude, distance, speed or separation. “Fast” therefore describes how the movement looked to the witnesses, not a calculated velocity.

GEIPAN considered several ordinary possibilities. Sky lanterns were discussed, but one witness believed the reported movements were too fast and too tightly centred on the fixed light. A pyrotechnic or electrical display associated with a celebration was also considered. Investigators found no indication of a major festival in Mirande that night, but that did not establish that no private event, small display or other local activity had occurred. The official summary also rejected a simple natural explanation on the basis of the complexity of the reported movements.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://geipan.fr/fr/cas/2010-11-02743?field_date_value=2007-03-01&amp;field_is_new_value=All&amp;field_is_revisited_value=All&amp;field_type_de_cas_target_id=All&amp;order=field_classification_des_cas&amp;page=148&amp;sort=asc" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: geipan.fr">[Geipan]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">MIRANDE (32) 26.11.2010 &#124; GEIPAN...</span></span></span>

These points narrowed the obvious suggestions without resolving the event. The lantern hypothesis was challenged mainly by remembered motion, while the entertainment-display hypothesis lacked timely local confirmation. Neither was decisively disproved by photographs, wind reconstruction, launch records, multiple independent sight lines or physical traces.

## Why GEIPAN could not classify it as genuinely unexplained

GEIPAN’s categories are intended to separate cases that have been identified, probably identified, left unusable through weak evidence, and still unexplained after a sufficiently strong investigation. Category C applies when the available information is too limited or unreliable to support a conclusion. Category D is reserved for reports that retain significant unexplained features despite having enough dependable evidence for serious analysis.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.geipan.fr/fr/actualites/methodologie-classification-geipan" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: geipan.fr">[Geipan]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-title">La méthodologie de classification au GEIPAN &#124; GEIPAN</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">La méthodologie de classification au GEIPAN &#124; GEIPAN...</span></span></span>

Under GEIPAN’s current approach, investigators assess two linked qualities:

* **Residual strangeness:** how poorly the best known explanation fits after investigation.
* **Consistency:** how much dependable, objective information supports the reported details.

Consistency depends on matters such as the number and independence of witnesses, the precision of their answers, internal coherence, photographs or recordings, and whether the phenomenon can be located accurately enough to test competing hypotheses. GEIPAN’s methodology expressly states that a case becomes unusable when its apparent strangeness is not matched by sufficiently strong information.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.geipan.fr/fr/actualites/methodologie-classification-geipan" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: geipan.fr">[Geipan]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-title">La méthodologie de classification au GEIPAN &#124; GEIPAN</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">La méthodologie de classification au GEIPAN &#124; GEIPAN...</span></span></span>

Mirande failed chiefly on consistency. GEIPAN said the events were already too old by the time they could be examined properly. Investigators could no longer conduct a meaningful search for other witnesses or mount an effective local enquiry. The case was therefore classified C for lack of information.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://geipan.fr/fr/cas/2010-11-02743?field_date_value=2007-03-01&amp;field_is_new_value=All&amp;field_is_revisited_value=All&amp;field_type_de_cas_target_id=All&amp;order=field_classification_des_cas&amp;page=148&amp;sort=asc" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: geipan.fr">[Geipan]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">MIRANDE (32) 26.11.2010 &#124; GEIPAN...</span></span></span>

That conclusion is narrower than “no explanation exists”. It means that the remaining hypotheses could not be verified or rejected with confidence. GEIPAN’s formal procedure recognises exactly this problem: when an interpretation remains plausible but the evidence cannot test it, the case is insufficiently consistent. Stronger cases require confirmed location, corroboration and enough information to challenge all reasonable explanations.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.geipan.fr/sites/default/files/Procedure_classif_PAN.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: geipan.fr">[Geipan]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Procedure classif PAN</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Procedure classif PAN</span></span></span>

## What the investigation was missing

The witnesses supplied a narrative, but a narrative alone cannot establish the physical behaviour of distant lights. Several missing pieces prevented the Mirande account from becoming a robust case.

**Precise geometry was absent.** Investigators needed measured directions, elevations and changes in position from identifiable observation points. Without them, it is impossible to determine whether the lights were close together, widely separated or merely aligned from one viewpoint.

**Distance was unknown.** A nearby light moving slowly can appear faster than a distant light covering a larger path. With no known distance, descriptions of rapid acceleration or curved motion cannot be converted into meaningful performance figures.

**Independent corroboration was not secured in time.** Two witnesses are more useful than one only when their accounts can be compared and their viewing positions established. Additional observers elsewhere in or around Mirande might have provided intersecting lines of sight, revealed a wider local display or contradicted the apparent central arrangement.

**Contemporary local checks were no longer practical.** A prompt enquiry could have asked residents, event organisers, emergency services and nearby businesses about lanterns, fireworks, illuminated balloons, advertising devices or private celebrations. Once sufficient time had passed, memories faded and short-lived records may no longer have existed.

**No instrumental record fixed the appearance.** A photograph or video would not necessarily have solved the case, but it could have preserved angular movement, brightness changes and timing. Even then, useful analysis would have required metadata, a known camera position and reference points.

These are not unusually demanding standards invented to dismiss the report. They reflect the basic data problem recognised in modern official investigations elsewhere: analysis is repeatedly hindered when observations lack multiple measurements, sensor information, metadata and baseline data. NASA’s independent study on unidentified anomalous phenomena reached the same broad conclusion, stressing that poor calibration and incomplete supporting information make firm assessment difficult.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://science.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2023/09/uap-independent-study-team-final-report.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: science.nasa.gov">[NASA Science]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">science.nasa.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Science Independent Study Team Report</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">NASA ScienceIndependent Study Team ReportSeptember 13, 2023 — At present, analysis of UAP data is hampered by poor sensor calibration, th...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: September 13, 2023</span></span></span>

## Unexplained is not the same as poorly documented

The word “unexplained” is often used too loosely. It can describe at least three very different situations.

A report may be **temporarily unidentified** because nobody has yet compared it with flight paths, astronomical positions, satellite activity or weather data. It may be **poorly documented**, meaning that several explanations remain possible but the evidence cannot discriminate between them. Or it may be **genuinely resistant to explanation**, supported by dependable information while reasonable conventional hypotheses have been tested and rejected.

Mirande belongs to the second group. Its category-C status does not certify unusual flight performance, physical structure or intelligent control. It preserves the fact that the witnesses reported something they could not identify while also recording that investigators lacked the material needed to decide what it was.

GEIPAN’s own classification history shows why this distinction is necessary. An earlier procedural review acknowledged that the boundary between weak category-C cases and stronger unexplained cases had sometimes been unclear. The revised method therefore treated classification as a summary of the knowledge available when an investigation stopped, rather than as a permanent judgement about the phenomenon itself. It also emphasised that unresolved files can be revised if better information later emerges.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.geipan.fr/sites/default/files/Procedure_classif_PAN.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: geipan.fr">[Geipan]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Procedure classif PAN</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Procedure classif PAN</span></span></span>

The official Gers database reinforces this reading. Alongside Mirande, it lists category-C reports from places including Condom, Miramont-d’Astarac, Faget-Abbatial, Boulaur, Lahas, the Eauze–Cazaubon road and Marsolan. The wider departmental record therefore warns against treating the residual category-C group as a separate population of more exotic objects. It may instead contain reports for which the same kinds of ordinary comparisons were no longer possible.

## Could Mirande still be solved?

A category-C classification can change, but Mirande would require genuinely new evidence rather than another interpretation of the same brief summary. GEIPAN states that category-C and category-D cases may be revisited when relevant new information is supplied.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.geipan.fr/fr/node/58787" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: geipan.fr">[Geipan]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Une revisite, soit une nouvelle analyse, des cas C et D peut être réalisé si de nouvelles informations liées à ces cas sont communiquées...</span></span></span>

Useful additions could include a dated original video, photographs with recoverable metadata, an independent witness account written before publicity, records of a private event, or evidence placing a known aerial display in the correct direction and time. Even a reliable weather reconstruction could strengthen or weaken a lantern explanation if the observed paths and local winds were documented precisely.

By contrast, repeating that the lights looked too fast for lanterns would add little. Apparent speed is one of the hardest properties to assess when both distance and scale are unknown. Nor would the absence of a recorded major festival eliminate smaller human activities. The original file’s weakness is not that investigators ignored an obvious decisive clue; it is that too few clues survived in a form that could be checked.

Later reporting has therefore neither strengthened nor convincingly debunked the Mirande claim. The published classification remains C, and the official case page continues to frame it as a lack-of-information case.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://geipan.fr/fr/cas/2010-11-02743?field_date_value=2007-03-01&amp;field_is_new_value=All&amp;field_is_revisited_value=All&amp;field_type_de_cas_target_id=All&amp;order=field_classification_des_cas&amp;page=148&amp;sort=asc" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: geipan.fr">[Geipan]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">MIRANDE (32) 26.11.2010 &#124; GEIPAN...</span></span></span>

## What Mirande contributes to Gers UFO history

Mirande matters because it shows how an apparently elaborate sighting can remain unresolved without becoming strong evidence for an extraordinary object. The witnesses may have accurately conveyed what the display looked like to them. Their sincerity need not be questioned for the evidential limits to remain severe.

The case also marks the point at which investigation becomes historical reconstruction. Once immediate witness searches, local checks and measurements are lost, investigators are left comparing memories with broad classes of explanation. That can identify a strong match in some cases, but it cannot always produce a defensible conclusion.

For readers assessing unresolved Gers reports, the most useful question is therefore not simply, “Was it explained?” It is, “Was there enough reliable evidence to test the explanations?” In Mirande, the answer is no. The result is a legitimate unsolved report, but not a demonstrated anomaly. Its lasting value lies in clarifying that difference.
