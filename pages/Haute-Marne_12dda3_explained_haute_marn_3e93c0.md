## Introduction

Many [Haute-Marne]({{ 'haute-marnes-ufo-record-under-the/' | relative_url }}) UFO reports became less mysterious once investigators stopped treating every light as a distant object in the sky. The most persuasive ordinary explanations involved thunderstorms, vehicles or people on the ground, bright astronomical objects and an orbiting spacecraft seen under unfamiliar conditions. In other cases, including Wassy in 1977, an ordinary cause was plausible but the surviving information was too weak to prove it.



<img src="{{ "/assets/images/Haute-Marne_12dda3_explained_haute_marn_3e93c0-overview.webp" | relative_url }}" alt="Overview image for Explained Cases" loading="eager" decoding="sync" fetchpriority="high">

This distinction matters. An “unidentified” report does not necessarily describe an extraordinary craft; it may simply lack the timing, direction, duration, photographs or independent witnesses needed for a firm identification. Haute-Marne’s official record shows the full range: a poorly documented storm-time observation, probable ground activity near Peigney, and recent cases securely matched to the Moon, Jupiter and the International Space Station.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cnes-geipan.fr/fr/cas/1977-10-00445" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">GEIPANWASSY (52) 21.10.1977 &#124; GEIPAN...</span></span></span>

## From storm lights to mistaken aerial objects

### Wassy: plausible weather, insufficient proof

At about 6.30 pm on 21 October 1977, a witness at Wassy reported a yellow, flashing ball travelling from west to east. The observation lasted only a few seconds. No sound or trail was noticed, and a gendarmerie inquiry found no other witnesses. The official file also records particularly stormy weather and numerous lightning flashes across the region that day.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cnes-geipan.fr/fr/cas/1977-10-00445" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">GEIPANWASSY (52) 21.10.1977 &#124; GEIPAN...</span></span></span>

Those circumstances make an atmospheric explanation plausible. A brief luminous event seen during an active thunderstorm could have been lightning viewed at an unusual angle, illumination within or behind cloud, or another weather-related effect whose apparent shape was difficult to judge. Yet the evidence does not support choosing confidently between those possibilities. There is no photograph, measured bearing, precise angular path or corroborating account with which to reconstruct the event.

GEIPAN therefore lists Wassy as category C: not identified because reliable information is lacking. That is different from category D, in which a phenomenon remains unidentified despite an investigation supported by sufficiently substantial evidence. The Wassy file does not establish an exotic event, but neither does it demonstrate a particular meteorological mechanism. It is best understood as a weakly constrained report made under conditions already capable of producing unusual light effects.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cnes-geipan.fr/fr/cas/1977-10-00445" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">GEIPANWASSY (52) 21.10.1977 &#124; GEIPAN...</span></span></span>

### Peigney: the “objects” were probably on the hillside

The Peigney reports show more clearly how distance and darkness can lift ordinary ground activity into an apparently aerial mystery. At 5.45 am on 17 August 2016, a witness looking west from a terrace saw a large, stationary orange glow accompanied by three smaller lights. Through binoculars, the smaller lights seemed to rotate around the larger one. The witness estimated that the display was about two kilometres away, on or near a country hillside. It disappeared while the witness went to fetch a camera.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/en/node/57862" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">GEIPANPEIGNEY (52) 17.08.2016 &#124; GEIPAN...</span></span></span>

GEIPAN found little reason to place the lights in the air. A track ran through or near the relevant area, making a vehicle or agricultural machine a straightforward possibility. People moving around it with torches could have created the impression that smaller lights were circling a central glow. Investigators also considered a fire with people carrying lamps or torches. The file was classified B, meaning that the phenomenon was probably identified rather than conclusively demonstrated.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/en/node/57862" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">GEIPANPEIGNEY (52) 17.08.2016 &#124; GEIPAN...</span></span></span>

A similar observation by the same witness strengthened that interpretation. In the 2016 police statement, the witness recalled seeing moving lights around a central flame-like glow on 4 June 2012, at about 3 am, on almost the same hillside. The witness also noted that a church in the area could accommodate scout groups. GEIPAN considered a campfire or organised outdoor activity another plausible source for both the central light and the moving points around it.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/en/node/57862" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">GEIPANPEIGNEY (52) 17.08.2016 &#124; GEIPAN...</span></span></span>

The repetition did not necessarily indicate a recurring aerial phenomenon. Instead, it supplied useful geographical context: the same distant location could host vehicles, fires or people moving after dark. Once the presumed altitude was removed from the account, the apparent “formation” could be reconstructed as several independent lights at ground level.

### Vicq: photographs turned strange shapes into the Moon and Jupiter

A 2025 report from Vicq demonstrates how modern records can be resolved more firmly. At about 1 am on 6 March, a witness saw two white luminous forms low in the sky above nearby houses. The witness took two smartphone photographs, one with digital zoom, and later reported that the lights had disappeared when viewed again roughly fifteen minutes afterwards.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/fr/cas/2025-03-51630" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">GEIPANVICQ (52) 06.03.2025 &#124; GEIPAN...</span></span></span>

GEIPAN analysed the images with IPACO, image-examination software used in its investigations. The recorded positions agreed with the Moon and Jupiter as they were setting. Trees partly obscured the Moon, preventing its familiar disc from being recognised, while slight camera movement probably produced an irregular, flattened-V shape in one image. The shape changed between photographs, supporting an imaging effect rather than a stable object.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/fr/cas/2025-03-51630" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">GEIPANVICQ (52) 06.03.2025 &#124; GEIPAN...</span></span></span>

The apparent shift of Jupiter relative to the Moon was attributed to the witness changing position between photographs, producing parallax against nearby roofs and vegetation. Astronomical reconstruction placed both bodies in the reported direction at 1.01 and 1.02 am. Their disappearance fifteen minutes later was consistent with the Moon and Jupiter dropping behind trees and buildings. A possible third point was probably Capella, a bright star. GEIPAN classified the case A: identified as the Moon and Jupiter.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/fr/cas/2025-03-51630" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">GEIPANVICQ (52) 06.03.2025 &#124; GEIPAN...</span></span></span>

This case is instructive because photographs did not automatically make the report extraordinary. Instead, they preserved enough information to test the witness’s impression against the sky at a precise time and place. The images strengthened the investigation while weakening the interpretation of structured aerial objects.

### Vals-des-Tilles: the curved light was the ISS

At 6.40 am on 2 March 2025, a witness at Vals-des-Tilles watched a bright point move steadily from the west towards the south for about 20 to 30 seconds. The witness considered the International Space Station but rejected it because the light seemed dimmer than expected and its route appeared to curve.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/fr/cas/2025-03-51627" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">GEIPANVALS-DES-TILLES (52) 02.03.2025 &#124; GEIPAN...</span></span></span>

CNES orbital specialists confirmed that the ISS was passing at the reported time and position. Its steady speed and point-like white appearance matched the description. The comparatively low brightness was consistent with the approaching dawn, while the apparently bending path resulted from perspective as the station followed its orbital track across a curved Earth and the observer’s view of the sky. GEIPAN also noted that, had the reported object been something else, the witness should have been able to see the independently confirmed ISS as a second moving light. The case was classified A.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/fr/cas/2025-03-51627" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">GEIPANVALS-DES-TILLES (52) 02.03.2025 &#124; GEIPAN...</span></span></span>

The important point is not simply that satellites are often reported as UFOs. The witness already knew what the ISS could look like. The misidentification arose because the real pass did not match an overly rigid expectation that it should always be exceptionally bright and should cross the sky along an apparently straight line.

## How distance and darkness distort perception

The Peigney, Vicq and Vals-des-Tilles reports involve different physical sources, but they share one observational problem: a bright light against a dark or poorly detailed background provides few reliable clues about distance. Without distance, witnesses cannot establish physical size, altitude or speed. A vehicle two kilometres away, a planet hundreds of millions of kilometres away and a space station in low Earth orbit can all initially appear as unresolved points or patches of light.

At Peigney, estimating the lights as distant and airborne encouraged the interpretation of a large central object with smaller bodies rotating around it. The same visual arrangement becomes much less strange when placed on a hillside: a stationary vehicle or fire, with people carrying lights nearby. At Vicq, nearby trees and buildings partly masked familiar astronomical forms, while a change in the photographer’s position altered their apparent relationship. Small points of light may also seem to move when viewed without a stable visual reference. Laboratory research describes this as autokinesis: apparent movement perceived in a small light presented against darkness. Cameras introduce another layer of ambiguity. Digital zoom enlarges pixels rather than recovering detail that was never captured. Hand movement can stretch a bright point into a line, hook or V-shaped form, while autofocus and exposure processing may produce an enlarged luminous patch. In the Vicq investigation, the changing photographed shape and the precise astronomical match allowed investigators to separate camera artefacts from the underlying celestial sources.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/fr/cas/2025-03-51630" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">GEIPANVICQ (52) 06.03.2025 &#124; GEIPAN...</span></span></span>

## What GEIPAN classifications actually mean

GEIPAN’s four principal categories are often mistaken for a scale running from mundane to extraordinary. They are better read as conclusions about identification and evidence:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--example" markdown="1">

* **Category A** means the phenomenon has been identified after investigation. Vicq’s Moon-and-Jupiter observation and the ISS pass at Vals-des-Tilles fall into this group.
* **Category B** means a proposed identification is highly probable but not fully demonstrated. Peigney fits here because investigators could reconstruct a convincing ground-light explanation without locating the exact vehicle, fire or individuals involved.
* **Category C** means the phenomenon cannot be identified because too little dependable information survives. Wassy’s short, single-witness report is an example.
* **Category D** is reserved for a phenomenon that remains unidentified after investigation rather than merely because the file is deficient.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/en/node/412" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Phenomenon not identified due to lack of data or information.Read more...</span></span></span>

</div>

A category B case is therefore not an official declaration that the proposed cause has been proved beyond doubt. It records a convergence of circumstances strong enough to make that explanation more likely than alternatives. Conversely, category C should not be presented as evidence that investigators encountered something beyond conventional explanation. It often means that the case cannot bear much analytical weight at all.

The distinction changes how Haute-Marne’s historical record should be read. Wassy remains technically unidentified, yet it is evidentially weaker than the identified Vicq case, which included photographs and a precise astronomical reconstruction. Peigney remains only “probably” explained, but its repeated location, nearby track and possible human activity make ordinary ground lights more persuasive than an aerial formation. Classification reflects the strength and outcome of an investigation, not the dramatic quality of the original story.

Across the national archive, identified and probably identified reports form a clear majority. CNES figures published in 2025 listed 24.6 per cent as clearly identified and 39.7 per cent as probably identified, compared with 32.4 per cent lacking enough data and 3.3 per cent remaining unidentified after investigation. Haute-Marne’s mixture of categories follows that broader pattern: many reports arise from sincere observations, but the eventual explanation depends heavily on whether enough objective information was captured.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cnes.fr/en/projects/geipan" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes.fr">[CNES]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on cnes.fr.</span></span></span>

## Why later reporting weakened the mystery

Haute-Marne’s explained cases do not suggest that witnesses were dishonest or careless. Most reported something genuinely visible: a flash during stormy weather, orange lights on a hillside, bright bodies setting behind trees, or the ISS crossing the morning sky. The disagreement concerned what those lights represented.

Later investigation weakened the more extraordinary readings by restoring information missing from the initial impression. Topography placed the Peigney lights near tracks and possible human activity. Astronomical software placed the Moon and Jupiter exactly where the Vicq photographs showed them. Orbital data confirmed the ISS over Vals-des-Tilles. In each instance, location and timing proved more useful than the apparent shape or behaviour described in isolation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/en/node/57862" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">GEIPANPEIGNEY (52) 17.08.2016 &#124; GEIPAN...</span></span></span>

Wassy remains the cautionary counterexample. Storm conditions supply an obvious family of possible explanations, but the brief duration and absence of corroboration prevent a secure reconstruction. Calling the report “unexplained” is accurate only when paired with the reason: the file lacks reliable information, rather than containing strong evidence that resisted conventional analysis.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cnes-geipan.fr/fr/cas/1977-10-00445" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">GEIPANWASSY (52) 21.10.1977 &#124; GEIPAN...</span></span></span>

Taken together, these cases show why ordinary explanations often emerge slowly. Witnesses experience the event from one viewpoint and under imperfect conditions; investigators can later compare that account with weather, terrain, astronomy, orbital predictions and image behaviour. Haute-Marne’s most clearly resolved reports became ordinary not because their witnesses saw nothing, but because better context showed that familiar phenomena had briefly appeared unfamiliar.
