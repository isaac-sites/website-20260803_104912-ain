## Introduction

Ariège’s official UFO record is striking not because it contains a hard core of extraordinary incidents, but because it shows how often an initially puzzling sight can be reduced to a familiar cause. In the published GEIPAN cases reviewed for the department, investigators identify or provisionally explain reports through the Moon, Venus, Sirius, atmospheric re-entry, satellites, a sky lantern and camera reflections. Three reports are classified as lacking enough reliable information, rather than as unexplained craft.



<img src="{{ "/assets/images/Ariege_996ae1_ariege_official_ufo_13bf80-overview.webp" | relative_url }}" alt="Overview image for Official Files" loading="eager" decoding="sync" fetchpriority="high">

That distinction matters. GEIPAN, the unit of France’s national space agency CNES that investigates unidentified aerospace phenomena, does not treat every report without a firm solution as equally mysterious. Its files separate strong identifications, probable explanations and cases that cannot be assessed properly. Ariège’s record therefore says more about mistaken interpretation and missing evidence than about objects defying conventional explanation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/fr/recherche/cas/tab?field_agregation_index_value=&amp;field_classification_des_cas_target_id%5B11%5D=11&amp;field_classification_des_cas_target_id%5B12%5D=12&amp;field_classification_des_cas_target_id%5B13%5D=13&amp;field_classification_des_cas_target_id%5B14%5D=14&amp;field_date_d_observation_value%5Bmax%5D=&amp;field_date_d_observation_value%5Bmin%5D=&amp;field_date_value=&amp;field_departement_target_id=&amp;field_document_existe_ou_pas_value=1&amp;field_is_new_value=All&amp;field_is_revisited_value=All&amp;field_latitude_value%5Bmax%5D=&amp;field_latitude_value%5Bmin%5D=&amp;field_longitude_value%5Bmax%5D=&amp;field_longitude_value%5Bmin%5D=&amp;field_phenomene_target_id=&amp;field_type_de_cas_target_id=All&amp;order=field_departement_textuel&amp;page=11%2C14&amp;select-category-export=nothing&amp;sort=asc&amp;video=on" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[cnes-geipan.fr]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Recherche de cas &#124; GEIPANListe des nouveaux cas; BENAGUES (09) 26.10.1989, 26/10/1989, B; MONTEGUT-EN-COUSERANS (09) 12.04.2008, 12/04/...</span></span></span>

## What the classifications actually mean

GEIPAN’s letter grades describe the state of the evidence after investigation. They are not a scale running from “ordinary” to “alien”.

A **Category A** case has been identified with a high degree of confidence. A **Category B** case has a likely conventional explanation, although some uncertainty remains. **Category C** indicates that the available information is insufficient or unreliable. Category D is reserved for reports that remain unexplained after investigation and have enough evidential substance to support further analysis. GEIPAN says its assessments consider both the consistency of the evidence and the residual strangeness left after possible explanations have been tested.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/fr/actualites/evolution-classification-des-cas" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[cnes-geipan.fr]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-title">evolution classification des cas</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">evolution classification des cas</span></span></span>

This framework prevents two common errors. First, an unidentified report is not automatically an extraordinary one. Second, a probable identification is not necessarily a proven reconstruction of every detail remembered by a witness.

The distinction is visible across Ariège’s files. Some observations match an astronomical object so closely that GEIPAN assigns Category A. Others fit satellites, lanterns or atmospheric events but retain a small doubt because the direction, timing or weather information is incomplete. Category C reports generally tell investigators too little to choose confidently between several ordinary possibilities.

In practical terms, Ariège’s official record breaks down into three broad groups:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* **Firmly identified:** the Moon, Venus, camera reflections and a Starlink satellite train.
* **Probably identified:** Sirius, atmospheric re-entry, a sky lantern and ordinary satellites affected by visual illusion.
* **Unassessable:** reports from Cos, Saint-Girons and Taurignan-Vieux that lack sufficiently detailed testimony.

</div>

None of those classifications establishes a technically strong unexplained case in the department’s published GEIPAN set.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/fr/cas/2022-05-51341" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[cnes-geipan.fr]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on cnes-geipan.fr.</span></span></span>

## The Moon became two very different “objects”

The Moon provides the clearest demonstration of how landscape, cloud and expectation can change the apparent character of a familiar object.

At Foix on 4 July 1979, a witness returning home at about 2am reported a large red-orange mass shaped rather like an artillery shell. It appeared silent, remained visible for about two minutes and then disappeared into the landscape. The report was originally among cases placed in Category C, but GEIPAN later revisited it. Investigators compared the reported direction, duration, colour and disappearance with the Moon’s position near the horizon and reclassified the incident as Category A.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/fr/cas/1979-07-00637" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[cnes-geipan.fr]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on cnes-geipan.fr.</span></span></span>

The important point is not that the witness failed to see anything. GEIPAN’s interpretation is that the witness saw the Moon but did not recognise it. Near the horizon, the Moon can look unusually orange or red, appear larger than expected and vanish behind terrain. With no visible detail connecting the light to a familiar lunar disc, fear and fatigue can encourage a more threatening interpretation.

A second lunar case occurred at Sentenac-d’Oust on 30 July 2016. At around 4.15am, a witness saw a bright orange light roughly 20 to 25 degrees above the horizon through broken cloud. The light seemed to stretch into an inverted L before fading. The witness considered a forest fire and an object entering the atmosphere. GEIPAN reconstructed the sightline and concluded that the Moon was being seen through an irregular opening in the clouds; the illuminated gap, rather than the full lunar disc, created the changing shape. The case was classified A.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/fr/cas/2016-07-09501" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[cnes-geipan.fr]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on cnes-geipan.fr.</span></span></span>

Together, the two reports explain why “it did not look like the Moon” is not decisive evidence against a lunar identification. At Foix, the horizon altered its colour and apparent form. At Sentenac-d’Oust, clouds acted as a moving mask. The object remained ordinary while the viewing conditions made its appearance unfamiliar.

Two other Ariège files concern bright astronomical objects whose apparent behaviour was more dramatic than their real movement.

### Venus over Lavelanet

A witness near Lavelanet filmed a white light on several mornings in February and March 2011. It remained almost motionless for as long as 45 minutes, appeared to scintillate and faded as daylight increased. Repetition might seem to strengthen a mysterious claim, but in this case it gave investigators a testable pattern.

GEIPAN checked the viewing direction towards the south-south-east and reconstructed the sky at about 7am on the relevant dates. Venus occupied the reported part of the sky and was bright enough to be conspicuous to the naked eye. Its repeated appearance at approximately the same time and bearing, followed by its loss in daylight, supported an astronomical explanation rather than a manoeuvring object. The case was classified A.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/fr/cas/2011-03-02747" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[cnes-geipan.fr]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on cnes-geipan.fr.</span></span></span>

The reported flickering did not require Venus itself to change. Light passing through turbulent layers of air is refracted unevenly, especially when an object is relatively low in the sky. This can produce apparent flashes, colour changes and small positional shifts.

### Sirius over Montégut-en-Couserans

On 12 April 2008, two witnesses watched a stationary, strongly scintillating light for about ten minutes. It appeared round, brighter than an ordinary star and flashed several colours, with blue particularly noticeable. They had enough time to fetch binoculars before it disappeared behind a hill.

GEIPAN’s sky chart placed Sirius—the brightest star in the night sky—low in the south-west at the time. Bright stars close to the horizon often flash through several colours because their light travels through more atmosphere than it does when they are overhead. The disappearance behind local terrain also fitted the account. GEIPAN classified the report B rather than A because the witnesses had not recorded the direction precisely enough to eliminate every residual doubt.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/fr/recherche/cas/tab?field_agregation_index_value=&amp;field_classification_des_cas_target_id%5B11%5D=11&amp;field_classification_des_cas_target_id%5B12%5D=12&amp;field_classification_des_cas_target_id%5B13%5D=13&amp;field_classification_des_cas_target_id%5B14%5D=14&amp;field_date_d_observation_value%5Bmax%5D=&amp;field_date_d_observation_value%5Bmin%5D=&amp;field_date_value=&amp;field_departement_target_id=&amp;field_document_existe_ou_pas_value=1&amp;field_is_new_value=All&amp;field_is_revisited_value=All&amp;field_latitude_value%5Bmax%5D=&amp;field_latitude_value%5Bmin%5D=&amp;field_longitude_value%5Bmax%5D=&amp;field_longitude_value%5Bmin%5D=&amp;field_phenomene_target_id=&amp;field_type_de_cas_target_id=All&amp;order=field_date_d_observation&amp;page=97%2C0&amp;select-category-export=nothing&amp;sort=desc&amp;video=on" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[cnes-geipan.fr]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on cnes-geipan.fr.</span></span></span>

The contrast between Lavelanet and Montégut shows why similar explanations can receive different grades. Venus at Lavelanet was supported by repeated observations, recorded timing, filmed material and a firm viewing direction. Sirius was highly plausible at Montégut, but the less exact directional evidence justified a more cautious conclusion.

## Fireballs, lanterns and satellites

Not all of Ariège’s explained reports were stationary lights. Several involved apparent flight, rapid passage or groups of moving points.

At Bénagues on 26 October 1989, a witness saw a “ball of fire” cross the sky in only three or four seconds. GEIPAN judged atmospheric re-entry to be the likely explanation and classified the case B. The short duration and rapid, luminous passage are fundamentally different from the prolonged hovering often implied in popular UFO imagery. The file is cautious because a brief single-witness report rarely allows a precise object or piece of debris to be reconstructed.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/fr/recherche/cas/tab?field_agregation_index_value=&amp;field_classification_des_cas_target_id%5B11%5D=11&amp;field_classification_des_cas_target_id%5B12%5D=12&amp;field_classification_des_cas_target_id%5B13%5D=13&amp;field_classification_des_cas_target_id%5B14%5D=14&amp;field_date_d_observation_value%5Bmax%5D=&amp;field_date_d_observation_value%5Bmin%5D=&amp;field_date_value=&amp;field_departement_target_id=&amp;field_document_existe_ou_pas_value=1&amp;field_is_new_value=All&amp;field_is_revisited_value=All&amp;field_latitude_value%5Bmax%5D=&amp;field_latitude_value%5Bmin%5D=&amp;field_longitude_value%5Bmax%5D=&amp;field_longitude_value%5Bmin%5D=&amp;field_phenomene_target_id=&amp;field_type_de_cas_target_id=All&amp;order=field_departement_textuel&amp;page=143&amp;select-category-export=nothing&amp;sort=desc&amp;video=on" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[cnes-geipan.fr]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on cnes-geipan.fr.</span></span></span>

A more local explanation was proposed for a red-orange light seen at Crampagna shortly after midnight on 9 June 2013. It remained visible for four or five minutes, moved slowly and silently from north to south, then disappeared behind a hill. GEIPAN considered a Thai-style sky lantern released after a nearby celebration the probable cause. The light’s colour, weekend timing, slow drift and silence all fitted, although variable winds in hilly terrain prevented an exact trajectory match. The result was therefore Category B rather than A.

The Perles-et-Castelet case of 19 July 2014 is more revealing because it separates an object’s real movement from movement apparently added by human vision. A group was watching the night sky in the mountains when one participant noticed several white points that seemed to hesitate, curve, stop, accelerate and sometimes merge. Only one detailed testimony was eventually obtained.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/fr/cas/2014-07-08734?field_agregation_index_value=&amp;order=field_date&amp;page=117&amp;sort=asc" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[cnes-geipan.fr]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on cnes-geipan.fr.</span></span></span>

GEIPAN found that several basic features fitted satellites: point-like white lights, generally linear travel, intermittent visibility and favourable conditions shortly after nightfall, when satellites can remain sunlit while observers below are in darkness. The more erratic movements were attributed to tiny involuntary eye movements and the difficulty of holding a precise visual reference while staring at a small light in a dark sky. The assessment remained Category B because the other observers did not provide statements and no individual satellite identification was secured.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/fr/cas/2014-07-08734?field_agregation_index_value=&amp;order=field_date&amp;page=117&amp;sort=asc" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[cnes-geipan.fr]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on cnes-geipan.fr.</span></span></span>

By contrast, the Vernajoul observation on 11 May 2022 offered a much more distinctive technological signature. At about 5am, a witness saw roughly ten similar white points moving together from south to north and disappearing successively. GEIPAN matched the formation to a Starlink satellite train, probably associated with a Falcon 9 launch on 19 March 2022, and classified the case A.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/fr/cas/2022-05-51341" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[cnes-geipan.fr]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on cnes-geipan.fr.</span></span></span>

These files also show how the pool of likely explanations changes over time. A chain of evenly spaced lights would not have suggested Starlink before large satellite constellations began operating. Official investigation must therefore compare testimony not only with longstanding possibilities such as planets and meteors, but with the technology present on the date of observation.

## The photograph that contained no sighting

The Gourbit file from June 2011 challenges another widespread assumption: that a photograph is automatically stronger evidence than an eyewitness account.

The witness did not notice an unusual object while taking the pictures. Strange, saucer-like forms were discovered only afterwards in long-exposure photographs of a dark landscape containing bright light sources. The effect reappeared when the witness repeated the experiment under similar conditions the next day.

GEIPAN’s photographic analysis attributed the shapes to internal reflections within the camera optics. Bright sources were being reproduced as curved or structured artefacts against the darker image. The long exposure—between roughly 23 and 35 seconds—made the effect more prominent. The successful repetition under comparable conditions supported a Category A identification. The archive lists Gourbit as a case involving reflections rather than an object present in the photographed scene.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/en/recherche/cas/tab?customGetLattitude=46.124763699209396&amp;customGetLongitude=2.4169921874999996&amp;customGetZoom=6&amp;field_agregation_index_value=&amp;field_classification_des_cas_target_id=All&amp;field_date_d_observation_value%5Bmax%5D=&amp;field_date_d_observation_value%5Bmin%5D=&amp;field_date_value=&amp;field_departement_target_id=&amp;field_document_existe_ou_pas_value=All&amp;field_is_new_value=All&amp;field_is_revisited_value=All&amp;field_latitude_value%5Bmax%5D=50.52739681329302&amp;field_latitude_value%5Bmin%5D=41.72213058512578&amp;field_longitude_value%5Bmax%5D=9.8876953125&amp;field_longitude_value%5Bmin%5D=-5.053710937500001&amp;field_phenomene_target_id=&amp;field_type_de_cas_target_id=All&amp;order=field_departement_textuel&amp;page=146%2C2&amp;sort=desc" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[cnes-geipan.fr]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on cnes-geipan.fr.</span></span></span>

This is a stronger explanation than simply saying that the photograph “could be a lens flare”. It connects the effect to the lighting arrangement, the camera system and a repeat observation. It also reverses the usual evidential hierarchy. Because nobody saw the supposed objects directly, the discrepancy between the image and the scene became evidence for a photographic artefact rather than for something invisible to the witnesses.

## Why Category C does not mean extraordinary

Three Ariège reports illustrate the archive’s most easily misunderstood label.

At Cos on 2 April 1977, several people travelling in a vehicle reportedly noticed a luminous cloud and then an elongated light emerging from it. They stopped to watch as the silent light travelled in a straight line and disappeared into cloud. No corroborating report or sufficiently detailed follow-up information allowed GEIPAN to determine what produced it, so the case remained Category C.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/en/recherche/cas/tab?field_is_new_value=All&amp;field_is_revisited_value=All&amp;field_type_de_cas_target_id=All&amp;order=field_date_d_observation&amp;page=8&amp;sort=asc" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[cnes-geipan.fr]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on cnes-geipan.fr.</span></span></span>

At Saint-Girons on 15 January 2009, a witness described a white, lens-shaped phenomenon moving slowly across the daytime sky. A lenticular cloud was considered, but GEIPAN could not conduct a proper inquiry because the requested additional information was not supplied. The archive therefore records insufficient reliable information, not a failed conventional explanation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/fr/recherche/cas/tab?field_agregation_index_value=&amp;field_date_d_observation_value%5Bmax%5D=&amp;field_date_d_observation_value%5Bmin%5D=&amp;field_date_value=&amp;field_departement_target_id=&amp;field_document_existe_ou_pas_value=All&amp;field_is_new_value=All&amp;field_is_revisited_value=All&amp;field_latitude_value%5Bmax%5D=&amp;field_latitude_value%5Bmin%5D=&amp;field_longitude_value%5Bmax%5D=&amp;field_longitude_value%5Bmin%5D=&amp;field_phenomene_target_id=&amp;field_type_de_cas_target_id=All&amp;order=title&amp;page=123%2C0&amp;select-category-export=nothing&amp;sort=asc" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[cnes-geipan.fr]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on cnes-geipan.fr.</span></span></span>

The Taurignan-Vieux report of 28 March 2009 is thinner still. A witness saw a very bright, star-like light move slowly along what appeared to be a curved path and viewed it with binoculars. The surviving account lacks the precise bearing, angular elevation, duration and other details needed to test aircraft, satellites or astronomical explanations. It is also classified C.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/fr/recherche/cas/tab?field_agregation_index_value=&amp;field_classification_des_cas_target_id%5B13%5D=13&amp;field_classification_des_cas_target_id%5B14%5D=14&amp;field_classification_des_cas_target_id%5B15%5D=15&amp;field_classification_des_cas_target_id%5B16%5D=16&amp;field_date_d_observation_value%5Bmax%5D=&amp;field_date_d_observation_value%5Bmin%5D=&amp;field_date_value=&amp;field_departement_target_id=&amp;field_document_existe_ou_pas_value=All&amp;field_is_new_value=All&amp;field_is_revisited_value=All&amp;field_latitude_value%5Bmax%5D=&amp;field_latitude_value%5Bmin%5D=&amp;field_longitude_value%5Bmax%5D=&amp;field_longitude_value%5Bmin%5D=&amp;field_phenomene_target_id=&amp;field_type_de_cas_target_id=All&amp;order=field_departement_textuel&amp;page=4&amp;select-category-export=nothing&amp;sort=asc" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[cnes-geipan.fr]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on cnes-geipan.fr.</span></span></span>

These reports may remain unidentified in everyday language, but that is not the same as having survived a demanding investigation. A case can reach no conclusion because the observation was too brief, the witness did not complete the questionnaire, the viewing direction was vague or no independent evidence existed.

Missing data cannot establish unusual speed, physical size or intelligent manoeuvring. Without distance, even apparent speed is indeterminate. Without an accurate direction and time, astronomical and satellite checks become unreliable. Without other witnesses, radar records, photographs or a repeatable pattern, investigators cannot test whether the reported shape belonged to an object, a cloud, a reflection or a perceptual effect.

Category C is therefore best read as **unresolved administratively and evidentially**, not **mysterious after alternatives were eliminated**.

## What the official record really shows

The most consistent pattern in Ariège’s GEIPAN files is not a geographical concentration of exotic craft. It is the repeated loss of ordinary visual cues.

Mountain ridges make lights disappear abruptly and obscure their true elevation. Clear, dark skies make planets, stars and satellites unusually prominent. Broken cloud can reshape the Moon. Atmospheric turbulence can give stationary celestial objects apparent colour and motion. Cameras can create forms that were never present in the scene. A drifting lantern can appear purposeful when its distance and scale are unknown.

The files also demonstrate the value of information that may initially seem mundane: exact time, compass direction, angular height, weather, photographs of the horizon and testimony obtained before witnesses compare stories. Those details allowed GEIPAN to identify Venus at Lavelanet, reconstruct the Moon at Sentenac-d’Oust and match the Vernajoul formation to Starlink. Where equivalent information was absent, as at Saint-Girons and Taurignan-Vieux, the inquiry largely stopped.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/fr/cas/2011-03-02747" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[cnes-geipan.fr]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on cnes-geipan.fr.</span></span></span>

Later analysis has generally weakened rather than strengthened Ariège’s unidentified claims. The Foix report moved from an information-poor category to a lunar identification after re-examination. New satellite technology supplied a direct explanation for Vernajoul. Detailed photographic analysis resolved Gourbit. None of this proves that every unusual observation in Ariège must have a conventional cause. It shows something narrower and more defensible: within the department’s published official record, the cases with the most usable evidence tend to become less mysterious, while those left without an answer are chiefly the ones with too little information to test properly.

    if (trigger && menu) {

        event.stopPropagation();

      });
      Array.prototype.slice.call(menu.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-option]')).forEach(function (button) {

          event.stopPropagation();

        });
      });
      document.addEventListener('click', function () { closeMenu(section); });
    }

        applyMarket(section, button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-button'), true);
