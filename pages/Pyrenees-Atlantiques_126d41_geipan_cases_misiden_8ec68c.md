## Introduction

The published GEIPAN files for Pyrénées-Atlantiques show a department rich in unusual-looking observations but poor in firm evidence for extraordinary objects. Across Pau, Bayonne, Biarritz, Orthez and the mountain valleys, investigators repeatedly encounter the same causes: aircraft seen from misleading angles, bright stars distorted near the horizon, lanterns drifting with the wind, birds crossing dark skies and reports too old or incomplete to test properly.

A November 2025 export of GEIPAN’s public database contained 40 records assigned to the department. Fifteen were classified A, meaning an explanation was established with high confidence; thirteen were B, meaning probably explained; eleven were C, meaning there was not enough reliable information to decide; and only one retained the older D classification for an unexplained case. That distinction matters. A witness may have seen something genuinely striking, and may describe it honestly, without having observed an unknown craft. GEIPAN’s files are most useful when read as investigations into distance, perspective, memory and incomplete information rather than as a simple catalogue of “UFO sightings”.

## The main cases by place and date

The files cover more than seventy years, but their value varies enormously. Some contain several statements, aviation checks or reconstructed sight lines. Others are little more than recollections submitted decades after the event.

## Why ordinary lights become extraordinary objects

The recurring explanations are not random. Each exploits a familiar weakness in night observation: the sky usually supplies no dependable scale, distance or fixed frame of reference.

### Stars that shimmer, move and change colour

The Urepel and Biarritz airport files are especially instructive because the witnesses were not simply mistaking a normal-looking star for an aircraft. They saw lights whose appearance had been altered by atmospheric conditions.

At Urepel, Arcturus was low enough for its light to pass through a long and unstable path in the atmosphere. Turbulence can make a bright star scintillate, break into colours and appear to jump slightly. GEIPAN also considered an unusual refraction effect that could have lifted its apparent position above the local horizon. The close match in direction, plus a similar report by an independent group near Somport, supported the identification.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cnes-geipan.fr/fr/cas/1985-02-01052" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">GEIPANUREPEL (64) 02.02.1985 &#124; GEIPAN## UREPEL (64) 02.02.1985... Le GEIPAN classe ce cas en A: observation de Arcturus...</span></span></span>

The January 2001 Biarritz case is even more revealing. Air-traffic controllers at Biarritz and San Sebastián saw the same bright light and conducted appropriate checks for civil traffic, military activity and balloons. The absence of a confirmed radar target initially increased the mystery. A site reconstruction later showed that both viewing directions were effectively parallel, placing the source extremely far away. GEIPAN’s re-examination identified the bright star Capella, affected by unusually strong scintillation.

The lesson is not that controllers are unreliable. It is that aviation expertise is designed primarily to interpret aircraft, traffic patterns and instrument data. A star distorted by the atmosphere can fall outside those familiar patterns and therefore look more, not less, anomalous.

### Aircraft that appear stationary or silent

Aircraft are among the most persistent sources of reports because their apparent behaviour depends heavily on viewing geometry. A plane travelling nearly towards an observer can seem motionless for several minutes. Its landing lights may dominate the view while its body and navigation lights remain difficult to see. When it turns, the apparently fixed light can suddenly move sideways or disappear.

The Orthez report shows how this can create an imposing shape. Witnesses described a large, dark triangular mass at low altitude with several coloured lights. Yet enquiries found that a C-160 Transall military transport was conducting a planned low-level mission in the area, supported by radar information. The reported green, reddish and white lights were compatible with aircraft lighting, while the impression of hovering arose from its approach and relative movement. The case was classified A.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cnes-geipan.fr/fr/recherche/cas/tab?field_agregation_index_value=&amp;field_date_d_observation_value%5Bmax%5D=&amp;field_date_d_observation_value%5Bmin%5D=&amp;field_date_value=2021-06-26&amp;field_departement_target_id=&amp;field_document_existe_ou_pas_value=All&amp;field_is_new_value=1&amp;field_is_revisited_value=All&amp;field_latitude_value%5Bmax%5D=&amp;field_latitude_value%5Bmin%5D=&amp;field_longitude_value%5Bmax%5D=&amp;field_longitude_value%5Bmin%5D=&amp;field_phenomene_target_id=&amp;field_type_de_cas_target_id=All&amp;order=field_classification_des_cas&amp;page=%2C31&amp;sort=asc" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on cnes-geipan.fr.</span></span></span>

Silence is less decisive than witnesses often assume. Wind direction, distance, terrain and background noise can suppress or delay engine sound. In the mountain Urepel file, GEIPAN considered that a north-westerly wind could have carried aircraft noise away from the observers. Similar effects matter throughout the Béarn valleys, where ridges obstruct both sight and sound.

Aircraft identifications are strongest when backed by flight plans, radar plots or control records. A mere resemblance to an aircraft is weaker, especially in old files where traffic records no longer exist. That is why one aviation-like report may be classified A and another C.

### Lanterns that form silent fleets

Sky lanterns repeatedly generate reports of orange or reddish lights travelling slowly and without sound. The flame illuminates the paper envelope but usually conceals its structure, leaving only a glowing point or ball. Several released together may form a line, triangle or loose group, while uneven winds make them separate, change relative position or appear to climb.

The Pau case of 6 May 2012 contained three orange lights moving for several minutes. Two vanished while a third appeared to descend behind buildings. GEIPAN judged that the duration, colour, silence and slow motion fitted lanterns; the observation also occurred shortly after the result of the French presidential election, providing a plausible celebratory context. A falling burning component may have created the impression that one light was dropping towards the ground.

At Bayonne on 24 August 2014, twelve sparkling red-orange lights moved irregularly and gradually faded during a summer weekend in a tourist area. GEIPAN regarded a post-celebration lantern release as the most likely explanation, although incomplete direction data prevented a full comparison with the wind. It therefore classified the case B rather than A.

That difference is important. “Probable lanterns” means the hypothesis explains the main features better than its alternatives; it does not mean investigators located the person who released them. GEIPAN’s A/B distinction is intended to preserve that gap between demonstrated identification and a persuasive reconstruction.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/en/actualites/methodologie-classification-geipan" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-title">methodologie classification geipan</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">GEIPANLa méthodologie de classification au GEIPAN26 Feb 2021 — La classification du GEIPAN (A/B/C/D) s&#x27;appuie sur l&#x27;évaluation de deux no...</span></span></span>

The December 2022 Uzein airport case shows how lanterns can concern even trained observers in controlled airspace. Personnel in the Pau-Pyrénées control tower reported successive objects with lights and alerted aviation and gendarmerie services. GEIPAN later concluded that the direction of travel, estimated speed, oval form, light near the lower part and extinction pattern were consistent with two sky lanterns carried by the wind. The published record classified the incident A. The seriousness of the initial response reflected appropriate aviation caution, not proof that the objects were technically unusual.

### Birds that look like luminous formations

The Bayonne sighting of 16 March 2019 demonstrates a less familiar source of confusion. A witness saw seven blurred white “discs” cross the sky silently in a geometrical formation within four or five seconds. GEIPAN concluded that they were probably light-plumaged birds, most likely gulls.

Several details supported that conclusion: nearby wetlands and feeding areas, the spring breeding or migration season, group movement along a straight path and calculated dimensions compatible with birds flying perhaps 20–30 metres above the ground. Darker heads and wings may have blended into the night sky, leaving only the pale undersides visible as separate white patches. The observer therefore saw apparently wingless discs rather than complete birds. The case remained B because there was only one witness and no photograph, but its residual strangeness was low.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/fr/recherche/cas/tab?field_agregation_index_value=&amp;field_classification_des_cas_target_id%5B11%5D=11&amp;field_classification_des_cas_target_id%5B12%5D=12&amp;field_classification_des_cas_target_id%5B13%5D=13&amp;field_classification_des_cas_target_id%5B14%5D=14&amp;field_date_d_observation_value%5Bmax%5D=&amp;field_date_d_observation_value%5Bmin%5D=&amp;field_date_value=&amp;field_departement_target_id=&amp;field_document_existe_ou_pas_value=1&amp;field_is_new_value=All&amp;field_is_revisited_value=All&amp;field_latitude_value%5Bmax%5D=&amp;field_latitude_value%5Bmin%5D=&amp;field_longitude_value%5Bmax%5D=&amp;field_longitude_value%5Bmin%5D=&amp;field_phenomene_target_id=&amp;field_type_de_cas_target_id=All&amp;order=field_classification_des_cas&amp;page=101&amp;select-category-export=nothing&amp;sort=asc&amp;video=on" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on cnes-geipan.fr.</span></span></span>

This mechanism helps explain why witnesses sometimes insist that an object had no wings. At night, not every part of an ordinary object contributes equally to what the eye perceives. Illumination can reveal a bird’s underside while concealing its outline and wing beats.

## Strong witnesses do not guarantee the right interpretation

The best local demonstration comes from the Civil Security helicopter report of 14 December 2022. The principal witness was a helicopter captain conducting mountain night training between Bedous and Pau, accompanied by a mechanic-operator and using night-vision equipment. An air-traffic controller also contributed information. This gave GEIPAN a much better evidence base than the usual single-witness civilian report.

The crew observed several intense lights that appeared to occupy the surrounding airspace. The combination of experienced witnesses, an operational aircraft and night-vision binoculars made the report worthy of detailed investigation. Nevertheless, GEIPAN concluded that the lights were probably meteors, including activity associated with the Geminid meteor shower, and classified the case B. The high-altitude phenomena had created misleading impressions of proximity, relative movement and position.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/fr/recherche/cas?field_agregation_index_value=&amp;field_date_d_observation_value%5Bmax%5D=&amp;field_date_d_observation_value%5Bmin%5D=&amp;field_date_value=&amp;field_departement_target_id=&amp;field_document_existe_ou_pas_value=All&amp;field_is_new_value=All&amp;field_is_revisited_value=All&amp;field_latitude_value%5Bmax%5D=&amp;field_latitude_value%5Bmin%5D=&amp;field_longitude_value%5Bmax%5D=&amp;field_longitude_value%5Bmin%5D=&amp;field_phenomene_target_id=&amp;field_type_de_cas_target_id=All&amp;order=title&amp;page=%2C32&amp;sort=desc" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on cnes-geipan.fr.</span></span></span>

This is not a dismissal of the crew. A meteor can be reported accurately as a bright, fast-moving light while being misjudged in distance and altitude. Night-vision devices intensify faint light but do not automatically supply range. Nor does professional aviation experience provide a dependable scale for a luminous object with no radar return, visible structure or known background.

The episode parallels the 2015 fireball seen across southern France, including from Pyrénées-Atlantiques. More than 80 reports reached media and astronomy channels, and an automatic CNES camera in Toulouse recorded the event. GEIPAN explained that bright meteors occur high in the atmosphere yet often seem very close because of their intensity. Witness estimates of time also varied considerably, despite everyone observing the same physical event.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/fr/cas/2015-02-09100" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on cnes-geipan.fr.</span></span></span>

Together, these files weaken a common argument in UFO discussion: that a trained witness cannot mistake a natural or conventional phenomenon. Training increases the value of technical observations, but interpretation still depends on suitable reference points and data.

## Poorly documented is not the same as unexplained

GEIPAN’s category C is frequently misunderstood. It does not describe a phenomenon that resisted every conventional explanation. It means that the available material is too weak to establish either an ordinary explanation or a genuinely unexplained event.

The department’s C files commonly contain one or more of the following problems:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--comparison" markdown="1">

* a report made years or decades after the observation;
* no exact date or time;
* only one surviving statement, even where several people were allegedly present;
* no measured direction or elevation;
* no photographs, radar data or contemporary weather information;
* a description too brief to compare competing hypotheses;
* records submitted too late for aviation or astronomical checks.

</div>

The Biarritz 1951 account is a clear example. The witness described a dramatic disc-like mass with green fluorescence, but the statement was recorded in 1975 and the exact day in 1951 was unknown. GEIPAN noted some resemblance to a bright meteor, including the green colour and sense of proximity, but other reported features did not fit. With no date, corroboration or contemporary record, the case could not be reconstructed and was classified C.

The Bayonne 1978 file suffered similar problems. The report was submitted in 2016, the date was uncertain and only one account was available. The apparently angular motion might have reflected aircraft activity, overlapping satellite tracks or the autokinetic effect, in which an isolated light seems to move against a dark, featureless background. GEIPAN did not select among these possibilities because the necessary historical data were unavailable.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/fr/recherche/cas/tab?customGetLattitude=46.124763699209396&amp;customGetLongitude=2.4169921874999996&amp;customGetZoom=6&amp;field_agregation_index_value=&amp;field_classification_des_cas_target_id=All&amp;field_date_d_observation_value%5Bmax%5D=&amp;field_date_d_observation_value%5Bmin%5D=&amp;field_date_value=&amp;field_departement_target_id=&amp;field_document_existe_ou_pas_value=All&amp;field_is_new_value=All&amp;field_is_revisited_value=All&amp;field_latitude_value%5Bmax%5D=50.52739681329302&amp;field_latitude_value%5Bmin%5D=41.72213058512578&amp;field_longitude_value%5Bmax%5D=9.8876953125&amp;field_longitude_value%5Bmin%5D=-5.053710937500001&amp;field_phenomene_target_id=&amp;field_type_de_cas_target_id=All&amp;order=title&amp;page=12&amp;sort=asc" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on cnes-geipan.fr.</span></span></span>

The Arbus report of May 2022 illustrates that category C is not limited to distant history. Three car occupants saw four aligned warm-white lights, but the file still lacked enough dependable geometric information to determine their nature. Multiple witnesses add value only when their observations provide independent, precise and comparable information.

GEIPAN’s methodology evaluates two broad qualities: residual strangeness after plausible explanations have been considered, and “consistency”, meaning the quantity and reliability of the information. A highly strange account with low consistency is not promoted into an unexplained category merely because no solution can be demonstrated. It remains C because the evidence cannot carry a firm conclusion.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/en/actualites/methodologie-classification-geipan" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-title">methodologie classification geipan</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">GEIPANLa méthodologie de classification au GEIPAN26 Feb 2021 — La classification du GEIPAN (A/B/C/D) s&#x27;appuie sur l&#x27;évaluation de deux no...</span></span></span>

## What the departmental pattern actually tells us

Read together, the Pyrénées-Atlantiques files reveal several recurring features of the department’s UFO history.

First, geography shapes the errors. Along the coast, airport traffic, distant stars over the sea, gulls and tourist celebrations provide repeated sources of unusual lights. Around Pau and the Béarn plain, civil and military aviation, public events and lantern releases are prominent. In the valleys, mountain horizons, atmospheric refraction, wind and obstructed sound make distance especially difficult to judge.

Second, apparent complexity is not a reliable guide to exotic origin. A triangular pattern can be an aircraft’s lights. A fleet of orange objects can be lanterns. A formation of pale discs can be birds. A multicoloured light can be a star. Each explanation must be tested against time, direction, weather and behaviour, but unusual shape alone carries little weight at night.

Third, official involvement should not be mistaken for endorsement. Gendarmes, controllers and aviation services often appear in the files because France has established reporting channels and because possible airspace hazards must be checked. Their participation improves documentation; it does not establish that a mysterious vehicle was present.

Finally, the small residue of unexplained material is not uniform. The department’s lone older D case, at Tardets in April 1982, has a different status from the eleven C reports. A D classification indicates that investigators considered the phenomenon genuinely strange relative to the available evidence. A C classification means the file never became strong enough for such a judgement. GEIPAN’s current method deliberately separates mystery from missing information.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/en/actualites/methodologie-classification-geipan" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-title">methodologie classification geipan</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">GEIPANLa méthodologie de classification au GEIPAN26 Feb 2021 — La classification du GEIPAN (A/B/C/D) s&#x27;appuie sur l&#x27;évaluation de deux no...</span></span></span>

The overall picture is therefore more useful than sensational. Pyrénées-Atlantiques has produced credible witnesses, striking observations and several cases serious enough to trigger aviation or gendarmerie checks. Yet its published record is overwhelmingly a history of how ordinary phenomena become extraordinary when seen without scale, measured direction or timely supporting data. The most durable lesson from the department’s GEIPAN files is not that every sighting has been solved, but that “unidentified”, “poorly documented” and “genuinely unexplained” are three very different conclusions.
