## Introduction

Issoudun and Le Blanc contain the two best-known unresolved sightings in [Indre]({{ 'why-indres-ufo-record-is-more-revealing/' | relative_url }})’s official record, but “unresolved” needs careful interpretation. The Issoudun witnesses described six orange lights, including an apparently rectangular object with illuminated openings, moving silently during the early hours of 23 January 1977. Near Le Blanc in July 1980, a witness reported an oval orange glow apparently hovering about 50 metres from a house for roughly 40 minutes; the same file also records a second, less detailed account from the surrounding area.



<img src="{{ "/assets/images/Indre_4b1413_unresolved_issoudun_6c6a70-overview.webp" | relative_url }}" alt="Overview image for Unresolved Cases" loading="eager" decoding="sync" fetchpriority="high">

Neither case was identified, yet neither is classed as a strong unexplained anomaly. GEIPAN, the investigation unit of France’s national space agency CNES, placed both in category C: reports that cannot be assessed properly because reliable information is missing. Their importance within Indre’s UFO history therefore lies less in what they prove than in what they reveal about the limits of old witness testimony. Vivid descriptions can survive for decades even when the measurements needed to test them do not.

## What witnesses reported at Issoudun and Le Blanc

The two cases differ considerably. Issoudun was a multiple-witness observation involving moving lights and an apparent structured form. Le Blanc centred on a largely stationary light reported at close range by one principal witness. What connects them is not a shared object or pattern of behaviour, but the absence of enough dependable data to distinguish an unusual event from a mistaken interpretation.

### Six orange lights over Issoudun

At about 1.45 am on 23 January 1977, a witness in Issoudun noticed an orange light in the distance that seemed to be approaching. Other people were awakened and joined the observation. According to GEIPAN’s summary, the witnesses then saw several luminous balls packed closely together. One formation or object appeared different from the others: it was described as rectangular, narrower towards the rear and fitted with illuminated openings. The lights reportedly moved slowly and produced no audible sound.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cnes-geipan.fr/fr/cas/1977-01-00385" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">GEIPANISSOUDUN (36) 23.01.1977 &#124; GEIPAN...</span></span></span>

The published witness records identify two women, aged 60 and 40, although the narrative indicates that additional people were present. Both surviving witness entries describe six orange or fire-coloured phenomena, slow movement, an urban setting and complete silence. One record compares the apparent size with a football; the other includes both a football and a car as visual comparisons. Weather information is incomplete: one entry says conditions were unknown, while the other records cloud with some clear spells.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/en/node/47106" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">GEIPANISSOUDUN (36) 23.01.1977 T2 &#124; GEIPAN...</span></span></span>

GEIPAN considered a transport aircraft because of aviation activity around Châteauroux, but the hypothesis could not be confirmed from the available information. That is an important distinction. The official file does not say that an aircraft explanation was disproved. It says that investigators lacked enough material to establish whether an aircraft was responsible.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cnes-geipan.fr/fr/cas/1977-01-00385" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">GEIPANISSOUDUN (36) 23.01.1977 &#124; GEIPAN...</span></span></span>

The multiple witnesses make Issoudun more interesting than an unsupported second-hand story. They show that several people perceived something sufficiently unusual to discuss and remember. Multiple witnesses do not, however, automatically supply independent evidence. People observing together can share the same viewing position, visual limitations and assumptions; later witnesses may also be influenced by what the first person has already said. GEIPAN’s methodology therefore considers not only witness numbers but also the precision, independence and reliability of the information they provide.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/en/node/58787" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-title">GEIPANClassification &#124; GEIPAN</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">GEIPANClassification &#124; GEIPAN</span></span></span>

### A long-lasting orange glow near Le Blanc

The case listed as “Le Blanc 1980” combines two reports from communities around Le Blanc rather than describing a single object seen by several independent observers. GEIPAN’s current page gives the principal event as occurring at about 2.45 am on 25 July 1980. A witness said that an orange, oval luminous form was visible approximately 50 metres from the home. Its estimated dimensions were about 0.8 metres high and 0.4 metres wide, and it reportedly remained stationary for around 40 minutes. The light was gone after the witness went into the kitchen. No ground trace was found.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cnes-geipan.fr/fr/cas/1980-07-00781?field_date_d_observation_value%5Bmax%5D=&amp;field_date_d_observation_value%5Bmin%5D=&amp;field_departement_target_id=&amp;field_document_existe_ou_pas_value=All&amp;field_is_new_value=All&amp;field_is_revisited_value=All&amp;field_latitude_value%5Bmax%5D=&amp;field_longitude_value%5Bmax%5D=&amp;field_longitude_value%5Bmin%5D=&amp;field_phenomene_target_id=&amp;field_type_de_cas_target_id=All&amp;order=field_classification_des_cas&amp;page=13&amp;sort=desc" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">GEIPANLE BLANC (36) 1980LE BLANC (36) 1980; Date d&#x27;observation. --/06/1980; Région. The same official file mentions another person who reported a luminous ball roughly a month earlier. That light was said to have been about 50 metres above the ground and to have followed the witness’s vehicle. GEIPAN states that no further information was collected about either phenomenon. The archive’s date field remains only partly resolved, displaying June 1980 even though the principal narrative specifies 25 July. That discrepancy is itself a warning against treating every database field as an exact reconstruction of the original event.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cnes-geipan.fr/fr/cas/1980-07-00781?field_date_d_observation_value%5Bmax%5D=&amp;field_date_d_observation_value%5Bmin%5D=&amp;field_departement_target_id=&amp;field_document_existe_ou_pas_value=All&amp;field_is_new_value=All&amp;field_is_revisited_value=All&amp;field_latitude_value%5Bmax%5D=&amp;field_longitude_value%5Bmax%5D=&amp;field_longitude_value%5Bmin%5D=&amp;field_phenomene_target_id=&amp;field_type_de_cas_target_id=All&amp;order=field_classification_des_cas&amp;page=13&amp;sort=desc" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">GEIPANLE BLANC (36) 1980LE BLANC (36) 1980; Date d&#x27;observation. --/06/1980; Région. The reported 40-minute duration initially appears to strengthen the principal Le Blanc account: a prolonged observation should permit careful inspection. Yet duration is valuable only when accompanied by useful reference points. The surviving summary does not provide a measured bearing, elevation, movement against landmarks, photographs, instrument readings or a documented sequence of changes. Nor does it explain how the claimed 50-metre distance and sub-metre dimensions were calculated.

The absence of physical traces is not evidence that nothing unusual was present; a light in the sky need not affect the ground. It nevertheless removes one possible route to independent testing. Once no photograph, trace or contemporaneous measurement survives, investigators are left mainly with a narrative whose most striking details depend on uncertain visual estimates.

## Why size, distance and shape are unreliable at night

Descriptions such as “football-sized”, “car-sized” or “0.8 metres high” sound precise, but they often combine two separate judgements: how large something looked and how far away the witness believed it was. Without a known distance, the eye chiefly registers angular size—the portion of the visual field occupied by an object. A small nearby light and a much larger distant source can appear equally large. NASA uses the Sun and Moon as a familiar example: they have vastly different physical diameters but appear almost the same size because their distances differ so greatly.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://eclipse2017.nasa.gov/exploring-angular-diameter" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: eclipse2017.nasa.gov">[NASA Science]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">eclipse2017.nasa.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">exploring angular diameter</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">We have total solar eclipses visible on the surface of Earth because even though the diameter of the sun is 1,392,600 km and the diameter...</span></span></span>

This problem is central to both Indre cases. The Issoudun comparisons do not establish that the lights had the physical diameter of footballs. They indicate an impression produced against the sky. Similarly, the Le Blanc dimensions are meaningful only if the claimed 50-metre distance was established independently. If the light was actually hundreds of metres or several kilometres away, its physical size would have been entirely different.

A dark or sparsely detailed background also weakens the visual cues normally used to estimate range. Aviation guidance treats night observation as a recognised human-factors problem because isolated lights, uncertain horizons and limited terrain detail can generate incorrect impressions of distance, position and movement. Bright lights may be visible over unexpectedly long ranges at night, while differences in apparent brightness can be mistaken for changes in proximity.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.faa.gov/air_traffic/publications/atpubs/aip_html/chap8_section_1.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: faa.gov">[FAA]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">faa.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Fitness for Flight Autokinesis</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Although exposure to total darkness for at least 30 minutes is required for complete dark adaptation, a pilot can achieve a moderate degr...</span></span></span>

Shape is vulnerable for similar reasons. A witness may perceive several points of light as parts of one larger structure, especially when the dark body connecting them cannot be seen. Closely spaced lights can suggest a rectangle, triangle, row of windows or solid outline even though the observer has detected only luminous points. In Issoudun, the description of illuminated openings may accurately record the witnesses’ impression, but the surviving evidence cannot demonstrate that those openings belonged to a visible rectangular craft rather than to an arrangement of lights.

Apparent motion presents another difficulty. A stationary point of light viewed against a featureless background can appear to drift through the autokinetic effect. This does not explain every report of movement, particularly a complex observation involving multiple lights, but it demonstrates why motion should ideally be recorded against fixed landmarks. Aviation authorities teach pilots about autokinesis precisely because a small light in darkness can appear to move when no stable reference is available.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.faa.gov/air_traffic/publications/atpubs/aip_html/chap8_section_1.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: faa.gov">[faa.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">faa.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Fitness for Flight Autokinesis</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Although exposure to total darkness for at least 30 minutes is required for complete dark adaptation, a pilot can achieve a moderate degr...</span></span></span>

Silence is also less decisive than it seems. A distant aircraft may be difficult to hear because of range, wind direction, surrounding buildings or the delay between light and sound. Conversely, a genuinely nearby powered aircraft should generally provide stronger acoustic and positional clues. Since neither case preserves adequate distance measurements, “silent” cannot securely establish that the source was both close and mechanically extraordinary.

## Why ordinary explanations cannot now be tested

Several conventional possibilities can be suggested for the Issoudun observation: aircraft lights, more than one aircraft viewed along similar lines of sight, illuminated objects drifting in the atmosphere, or perceptual grouping of separate lights. The nearby aviation connection makes aircraft an obvious line of enquiry, and GEIPAN explicitly considered a transport aircraft. But the archive does not contain the operational information needed to confirm or reject that idea, such as a dependable viewing direction, altitude estimate, flight path, exact observation sequence or complete traffic record.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cnes-geipan.fr/fr/cas/1977-01-00385" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">GEIPANISSOUDUN (36) 23.01.1977 &#124; GEIPAN...</span></span></span>

That evidential gap cuts both ways. It prevents investigators from demonstrating a conventional explanation, but it also prevents advocates of an extraordinary interpretation from showing that aircraft movements were incompatible with the witnesses’ account. The rectangular impression and reported silence make the case memorable; they do not overcome the missing geometry.

Le Blanc permits an even wider range of possibilities because its principal light was reportedly stationary for a long period. A distant astronomical object, aircraft facing towards the observer, a terrestrial light seen through atmospheric haze, an illuminated balloon or another local source might all produce some elements of the description. Yet proposing possibilities is not the same as solving the case. Without a reliable direction, elevation, weather record and map of the witness’s exact sightline, none can be tested to an acceptable standard.

The claim that the second Le Blanc-area light followed a vehicle is particularly difficult to evaluate. A distant stationary source can seem to maintain its position relative to a moving observer, creating the impression that it is travelling alongside. The Moon is the familiar example, although many distant lights can produce a comparable effect. The surviving account is too brief to determine whether that perspective effect applies, whether the source truly changed direction with the vehicle, or whether the report described something else entirely.

The cases have therefore reached an evidential dead end rather than a scientific impasse. Investigators are not confronted with measurements that contradict known aircraft, astronomical or atmospheric behaviour. They are confronted with reports from which the crucial measurements were never obtained or no longer survive.

## What category C really means

GEIPAN classifies cases by considering both residual strangeness and consistency. Strangeness asks how poorly the best conventional hypothesis fits the observation. Consistency concerns the quantity and reliability of the available data, including the precision of testimony, witness relationships and the presence of photographs or other records. A case becomes category C when there is not enough dependable material to validate either an explanation or a genuinely unexplained result.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/en/node/58787" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-title">GEIPANClassification &#124; GEIPAN</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">GEIPANClassification &#124; GEIPAN</span></span></span>

That makes category C fundamentally different from category D. A D case remains unexplained after an investigation supported by sufficient evidence; category C is “not workable” because the evidence cannot sustain a firm conclusion. GEIPAN explicitly defines C as a phenomenon not identified because of missing data or information, while D denotes a phenomenon not identified after investigation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/en/node/58787" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-title">GEIPANClassification &#124; GEIPAN</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">GEIPANClassification &#124; GEIPAN</span></span></span>

Issoudun and Le Blanc are therefore unresolved in the weakest technical sense. The classification does not certify that unknown craft were present, nor does it establish that the witnesses invented or imagined their experiences. It says that the archive cannot reliably connect what was perceived with a specific physical cause.

The age of the reports makes improvement unlikely. GEIPAN can reconsider C cases when genuinely new information appears, and the Issoudun page was updated as recently as June 2021. However, reformatting or republishing an old file does not create missing flight records, photographs or measurements. A meaningful reassessment would require overlooked original documents, additional independent witnesses with detailed notes, or records capable of reconstructing the viewing geometry.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cnes-geipan.fr/fr/cas/1977-01-00385" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">GEIPANISSOUDUN (36) 23.01.1977 &#124; GEIPAN...</span></span></span>

## Why these are not strong anomalies

Both reports contain features that deserve attention. Issoudun has more than one witness, a group of six lights and an apparently structured form. Le Blanc includes a claimed close distance and an unusually long duration. Those details explain why the stories remain among Indre’s most memorable UFO accounts.

Their weaknesses, however, affect the core of each claim:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--comparison" markdown="1">

* **Distance was estimated rather than measured.** This makes physical size and proximity uncertain.
* **Apparent shapes depended on lights seen at night.** The record cannot establish whether the witnesses saw illuminated solid bodies or interpreted patterns of light as objects.
* **The viewing geometry is incomplete.** Bearings, elevations and movement against fixed landmarks are absent or inadequate.
* **Corroboration is limited.** Issoudun has multiple witnesses but no surviving photograph or instrument record; Le Blanc’s two accounts concern different dates and circumstances rather than one mutually confirmed event.
* **Conventional hypotheses were not eliminated.** In Issoudun, the aircraft possibility could not be checked properly. At Le Blanc, the lack of precise data prevents meaningful comparison with astronomical, aviation, atmospheric or ground-based sources.
* **Later reporting has not added decisive evidence.** The official classifications remain category C because the cases are insufficiently documented, not because successive investigations found ordinary explanations impossible.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cnes-geipan.fr/fr/cas/1977-01-00385" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">GEIPANISSOUDUN (36) 23.01.1977 &#124; GEIPAN...</span></span></span>

</div>

Within Indre’s wider record, this matters because category C cases can easily be misrepresented as the department’s strongest evidence for extraordinary craft. In reality, they sit below well-documented unexplained cases in evidential value. Their narratives are stranger than many of Indre’s identified aircraft, meteor, star, balloon and lantern reports, but narrative strangeness is not the same as a robust anomaly.

## The most defensible conclusion

Something drew the attention of witnesses at Issoudun in January 1977 and near Le Blanc in 1980. The official records give no compelling reason to dismiss those witnesses as dishonest. They also provide no secure basis for reconstructing the objects’ distance, dimensions, structure or origin.

Issoudun remains the more substantial report because several people apparently watched six moving orange lights and perceived one as a rectangular, windowed form. Even there, the missing positional and aviation data prevent the case from becoming more than an intriguing account. Le Blanc’s long-lasting oval glow sounds like a close encounter, but its claimed distance and size cannot be separated from the witness’s visual interpretation, while the associated vehicle-following report is too thinly recorded to strengthen it.

The appropriate verdict is therefore neither “explained” nor “evidence of an unknown craft”. These are unresolved sightings because the surviving files are too incomplete to decide what happened. Their lasting lesson for Indre’s UFO history is that a memorable observation can remain unidentified for reasons that have little to do with its physical strangeness. In both cases, uncertainty survives because the evidence needed to reduce it does not.
