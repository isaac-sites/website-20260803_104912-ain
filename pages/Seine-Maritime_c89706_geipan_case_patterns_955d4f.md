## Introduction

Official investigations did not find a single explanation for [Seine-Maritime]({{ 'why-seine-maritimes-strangest-sightings/' | relative_url }})’s UFO reports. Instead, GEIPAN—the specialist unit of the French space agency CNES—reconstructed each sighting from its direction, duration, weather, astronomical position, aircraft activity and the quality of the testimony. Bright planets, stars distorted by the atmosphere, illuminated lanterns, aircraft and event lighting accounted for several apparently dramatic reports. Other files remained undecided because witnesses reported too late, supplied contradictory estimates or lacked photographs, precise bearings and independent confirmation.



<img src="{{ "/assets/images/Seine-Maritime_c89706_geipan_case_patterns_955d4f-overview.webp" | relative_url }}" alt="Overview image for Official Files" loading="eager" decoding="sync" fetchpriority="high">

That distinction is central to understanding the department’s official record. A report classed as unexplained is not necessarily strong evidence of an extraordinary object, while a probable explanation does not mean investigators proved every detail. The published files show a deliberately cautious system in which the strength of the evidence matters as much as the strangeness of the story.

## What GEIPAN categories actually mean

GEIPAN receives witness questionnaires and supporting material, creates a case file, conducts an initial assessment and then checks possible astronomical, meteorological, aeronautical and human causes. It works with organisations including the gendarmerie, police, the French Air and Space Force, Météo-France and scientific specialists. Human testimony remains the starting point, but photographs, videos, sketches and instrument records can increase a case’s evidential value.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/en/node/58788" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[cnes-geipan.fr]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on cnes-geipan.fr.</span></span></span>

Its classifications are based on two broad considerations: **residual strangeness**, meaning how unusual the phenomenon still appears after ordinary explanations have been tested, and **consistency**, meaning the quantity and reliability of the available information. The categories are therefore conclusions about an investigation, not labels for different kinds of craft:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* **A:** the phenomenon has been identified.
* **B:** a conventional explanation is considered probable, although it cannot be demonstrated beyond doubt.
* **C:** there is insufficient reliable information to determine what happened.
* **D:** the phenomenon remains unidentified after investigation.

</div>

GEIPAN can reopen C or D files if useful new information appears.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/en/histoire-du-geipan" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">GEIPANIts historyClassification A: Phenomenon perfectly identified after investigation. · Classification B: Phenomenon probably identifie...</span></span></span>

This framework prevents a common misunderstanding in discussions of Seine-Maritime cases. Category C does not mean that investigators encountered something more mysterious than a category B case. It often means the opposite: the observation was too poorly documented to support either an ordinary identification or a robust unexplained finding.

The Dieppe report of 18 June 1976 illustrates the problem. Two people reportedly watched a long, luminous, cigar-shaped phenomenon moving steadily from north-east to south-west without audible sound. Yet only one formal testimony was collected, and the published record contains no photograph, measured bearing, reliable altitude, radar evidence or second independent account. GEIPAN therefore placed it in category C for lack of dependable information rather than declaring the reported object genuinely anomalous.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cnes-geipan.fr/fr/cas/1976-06-00306?order=field_departement_textuel&amp;page=%2C533&amp;sort=asc" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">GEIPANDIEPPE (76) 18.06.1976DIEPPE (76) 18.06.1976; Date d&#x27;observation. 18/06/1976; Région. ## When the testimony could not support the story

The weakest Seine-Maritime files repeatedly contain the same limitations: delayed reporting, very short viewing times, uncertain dates, estimates made without reference points and witnesses who did not submit separate accounts. These weaknesses matter because apparent speed, size and distance in a dark sky cannot usually be measured by eye alone.

A striking example comes from Dieppe in 1989. The witness did not report the experience until December 2009, roughly twenty years after the event, and could not give an exact observation date. The alleged triangular form was visible for less than five seconds, no other testimony was obtained and no contemporary investigation was possible. GEIPAN classified the case C, explicitly noting that the age of the report and shortage of information made meaningful inquiry extremely difficult.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/fr/cas/1989-11-02475?field_agregation_index_value=&amp;field_classification_des_cas_target_id%5B13%5D=13&amp;field_date_d_observation_value%5Bmax%5D=&amp;field_date_d_observation_value%5Bmin%5D=&amp;field_date_value=&amp;field_departement_target_id=&amp;field_document_existe_ou_pas_value=All&amp;field_is_new_value=All&amp;field_is_revisited_value=All&amp;field_latitude_value%5Bmax%5D=&amp;field_latitude_value%5Bmin%5D=&amp;field_longitude_value%5Bmax%5D=&amp;field_longitude_value%5Bmin%5D=&amp;field_phenomene_target_id=&amp;field_type_de_cas_target_id=All&amp;order=field_date&amp;page=15%2C27&amp;select-category-export=nothing&amp;sort=asc" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">GEIPANDIEPPE (76) --.11.1989Témoignage récent (2 décembre 2009) d&#x27;une observation ancienne (courant novembre -décembre 1989). At Montivilliers on 25 April 1992, a witness described three lights forming a triangle and apparently rotating for about twenty minutes. Such a description sounds substantial, but the published file contains no further information capable of fixing the lights’ position, separation, altitude or movement. The result was again category C—not because investigators established the presence of a triangular vehicle, but because they had too little material to test the impression.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/fr/cas/1992-04-01261" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on cnes-geipan.fr.</span></span></span>

The Trouville file of 24 August 2011 demonstrates an even more complicated outcome. Investigators found that some recurring lights could be explained by aircraft, satellites and the bright star Arcturus undergoing strong atmospheric scintillation. Those parts of the report might have justified an A classification. However, the witnesses also described a dark mass and beams of white or purple light for which the file contained too few concrete details. GEIPAN therefore retained category C for the combined case. The classification did not imply that every reported feature had resisted explanation; it reflected an inseparable mixture of identified observations and poorly documented claims.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/fr/cas/2011-08-02807" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on cnes-geipan.fr.</span></span></span>

These cases show why witness sincerity and evidential strength must be treated separately. A person may accurately describe how an experience appeared while being unable to supply the measurements needed to identify its cause. Conversely, a detailed account written many years later may feel vivid but offer investigators little verifiable information.

## Planets and stars that appeared to move

Astronomical objects were among the most straightforward recurring explanations. The difficulty is not simply that witnesses failed to recognise a planet. Atmospheric conditions, low viewing angles and intervening buildings or clouds can make a fixed light appear larger, coloured, flickering or intermittently mobile.

On 1 April 2017, three people in Rouen noticed a bright, low light that seemed to flash in several colours. The reporting witness compared it with a star but thought it looked unusually large and brilliant. GEIPAN checked the viewing direction and found Jupiter low in the south-east, above the hospital building specified in the account. At that altitude, atmospheric turbulence and diffusion could produce pronounced colour changes, while mist or cloud could explain the light’s disappearance. The case was classed A as Jupiter.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cnes-geipan.fr/fr/cas/2017-04-50363" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on cnes-geipan.fr.</span></span></span>

The investigation also exposed an important weakness in apparently multiple-witness reports. Although two other people were present, they did not provide their own questionnaires after GEIPAN requested them. The file therefore rested on one formal testimony rather than three independently comparable accounts. Investigators suggested that the other observers may not have experienced the same degree of strangeness.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cnes-geipan.fr/fr/cas/2017-04-50363" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on cnes-geipan.fr.</span></span></span>

A 1980 Rouen case had a similar astronomical pattern. A witness and gendarmes observed two points brighter than ordinary stars. The lights seemed to rise slowly as the evening progressed and reappeared on subsequent clear nights. That repetition was highly significant: a genuinely transient object would not normally occupy a predictable position night after night, whereas celestial bodies follow regular apparent paths as the Earth rotates. GEIPAN judged the report a probable observation of astronomical objects and classified it B.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cnes-geipan.fr/fr/cas/1980-01-01700?field_is_revisited_value=All&amp;field_type_de_cas_target_id=All&amp;order=field_date&amp;page=%2C77&amp;sort=asc" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on cnes-geipan.fr.</span></span></span>

These files show that apparent motion alone is not decisive. A stationary observer, moving cloud, changing reference point or prolonged stare can create a strong impression that a distant point has shifted. Investigators therefore compare the reported direction and time with calculated sky positions rather than relying solely on the witness’s sense of movement.

## Orange lights that followed the wind

Illuminated sky lanterns became another repeated explanation in Seine-Maritime, particularly for orange or reddish objects seen moving silently at night. Their appearance can be deceptive because distance removes the outline of the paper envelope, leaving what looks like a self-contained ball of fire. Wind can make the light pause, drift sideways or appear to climb, while the flame’s extinction produces a sudden disappearance.

At Auberville-la-Renault on 23 May 2010, several people watched an orange luminous object for about five minutes. It appeared to move horizontally, become stationary, rise and then disappear towards the horizon. Despite the apparently controlled sequence, GEIPAN considered a sky lantern the probable cause. The file included witness photographs and a questionnaire, but no independent observation established that the light was a structured craft rather than a wind-borne source.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cnes-geipan.fr/fr/cas/2010-05-02615" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on cnes-geipan.fr.</span></span></span>

A few months later, at Saint-Jacques-sur-Darnétal, an automobilist saw an orange-red ball moving slowly, changing colour and vanishing abruptly. GEIPAN compared its trajectory with the wind direction and found the characteristics consistent with an illuminated lantern: a yellow-white or orange light, an apparently oval form, slow drift and sudden extinction. The case was classified B.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cnes-geipan.fr/fr/cas/2010-09-02683" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on cnes-geipan.fr.</span></span></span>

The Rouen sighting of 21 July 2015 received the same judgement after a more explicit comparison of features. The object was rounded and fire-coloured, travelled in the direction of the prevailing wind, produced no sound and was visible for only ten to fifteen seconds. Each feature was individually ordinary, but together they formed a coherent lantern explanation. GEIPAN still used category B rather than A because no recovered lantern or identified launch established the cause with certainty.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cnes-geipan.fr/fr/cas/2015-07-09257" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on cnes-geipan.fr.</span></span></span>

These cases demonstrate how official investigation differs from matching a report to a single resemblance. An orange colour by itself proves little. The explanation becomes persuasive when colour, wind direction, speed, duration, silence and mode of disappearance all fit the same known phenomenon.

## Aircraft and lights hidden by viewing conditions

Some reports appeared extraordinary because an ordinary object was seen from an awkward position or in circumstances that disrupted normal cues. A fast glimpse through a windscreen, poor sound perception or cloud reflecting a distant projector can transform a familiar source into a seemingly impossible one.

On 6 September 2015, a motorist travelling between Écrainville and Goderville reported a dark triangular form that seemed to obscure the Sun before moving away rapidly. Investigators discovered that two French Air Force Rafale aircraft had flown over the nearby Étretat area during a commemoration at approximately the same time. GEIPAN could not retrieve sufficiently precise radar tracks to prove that one passed directly over the witnesses, but the aircraft’s form, location and timing made that the leading explanation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/fr/cas/2015-09-09346" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on cnes-geipan.fr.</span></span></span>

The viewing circumstances reduced the reliability of the more unusual details. The principal witness was driving, approaching a roundabout and looking through the windscreen during a brief encounter. The passenger did not see the object during its closest apparent passage. Investigators also noted that aircraft can sometimes be visually detected before their sound arrives or without the observer consciously registering it, particularly during a surprising and short event. The case was therefore classed B as a probable aircraft misidentification, not A, because the exact flight path remained unavailable.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/fr/cas/2015-09-09346" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on cnes-geipan.fr.</span></span></span>

A more recent file from Colleville, dated 12 October 2024, concerned repeated blue flashes close to the horizon. Three family members saw the phenomenon, and a video provided some objective support. The regular, alternating, approximately once-per-second flashes were consistent with a scanning outdoor projector—often called a sky-tracer—illuminating low cloud. The observation occurred on a cloudy Saturday night, conditions under which entertainment lighting was plausible, but investigators could not locate the exact projector. GEIPAN consequently chose category B: a strong conventional interpretation without complete source identification.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cnes-geipan.fr/fr/cas/2024-10-51576" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on cnes-geipan.fr.</span></span></span>

The Colleville case is particularly useful because it shows that video does not automatically settle a report. Recording can establish timing, colour and repetition, but it may not reveal the source when the camera captures only light reflected from cloud. Evidence strengthens an investigation only in relation to what it actually measures.

## Why probable explanations remain cautious

Across the Seine-Maritime files, category B often represents the most intellectually honest conclusion. Investigators may find that one hypothesis explains nearly all the observed features while still lacking the final piece of proof—a recovered lantern, an exact aircraft track or the identified operator of a projector.

This restraint cuts both ways. GEIPAN does not have to reproduce every detail of a witness’s interpretation before accepting an ordinary cause, because perceived size, distance and motion may themselves be mistaken. But it also avoids upgrading a plausible hypothesis to certainty when the source cannot be directly confirmed. The distinction between A and B preserves that uncertainty.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/en/node/58788" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on cnes-geipan.fr.</span></span></span>

Category C requires equal care. The Dieppe, Montivilliers and Trouville files were not officially endorsed mysteries. They remained undecided because the available record could not reliably discriminate between competing explanations. In practical terms, a well-documented B case may tell readers far more about what happened than a dramatic C case.

The small number of Seine-Maritime reports that remain unexplained should likewise be read in relation to their consistency. The 1994 Neufchâtel-en-Bray account, for example, describes dark triangular or pyramidal forms, including one said to have followed a car before accelerating away. GEIPAN left the case in category D, but the public summary still rests chiefly on one witness’s estimates of distance, altitude, shape and behaviour. “Unexplained after investigation” records the failure to identify the reported phenomenon; it does not establish the physical nature or origin of the object described.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/fr/cas/1994-06-01361" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on cnes-geipan.fr.</span></span></span>

## The clearest lesson from the official files

GEIPAN’s published Seine-Maritime investigations weaken the idea that the department’s reports form a single coherent phenomenon. Similar-sounding stories arose from very different sources: Jupiter low over Rouen, stars and satellites near Trouville, wind-borne lanterns, probable military aircraft and artificial beams reflected by cloud.

The files also explain why a residue remains. Investigators cannot reconstruct an exact sky from a vague direction, an uncertain time and a memory reported years later. Missing measurements do not prove that nothing unusual occurred, but they prevent a strong conclusion. The most responsible reading is therefore neither that every case was debunked nor that every unresolved file represents an unknown craft. Seine-Maritime’s official record is chiefly a study in how ordinary phenomena, difficult viewing conditions and incomplete testimony can produce compelling experiences—and how careful classification separates a plausible explanation from certainty.
