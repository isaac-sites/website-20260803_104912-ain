## Introduction

Official UFO verdicts can change because “unexplained” is not the same as “proved extraordinary”. In Toulouse, the French space agency’s investigation unit, GEIPAN, revisits older files when improved methods, fresh evidence or a closer reading of the original record alters the balance between a report’s strangeness and its reliability. Several [Haute-Garonne]({{ 'why-haute-garonnes-strangest-sightings/' | relative_url }}) cases once treated as unexplained have consequently been downgraded—not because investigators found a definitive conventional answer, but because the available testimony could no longer support a firm conclusion.



<img src="{{ "/assets/images/Haute-Garonne_34cc0a_geipan_case_reassess_0e9117-overview.webp" | relative_url }}" alt="Overview image for Case Reviews" loading="eager" decoding="sync" fetchpriority="high">

The distinction matters. A case may remain puzzling in everyday language while becoming unusable as evidence. The Castanet-Tolosan triangle lost an apparent radar confirmation; a separate Toulouse triangle weakened when its sole witness declined further contact; and the Saint-Gaudens lights were reassessed as possibly occurring on distant terrain rather than in the sky. Together, these files show how poor geometry, missing corroboration and the passage of time can turn an apparent mystery into a classification of insufficient information.

## How Toulouse became France’s investigation centre

GEIPAN is based within the Centre national d’études spatiales, or CNES, in Toulouse. Created in 1977 under an earlier name, the unit collects reports of unidentified aerospace phenomena, investigates possible explanations, preserves case material and publishes anonymised files. Its remit is evidential rather than speculative: it examines what witnesses observed and whether known astronomical, meteorological, aeronautical, technological or perceptual causes can account for it.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cnes.fr/en/projects/geipan" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes.fr">[CNES]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">GEIPAN7 Jul 2025 — GEIPAN, the French UAP research and information group created by CNES in 1977, collects, analyses and archives inf...</span></span></span>

Witness accounts may be checked against flight information, weather records, astronomical reconstructions, photographs, video, radar material and observations from other people. Classification depends on two linked judgements. “Strangeness” measures how poorly the observation fits known phenomena after plausible explanations have been tested. “Consistency” measures the amount and reliability of the available information. A highly unusual description therefore needs especially strong support: independent witnesses, accurate directions and timings, photographs, radar returns or other objective material. An unusual story backed only by a short, imprecise account may belong in category C rather than D.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/fr/glossaire" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">GEIPANLe glossaire du GeipanCLASSIFICATION DES CAS. Investigators may discover that supposed corroboration was spurious, that witness drawings do not establish the geometry once attributed to them, or that crucial questions were never asked while memories were fresh. ## The Toulouse-area triangle that lost its radar support

The Castanet-Tolosan case of 1 September 2000 illustrates how one apparently objective detail can transform a sighting—and how the case can collapse when that detail is re-examined.

At about 5.30 am, a man briefly saw illuminated points forming what he interpreted as a silent triangular shape. It moved out of view behind a building, and no second witness was found. The initial investigation nevertheless appeared to contain an unusually important piece of corroboration: Toulouse airport radar had registered three unknown tracks in the south-east Toulouse area at about 5.40 am. For a time, the proximity in time and place made the radar returns look like evidence that a physical object had been present.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/fr/node/52819" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">GEIPANCASTANET-TOLOSAN (31) 01.09.2000 &#124; GEIPAN...</span></span></span>

Specialists from the French civil aviation authority subsequently determined that the tracks were not meaningful targets. They were parasitic responses associated with an aircraft beyond the radar’s effective range. The supposed instrument confirmation therefore had no demonstrated connection with the witness’s triangle. What remained was a single fleeting visual report with no photograph and poorly defined duration, size, spacing or appearance.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/fr/node/52819" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">GEIPANCASTANET-TOLOSAN (31) 01.09.2000 &#124; GEIPAN...</span></span></span>

When GEIPAN revisited the file 18 years later, it did not claim to have identified what the witness saw. Instead, it judged that too many basic questions were unanswerable. It was unclear whether the orange description referred to individual lights or to a perceived solid form joining them. “Barely enough time to see it” might have meant a fraction of a second or several seconds, a difference that substantially changes the range of possible explanations. The file also lacked the angular separation of the lights and useful detail about halos or movement.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/fr/node/52819" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">GEIPANCASTANET-TOLOSAN (31) 01.09.2000 &#124; GEIPAN...</span></span></span>

GEIPAN noted that witnesses can perceive separate lights as the corners of a single dark object, even when no connecting structure has been established. Depending on the missing timing and visual details, possibilities might have included meteoric activity, illuminated birds or another ordinary grouping of lights. None could be properly tested after so many years. The case was therefore changed from D to C: not explained, but no longer evidentially strong enough to count as an investigated unknown. Its published consistency score is only 0.10, compared with a strangeness score of 0.30.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/fr/node/52819" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">GEIPANCASTANET-TOLOSAN (31) 01.09.2000 &#124; GEIPAN...</span></span></span>

The lesson is sharper than a simple debunking. Radar evidence is valuable only when the return itself is valid and can be related to the observation through compatible time, position and motion. A coincidental radar artefact may make a weak visual report look independently confirmed, even though the two events have no physical connection.

## A second Toulouse triangle weakened when the inquiry stopped

Another triangular report, observed in Toulouse on 24 August 2011, reached category D1 before later being downgraded to C.

At about 3.32 am, a witness looking at the stars through a window reported a dark triangular form moving silently towards southern Toulouse. Orange rectangular lights were said to form rows around its underside. The observation lasted about five seconds before buildings obscured the view. There was no photograph, radar confirmation or independent witness.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/fr/cas/2011-08-02803" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">GEIPANTOULOUSE (31) 24.08.2011 &#124; GEIPAN...</span></span></span>

The description was genuinely difficult to match with a familiar object. Investigators considered whether an aircraft might account for the shape and trajectory, but the reported lighting did not resemble a known configuration. A very low aircraft might also have been expected to produce detectable noise. GEIPAN therefore did not replace the original unexplained verdict with a conventional identification.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/fr/cas/2011-08-02803" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">GEIPANTOULOUSE (31) 24.08.2011 &#124; GEIPAN...</span></span></span>

The reassessment instead turned on the strength of the file. Most of its informational value came from the witness’s drawing and short account. The witness had not originally made a gendarmerie statement and later declined an investigator’s request for further contact. GEIPAN acknowledged that a person may have legitimate reasons not to continue, but the practical result was that essential details could not be tested or clarified.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/fr/cas/2011-08-02803" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">GEIPANTOULOUSE (31) 24.08.2011 &#124; GEIPAN...</span></span></span>

That distinction is important for judging witness credibility fairly. Refusing another interview does not prove that an account is false. It does, however, limit the investigator’s ability to reconstruct the viewing angle, check the window and skyline, establish apparent size, refine the timing or test whether the “solid triangle” was inferred from separate lights. The original case had only just met the level of consistency needed to support its high strangeness. Once further inquiry became impossible, GEIPAN concluded that it no longer did so and reclassified it as C.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/fr/cas/2011-08-02803" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">GEIPANTOULOUSE (31) 24.08.2011 &#124; GEIPAN...</span></span></span>

The published scores make the reasoning visible: strangeness was assessed at 0.65, but consistency at 0.42. The file is therefore not presented as a likely aircraft or hoax. It is an unusual report whose evidential base is too limited to sustain an official unexplained verdict.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/fr/cas/2011-08-02803" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">GEIPANTOULOUSE (31) 24.08.2011 &#124; GEIPAN...</span></span></span>

## The Saint-Gaudens lights may not have been in the sky

The 14 August 1993 Saint-Gaudens case initially appeared stronger because it involved several witnesses, prompt notification of the authorities and formal gendarmerie statements.

At around 10 pm, people in Saint-Gaudens and nearby Bordes-de-Rivière reported a stationary red phenomenon towards the Pyrenees. Two witnesses in Saint-Gaudens placed it near Mont Valier, while another described a reddish light towards Pic de Cagire. Reported viewing times ranged from about ten to fifteen minutes. One witness used binoculars and described five regularly spaced red-orange points within a larger reddish mass. The file was originally classified D.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cnes-geipan.fr/en/node/51854" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">GEIPAN(DEP) HAUTE-GARONNE (31) 14.08.1993 &#124; GEIPAN...</span></span></span>

On paper, this resembles useful multi-witness corroboration. The later review, however, exposed a fundamental geometrical problem: the statements did not securely establish that the phenomenon was airborne.

One witness first saw a glow “in the woods” and later said that it disappeared into the mountain chain. The other viewing directions also crossed layered ridges that could appear at nearly the same angular height as Mont Valier. In darkness, witnesses may have been unable to distinguish the true skyline from nearer foreground relief. A light on a ridge or mountainside could consequently appear suspended against the sky.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cnes-geipan.fr/en/node/51854" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">GEIPAN(DEP) HAUTE-GARONNE (31) 14.08.1993 &#124; GEIPAN...</span></span></span>

Investigators also reconsidered drawings that seemed to show the object moving upwards or away. A flat sketch cannot represent depth directly. A witness trying to depict a light receding into the distance may draw progressively smaller shapes along an upward-slanting line, even when no increase in elevation was observed. Dimming can likewise be interpreted as rapid movement away when it might instead result from a stationary light fading or being obscured.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cnes-geipan.fr/en/node/51854" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">GEIPAN(DEP) HAUTE-GARONNE (31) 14.08.1993 &#124; GEIPAN...</span></span></span>

GEIPAN explored, without adopting, the possibility of a ground event such as a torchlit procession or local celebration along a ridge. The date was a Saturday evening before the Feast of the Assumption, and the reported red points were compatible with a group of lights. But the witnesses’ stated directions did not intersect cleanly: Pic de Cagire and Mont Valier are distinct reference points, and the surviving record could not establish whether one had been named imprecisely. The hypothesis therefore remained suggestive rather than demonstrated.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/sites/default/files/Compte%20rendu%20enquete52.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-title">GEIPANMicrosoft Word</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">CR TA JPA V2 MPD.doc...</span></span></span>

The decisive finding was narrower. Nothing securely proved that the observation belonged in GEIPAN’s aerospace remit at all. A fuller investigation in 1993 might have checked local festivities, revisited each viewing position and resolved the conflicting bearings. Twenty-five years later, those opportunities had largely disappeared. GEIPAN therefore changed the case from D to C, describing it as unusable within the unit’s competence rather than solved.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cnes-geipan.fr/en/node/51854" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">GEIPAN(DEP) HAUTE-GARONNE (31) 14.08.1993 &#124; GEIPAN...</span></span></span>

This reassessment demonstrates that three accounts are not automatically three independent measurements. Witnesses can describe similar colours and timings yet be looking along lines that do not converge on one location. Without reliable bearings, elevation angles and terrain reconstruction, apparent corroboration may remain only a resemblance between stories.

## What separates unexplained, weak and explained reports

The Toulouse reviews reveal three outcomes that are often blurred together in public discussion.

**An explained case has positive evidence for an ordinary cause.** In Saint-Gaudens on 18 September 2014, for example, a witness photographed an orange light moving silently across the sky. GEIPAN judged the images, wind direction, duration and behaviour to be characteristic of a sky lantern and classified the report A, even though the witness rejected that interpretation. Here the verdict rests on a specific explanation that fits the recorded evidence.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cnes-geipan.fr/fr/cas/2014-09-50082" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on cnes-geipan.fr.</span></span></span>

**A weak case lacks enough reliable information to choose between explanations.** The Castanet-Tolosan and 2011 Toulouse triangles fall into this category. The reported experiences may have been sincere and unusual, but the observations were brief, single-witness events without imagery or dependable independent confirmation. Missing measurements prevent ordinary hypotheses from being properly tested. Category C does not mean that investigators secretly know the answer; it means the archive cannot support one.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/fr/node/52819" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">GEIPANCASTANET-TOLOSAN (31) 01.09.2000 &#124; GEIPAN...</span></span></span>

**A genuinely unresolved case retains adequate evidence after reasonable alternatives have been examined.** Under GEIPAN’s methodology, the more extraordinary the remaining description, the stronger and more objective the supporting material must be. A D classification is consequently not awarded merely because investigators have failed to name an object. It represents a narrower claim: useful evidence remains, plausible checks have been made, and no satisfactory identification has emerged.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/fr/glossaire" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">GEIPANLe glossaire du GeipanCLASSIFICATION DES CAS. La classification d&#x27;un cas nécessite l&#x27;évaluation, d&#x27;une part, de son degré de consis...</span></span></span>

As of 25 June 2026, GEIPAN’s published dynamic statistics listed 3,368 classified cases: 28 per cent A, 38.8 per cent B, 30.1 per cent C and 3.1 per cent D. The large proportion of category C cases is a warning against treating every unidentified report as part of the unexplained residue. Most of the archive’s unidentified entries are unidentified because the information is insufficient, not because a robust anomaly has survived investigation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/en/stats" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on cnes-geipan.fr.</span></span></span>

## Why old mysteries usually become harder to defend

Later reviews benefit from better astronomical software, digital mapping, radar expertise and a larger library of known misidentifications. Yet the same passage of time that improves the investigator’s toolkit often damages the case itself.

Flight records may no longer be available. Witnesses may have moved, died or become unwilling to revisit the event. The original location may have changed. Memories can preserve the central impression while losing exact duration, direction and sequence—the very details needed to distinguish an aircraft, meteor, bird formation, distant ground light or optical effect. GEIPAN’s own workshops have stressed that much historical material is scientifically fragile because it consists of single reports, delayed testimony and few or poor-quality images.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/fr/atelier-caipan-2014" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-title">atelier caipan 2014</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">GEIPANATELIER CAIPAN AU CNES, 8-9 JUILLET 2014...PAN de catégorie B: observation pour laquelle l&#x27;hypothèse retenue par le GEIPAN est c...</span></span></span>

Investigators sometimes use a cognitive interview, a structured method intended to help witnesses retrieve detail by mentally reconstructing the original context and approaching the memory through more than one route. This can produce fuller accounts, but it cannot manufacture missing radar data, create a second witness or turn a decades-old estimate into a measured angle. It is most useful when conducted carefully and reasonably soon after an event.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/sites/default/files/06_PY_sli.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-title">GEIPANCOPEIPAN_Entretien Cognitif Inte</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">GEIPANCOPEIPAN_Entretien Cognitif Inte</span></span></span>

For Haute-Garonne, the central historical point is therefore institutional rather than sensational. Toulouse is not simply the setting for local UFO reports; it is where France’s official investigators demonstrate that verdicts must remain revisable. The strongest reassessments do not force every mystery into an ordinary explanation. They separate three different statements that should never be confused: “we know what this was”, “we still cannot explain this despite adequate evidence”, and “the surviving evidence is too weak to decide”.
