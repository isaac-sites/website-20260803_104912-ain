## Introduction

GEIPAN’s [Doubs]({{ 'why-doubs-ufo-reports-became-less/' | relative_url }}) files show that an official “UFO case” is not automatically an unexplained one. Most published reports from the department were classified as identified or probably identified after investigators compared the witness account with astronomical positions, satellite passes, aircraft activity, weather, photographs and the reported direction of travel. Other cases were placed in category C because the evidence was too incomplete to support either an identification or a meaningful mystery.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cnes-geipan.fr/fr/recherche/cas/tab?order=field_departement_textuel&amp;page=130&amp;sort=desc" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">GEIPANRecherche de cas &#124; GEIPANListe des cas; PONTARLIER (25) 22.06.2021, 22/06/2021, B · Doubs, Ballon d enfant; VIEILLEY (25) 19.05.2...</span></span></span>



<img src="{{ "/assets/images/Doubs_70fb5f_geipan_doubs_files_59c5b2-overview.webp" | relative_url }}" alt="Overview image for GEIPAN Files" loading="eager" decoding="sync" fetchpriority="high">

The distinction matters. A bright light may receive category A when its position matches Venus exactly, category B when photographs strongly suggest a balloon but do not prove it, or category C when the time, bearing or witness information is too uncertain to test. Category D is reserved for a report that remains unexplained despite having enough substance to investigate. In the Doubs material, the recurring lesson is therefore evidential rather than sensational: precise timing and direction often solve cases, while missing information usually weakens them rather than making them more extraordinary.

## How GEIPAN grades unusual-sky reports

GEIPAN, a unit of the French space agency CNES, collects, analyses and publishes reports of unidentified aerospace phenomena. It was created in 1977 and uses a national network that can draw on gendarmes, volunteer investigators and specialists in fields such as astronomy, meteorology, aviation and image analysis. Its purpose is to explain observations where possible and preserve the remaining cases, not to begin from the assumption that witnesses saw extraterrestrial craft.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cnes.fr/en/projects/geipan" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes.fr">[CNES]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">GEIPAN7 Jul 2025 — GEIPAN, the French UAP research and information group created by CNES in 1977, collects, analyses and archives inf...</span></span></span>

Its familiar A-to-D scale is based on two linked questions: how strong is the information, and how strange does the phenomenon remain after ordinary explanations have been tested? GEIPAN describes these qualities as the case’s **consistency** and **residual strangeness**. Consistency rises when the file contains accurate times, reliable directions, several compatible witnesses, original photographs, identifiable landmarks or independent records. Residual strangeness falls when those details fit a known object or event.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/fr/actualites/methodologie-classification-geipan" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-title">methodologie classification geipan</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">GEIPANLa méthodologie de classification au GEIPAN26 Feb 2021 — La classification du GEIPAN (A/B/C/D) s&#x27;appuie sur l&#x27;évaluation de deux no...</span></span></span>

The categories mean:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* **A — identified:** the explanation is considered secure.
* **B — probably identified:** one ordinary explanation fits very well, although the evidence does not establish it beyond reasonable doubt.
* **C — insufficiently documented:** the phenomenon cannot be identified because essential information is missing, unreliable or unusable.
* **D — unexplained after investigation:** the evidence was adequate for investigation, but no satisfactory explanation was found.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/en/faq-page" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">vestigation...</span></span></span>

</div>

This framework prevents two common mistakes. First, category B does not mean that investigators merely guessed. It normally means that an explanation accounts for the object’s appearance, motion, timing and circumstances but lacks one final confirmation, such as the recovery of the balloon or identification of the person who released a lantern. Secondly, category C is not a stronger version of category D. It is an information failure. GEIPAN may not know what the witness saw, but it also lacks enough evidence to treat the observation as a robust anomaly.

GEIPAN can revisit C and D cases when new material appears. A later witness, a better date, an original photograph or an archived aviation record may change the classification. The letter attached to a file is therefore the outcome of the evidence available at a particular stage, not an unalterable judgement on the witness.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/en/faq-page" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">vestigation...</span></span></span>

## Exact times and directions turn lights into known objects

The most decisive Doubs classifications are those in which the reported geometry can be reconstructed. A witness may describe an object as stationary, enormous or strangely coloured, but GEIPAN first asks where it appeared, at what time and how it moved against the landscape.

The Étupes report of 22 August 2017 is a clear example. At about 4.30 am, a witness watched and recorded a bright, nearly stationary light in the eastern sky for roughly half an hour. Zoomed images appeared to show changing shapes and colours. Those features might look unusual when viewed alone, but a daylight video contained enough landscape detail for investigators to estimate the line of sight. At 6.08 am, the object’s estimated bearing was about 84 degrees. Astronomical software placed Venus at the same bearing, shining at approximately magnitude −4. GEIPAN classified the case A.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/fr/cas/2017-08-50460" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on cnes-geipan.fr.</span></span></span>

The images did not overturn that identification. GEIPAN explained that heavy digital or optical zoom on a point-like light can produce defocused shapes, chromatic fringes and apparent changes caused by the camera’s aperture, electronics and movement. In other words, the odd-looking object in the recording was partly an image of Venus and partly an image of how the camera handled Venus. The case illustrates why a photograph is not automatically stronger evidence than naked-eye testimony: its value depends on whether investigators can establish scale, focus, direction and the camera’s limitations.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/fr/cas/2017-08-50460" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on cnes-geipan.fr.</span></span></span>

Several other Doubs files were solved through time-and-track matching. Reports from Rochejean on 19 April 2011, Fourg on 14 June 2011 and Pontarlier on 17 August 2013 were classified A as sightings of the International Space Station. A later report from Frambouhans on 17 July 2022 received the same identification. These cases show why a silent light can seem more unusual than an aircraft: the ISS can be exceptionally bright, cross a large part of the sky steadily and then disappear when it enters the Earth’s shadow. Matching the reported minute and trajectory with an orbital pass can turn a seemingly untraceable object into a highly specific identification.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/fr/recherche/cas/tab?field_agregation_index_value=&amp;field_classification_des_cas_target_id%5B11%5D=11&amp;field_date_d_observation_value%5Bmax%5D=&amp;field_date_d_observation_value%5Bmin%5D=&amp;field_date_value=&amp;field_departement_target_id=&amp;field_document_existe_ou_pas_value=1&amp;field_is_new_value=All&amp;field_is_revisited_value=All&amp;field_latitude_value%5Bmax%5D=&amp;field_latitude_value%5Bmin%5D=&amp;field_longitude_value%5Bmax%5D=&amp;field_longitude_value%5Bmin%5D=&amp;field_phenomene_target_id=&amp;field_type_de_cas_target_id=All&amp;order=field_departement_textuel&amp;page=9&amp;select-category-export=nothing&amp;sort=asc&amp;video=on" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on cnes-geipan.fr.</span></span></span>

The same principle applies to less exotic astronomical objects. A 1980 report from Grandfontaine was classified B as the Moon. The important point is not that witnesses cannot recognise familiar objects under normal conditions. It is that haze, cloud gaps, unfamiliar viewing angles, partial obstruction and the lack of a visible horizon can make even the Moon or a planet appear detached from its ordinary context.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/fr/recherche/cas/tab?field_is_new_value=All&amp;field_is_revisited_value=All&amp;field_type_de_cas_target_id=All&amp;order=field_departement_textuel&amp;page=37&amp;s=09&amp;sort=asc" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on cnes-geipan.fr.</span></span></span>

## Aircraft records resolved the Vieilley “fireball”

The Vieilley sighting of 19 May 2022 demonstrates the value of checking an observation against records unavailable to the witness. A single observer heard an exceptionally loud, thunder-like noise and saw a large red-orange luminous form moving rapidly towards Besançon. It was visible for only several seconds, and no photograph or video was taken. On description alone, the report could have suggested a meteor, a burning object or an unusually low craft.

GEIPAN examined the relationship between the sound, motion and reported direction. The National Air Operations Centre of the French Air and Space Force confirmed that two Mirage 2000 aircraft were operating near the area at the relevant time. Investigators concluded that the phenomenon was an aircraft seen under conditions that made its lights and exhaust appear like a fiery ball. The case was classified A.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/fr/cas/2022-05-51344" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on cnes-geipan.fr.</span></span></span>

This was not simply an argument that “there are aircraft in the sky”. The explanation gained weight because a specific military patrol was independently placed near the observation at the correct time. The powerful continuing noise also fitted fast military jets better than a silent astronomical object. GEIPAN noted that the file had only medium consistency before investigation because there was one witness and no imagery, but the external aviation confirmation supplied the missing corroboration.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/fr/cas/2022-05-51344" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on cnes-geipan.fr.</span></span></span>

The case also shows why witness estimates of size and distance are treated cautiously. A bright object against a dark or featureless sky provides no dependable scale. Something distant and intensely luminous may be interpreted as a large object nearby, especially when a loud sound reinforces the impression of closeness. The witness’s experience can therefore be sincere and dramatic even when the physical source is conventional.

## Balloons, lanterns and birds produce the harder probable cases

Not every ordinary explanation can be proved as firmly as Venus, the ISS or a recorded military flight. Many Doubs reports fall into category B because one explanation fits the observation substantially better than its alternatives but lacks a direct identifying record.

At Pontarlier on 22 June 2021, photographs pointed towards a novelty helium or Mylar balloon. Such balloons can rotate, reflect sunlight unevenly and present changing outlines, making them look mechanical or animated. GEIPAN regarded the photographic appearance as sufficiently characteristic to classify the case B, while stopping short of certainty because the actual balloon was not recovered and its release was not independently documented.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/fr/cas/2021-06-51209?field_agregation_index_value=&amp;field_classification_des_cas_target_id%5B11%5D=11&amp;field_classification_des_cas_target_id%5B12%5D=12&amp;field_classification_des_cas_target_id%5B13%5D=13&amp;field_classification_des_cas_target_id%5B14%5D=14&amp;field_date_d_observation_value%5Bmax%5D=&amp;field_date_d_observation_value%5Bmin%5D=&amp;field_date_value=&amp;field_departement_target_id=&amp;field_document_existe_ou_pas_value=1&amp;field_is_new_value=All&amp;field_is_revisited_value=All&amp;field_latitude_value%5Bmax%5D=&amp;field_latitude_value%5Bmin%5D=&amp;field_longitude_value%5Bmax%5D=&amp;field_longitude_value%5Bmin%5D=&amp;field_phenomene_target_id=&amp;field_type_de_cas_target_id=All&amp;order=field_date_d_observation&amp;page=%2C31&amp;select-category-export=nothing&amp;sort=desc&amp;video=on" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on cnes-geipan.fr.</span></span></span>

Pontarlier also supplied a stronger lantern case on 2 December 2019. A group of orange lights moved together, while one light appeared to behave slightly differently. GEIPAN examined the wind and considered it plausible that a lantern travelling through a calmer pocket of air would temporarily lag behind the others. The group formation, colour, motion and circumstances were sufficiently consistent with flying lanterns for the report to receive category A.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/fr/cas/2019-12-50892" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on cnes-geipan.fr.</span></span></span>

Other Doubs entries show the same recurring explanation. Besançon reports from 2 April 2011 and 26 June 2017 were classified B as flying lanterns, while the Montferrand-le-Château report of 18 August 2013 was classified A. The difference between A and B does not necessarily reflect how spectacular the sighting was. It reflects how completely the available details tied the lights to the proposed cause.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/fr/recherche/cas/tab?field_agregation_index_value=&amp;field_classification_des_cas_target_id%5B11%5D=11&amp;field_classification_des_cas_target_id%5B12%5D=12&amp;field_classification_des_cas_target_id%5B13%5D=13&amp;field_classification_des_cas_target_id%5B14%5D=14&amp;field_date_d_observation_value%5Bmax%5D=&amp;field_date_d_observation_value%5Bmin%5D=&amp;field_date_value=&amp;field_departement_target_id=&amp;field_document_existe_ou_pas_value=1&amp;field_is_new_value=All&amp;field_is_revisited_value=All&amp;field_latitude_value%5Bmax%5D=&amp;field_latitude_value%5Bmin%5D=&amp;field_longitude_value%5Bmax%5D=&amp;field_longitude_value%5Bmin%5D=&amp;field_phenomene_target_id=&amp;field_type_de_cas_target_id=All&amp;order=field_departement_textuel&amp;page=122%2C20&amp;select-category-export=nothing&amp;sort=desc&amp;video=on" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on cnes-geipan.fr.</span></span></span>

The Belvoir case of 30 July 2017 shows the limits of visual recognition even when witnesses describe an apparently structured object. Observers reported a brief, silent passage of a dark formation containing triangular elements. GEIPAN considered birds the most probable explanation. A flock seen at night can be visible only when its undersides catch artificial light, while the gaps and changing alignment between birds can create the impression of a single dark object with separate components. Because investigators could not prove that birds were present or fully explain why the witnesses failed to recognise them, the report remained a probable misidentification rather than a certain one.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cnes-geipan.fr/fr/cas/2017-07-50402" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on cnes-geipan.fr.</span></span></span>

These category B files are useful because they preserve uncertainty without treating every unresolved detail as evidence of an unknown technology. The explanation must fit the main features, but GEIPAN can still acknowledge that the precise balloon, lantern release or flock was not independently traced.

## Photographs help only when their position can be recovered

The Baume-les-Dames observation of 17 December 2013 is one of the clearest demonstrations of why photographs alone may not produce a classification. At about 7.15 pm, a witness noticed a stationary white light while using an astronomy application. The light remained visible for roughly 15 minutes and was photographed, but it had disappeared when the witness returned after a short break. No second independent report was obtained.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/fr/cas/2013-12-08619" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on cnes-geipan.fr.</span></span></span>

GEIPAN tested several possibilities. Astronomical checks did not place a suitably bright star or planet in the reported area. An aircraft appeared unlikely because the light seemed stationary for too long. A vehicle or temporary light on a nearby hill did not fit the witness’s estimated elevation. A flying lantern had the right broad colour and could have been released from several nearby sites, but its apparent lack of movement and the length of the observation were awkward.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/fr/cas/2013-12-08619" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on cnes-geipan.fr.</span></span></span>

The result was category C, not D. Investigators could not confirm the lantern hypothesis, but neither could they reconstruct the light’s exact celestial position. The photographs lacked enough fixed reference points to establish a dependable bearing and elevation, and there were no independent witnesses or external records. The case remained unidentified because the key measurement was missing.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/fr/cas/2013-12-08619" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on cnes-geipan.fr.</span></span></span>

This distinction is central to understanding the Doubs archive. A blurry light against a black background may accurately record that something bright entered the camera, yet reveal almost nothing about whether it was ten metres away or thousands of kilometres away. Without stars, buildings, terrain or unambiguous metadata, investigators may be unable to compare it with aircraft routes, orbital tracks or astronomical objects. A photograph can therefore document an experience while still failing to identify its source.

## Category C records missing evidence, not stronger mystery

Doubs contains a substantial group of older category C files. Published entries include reports from Rennes-sur-Loue in 1976, Glay in 1978, Valentigney in 1980, Seloncourt in 1987, an aviation-related record associated with La Rochelle in 1989, Béthoncourt in 1993, Saint-Vit in 1998 and Rouhe in 2009. GEIPAN’s listings describe these cases primarily as lacking reliable information.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cnes-geipan.fr/fr/recherche/cas/tab?order=field_departement_textuel&amp;page=130&amp;sort=desc" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">GEIPANRecherche de cas &#124; GEIPANListe des cas; PONTARLIER (25) 22.06.2021, 22/06/2021, B · Doubs, Ballon d enfant; VIEILLEY (25) 19.05.2...</span></span></span>

The Saint-Vit file shows what this means in practice. A press account described three people in a car seeing a shiny structure, reportedly 20 to 25 metres across, with dark markings and yellow and red lights. A later witness reported a luminous disc on another date. However, the original three witnesses could not be identified and formally interviewed, while the available descriptions were brief and partly contradictory. GEIPAN classified the combined material C because it could not conduct a serious reconstruction.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/fr/cas/1998-01-01497" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on cnes-geipan.fr.</span></span></span>

The dramatic language in the press account did not compensate for the missing primary evidence. Investigators could not establish precise positions, compare the passengers’ recollections separately or determine whether the two dates involved the same phenomenon. The estimated size was also impossible to verify without a known distance. The file therefore preserves a local UFO story, but not a strong unexplained case.

Older gendarmerie records often face similar problems. A statement may survive while its maps, original negatives, weather details or exact observation time do not. Witnesses may no longer be contactable, or a case may have reached the archive only through a summary. GEIPAN can catalogue such material without pretending that a modern investigation can recover information that was never recorded.

Category C also protects against false precision. Investigators are not required to choose the least implausible ordinary explanation merely to close the file. In the Baume-les-Dames case, for example, a lantern remained possible but did not fit well enough for category B. Calling the case C was an admission that the available evidence could neither confirm the lantern nor justify a more exotic conclusion.

## What category D would mean in Doubs

Category D is often misunderstood as an official declaration that an extraordinary craft was present. GEIPAN’s definition is narrower: the phenomenon remains unidentified after investigation, and the file is sufficiently consistent to make that failure meaningful. A D case should therefore have more evidential value than a C case, but the category does not identify the cause or endorse an extraterrestrial interpretation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/en/faq-page" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">vestigation...</span></span></span>

For a Doubs report to make a persuasive category D candidate, it would need dependable basics such as a precise date and time, a recoverable viewing direction, enough duration for careful observation and witness accounts that can be compared independently. Photographs would need original files and identifiable reference points. Claims involving aviation might require radar, flight or military checks. Astronomical and satellite explanations would need to be tested against the reconstructed line of sight.

The published Doubs material most readily visible in GEIPAN’s departmental listings is instead dominated by A, B and C classifications: known objects, probable misidentifications and files that cannot be reconstructed. That pattern is significant. It means the department’s official archive should not be read as a collection of equally mysterious incidents. A solved ISS pass and an old untraceable press report may both begin with an unidentified light, but they occupy very different evidential positions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cnes-geipan.fr/fr/recherche/cas/tab?order=field_departement_textuel&amp;page=130&amp;sort=desc" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">GEIPANRecherche de cas &#124; GEIPANListe des cas; PONTARLIER (25) 22.06.2021, 22/06/2021, B · Doubs, Ballon d enfant; VIEILLEY (25) 19.05.2...</span></span></span>

Nationally, category D forms only a small minority of GEIPAN’s database. CNES reported that, by the end of 2025, slightly more than 3,200 cases had been recorded: about 66 per cent were in A or B, 31 per cent in C and roughly 3 per cent in D. Those figures reinforce the proper interpretation of the Doubs files. Most reports are eventually explained or remain weak because of missing information; only a small residue combines adequate evidence with continuing strangeness.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cnes.fr/actualites/serie-ovnis-5-choses-savoir-geipan" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes.fr">[CNES]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-title">serie ovnis 5 choses savoir geipan</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">serie ovnis 5 choses savoir geipan</span></span></span>

## What the Doubs files actually establish

GEIPAN’s classifications do not show that Doubs witnesses were generally unreliable. They show that human observation is highly dependent on context. Venus filmed out of focus can appear to change shape. The ISS can resemble a silent, self-luminous craft. A lantern can look stationary when the wind is light. Birds can create a fleeting dark formation. Military aircraft can appear as a fiery object when their lights, exhaust and noise are encountered from an unfamiliar angle.

The strongest explanations are those supported by several independent matches. Étupes combined direction, time and astronomical position. Vieilley combined sound, trajectory and confirmed military activity. The ISS cases combined timing with orbital movement. In each, investigators did more than attach a familiar label to a strange description: they identified a known phenomenon expected to occupy the relevant part of the sky.

The weaker files reveal the opposite pattern. Baume-les-Dames had photographs but no dependable bearing or corroboration. Saint-Vit had vivid claims but no access to the principal witnesses. Other category C records preserve observations whose dates, directions or supporting materials are too poor for meaningful comparison. Their unresolved status reflects the limits of the file rather than the demonstrated qualities of the phenomenon.

For readers tracing UFO history across Doubs, the official archive is therefore most valuable as a record of how claims change under investigation. The crucial dividing lines are not between ordinary witnesses and supposedly exceptional ones, or between dull and spectacular stories. They are between observations that can be reconstructed, observations that support a probable explanation and reports whose missing evidence prevents any firm conclusion.
