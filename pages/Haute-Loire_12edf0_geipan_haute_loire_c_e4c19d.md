## Introduction

France’s official UFO service, GEIPAN, reached three notably different conclusions when it revisited later reports from [Haute-Loire]({{ 'haute-loire/' | relative_url }}). A bright crescent that seemed to pursue a car was identified as the setting Moon. A silent metallic object watched for half an hour was judged probably to have been a drifting Mylar balloon. A powerful yellow light reported near Salettes remained unclassified because the evidence was too incomplete to test properly.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://geipan.fr/fr/cas/1980-08-00795?field_date_value=2007-03-01&amp;field_is_new_value=All&amp;field_is_revisited_value=All&amp;field_type_de_cas_target_id=All&amp;order=title&amp;page=8&amp;sort=asc" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: geipan.fr">[Geipan]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">AUVERS (43) 17.08.1980AUVERS (43) 17.08.1980. Date d&#x27;observation. 17/08/1980. Région. Auvergne... Témoignages. Témoignages. AUVERS...</span></span></span>

Together, the cases show why an official file marked “unidentified” or “insufficient information” is not confirmation of an extraordinary craft. GEIPAN separates the strangeness of a story from the strength of the underlying evidence. A report may sound highly unusual yet remain scientifically weak if investigators lack precise directions, timings, independent witnesses or contemporary records. Conversely, an initially puzzling incident may become ordinary when astronomical calculations, terrain reconstruction or experience with balloons supplies the missing comparison.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.geipan.fr/en/node/58787" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: geipan.fr">[Geipan]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on geipan.fr.</span></span></span>

## The Moon that appeared to follow a car

The Auvers case of 17 August 1980 is the clearest Haute-Loire example of a dramatic encounter losing its mystery on re-examination. At about 10 p.m., two people at a forest house noticed an intensely bright phenomenon towards the west. Through binoculars, it appeared as a large crescent emitting strong rays. They entered a vehicle to approach it, but the light then seemed to move rapidly and follow them. Frightened, they turned back; the phenomenon appeared to stop and subsequently disappeared. Searches found no ground or vegetation traces, and no additional witnesses provided corroboration.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://geipan.fr/fr/cas/1980-08-00795?field_date_value=2007-03-01&amp;field_is_new_value=All&amp;field_is_revisited_value=All&amp;field_type_de_cas_target_id=All&amp;order=title&amp;page=8&amp;sort=asc" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: geipan.fr">[Geipan]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">AUVERS (43) 17.08.1980AUVERS (43) 17.08.1980. Date d&#x27;observation. 17/08/1980. Région. Auvergne... Témoignages. Témoignages. AUVERS...</span></span></span>

The case was originally classified as unexplained, but GEIPAN later changed it to category A, meaning that the phenomenon was considered identified after investigation. Its conclusion was a misidentification of the Moon near the horizon. The Moon occupied the relevant part of the sky, matched the reported crescent shape, brightness, colour and duration, and was not otherwise mentioned by the witnesses. GEIPAN stressed that it did not doubt their sincerity. The error lay in judging what the light was, how far away it was and how it was moving—not in inventing the sighting.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://geipan.fr/en/node/48665" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: geipan.fr">[Geipan]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on geipan.fr.</span></span></span>

The sensation of pursuit is a recognised problem when a very distant light is observed from a moving vehicle. Because the Moon is effectively fixed relative to the traveller over a short journey, it may remain in the same part of the windscreen while nearby trees, bends and hills sweep past. Once the observer assumes that the light is a nearby object, its stable direction can be interpreted as deliberate movement matching the car. Turns may make it appear to cross behind the vehicle, change sides or stop when the vehicle stops. GEIPAN describes this effect in other investigations as the “following ball” illusion.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.geipan.fr/sites/default/files/Notes%20d%27enquete12.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: geipan.fr">[Geipan]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Notes d&#x27;enquete12</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Notes d&#x27;enquete12</span></span></span>

Auvers is important because the later explanation did not depend on dismissing frightened witnesses as unreliable. GEIPAN regarded the two-person account as reasonably consistent and the location description as useful. The decisive change came from reconstructing the sky and comparing the reported behaviour with a known perceptual effect. Emotion may then have reinforced the impression that the light was acting intentionally: after the witnesses became afraid, each apparent movement was interpreted within the idea of pursuit.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://geipan.fr/fr/cas/1980-08-00795?field_date_value=2007-03-01&amp;field_is_new_value=All&amp;field_is_revisited_value=All&amp;field_type_de_cas_target_id=All&amp;order=title&amp;page=8&amp;sort=asc" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: geipan.fr">[Geipan]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">AUVERS (43) 17.08.1980AUVERS (43) 17.08.1980. Date d&#x27;observation. 17/08/1980. Région. Auvergne... Témoignages. Témoignages. AUVERS...</span></span></span>

That distinction matters throughout Haute-Loire’s UFO record. A sincere account can preserve the experience accurately—bright light, apparent motion, fear—while still assigning the wrong cause, distance and scale to what was seen.

## The balloon explanation and the limits of proof

A second official finding concerned Saint-Cirgues on the morning of 9 October 1994. One witness, positioned during a hunt, watched a silent, bright cylindrical object for roughly 25 to 30 minutes. It appeared white with metallic silver reflections and included blue and red features. A second witness nearby independently described a partly cylindrical, partly rounded object reflecting sunlight and moving through the landscape. Both observations lasted unusually long, and the gendarmerie prepared a plan of the area.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://geipan.fr/fr/temoignage/5820" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: geipan.fr">[Geipan]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">SAINT-CIRGUES (43) 09.10.1994 T2SAINT-CIRGUES (43) 09.10.1994 T1 · SAINT-CIRGUES (43) 09.10.1994 T2. Those details gave the case more substance than a brief report of a distant point of light. There were two separately recorded witnesses, lengthy observations and useful descriptions of the object’s appearance and movement. Yet GEIPAN eventually judged that the same details favoured a mundane explanation: a metallised Mylar balloon. It reclassified the formerly unexplained case as category B, indicating a probable rather than conclusively demonstrated identification.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://geipan.fr/fr/temoignage/5820" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: geipan.fr">[Geipan]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">SAINT-CIRGUES (43) 09.10.1994 T2SAINT-CIRGUES (43) 09.10.1994 T1 · SAINT-CIRGUES (43) 09.10.1994 T2. The proposed explanation drew on a combination of appearance, motion and local conditions:

* The silver or metallic reflections were compatible with a foil balloon catching the morning sunlight.
* The slow, mainly horizontal and silent movement fitted an object drifting in weak local air currents.
* The low movement within a mountain hollow could be explained by cool, relatively still air near the ground.
* The reported rise towards the end of the observation was consistent with warming air expanding inside the balloon and increasing its buoyancy.
* One of the witnesses independently considered a balloon possible.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://geipan.fr/fr/temoignage/5820" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: geipan.fr">[Geipan]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">SAINT-CIRGUES (43) 09.10.1994 T2SAINT-CIRGUES (43) 09.10.1994 T1 · SAINT-CIRGUES (43) 09.10.1994 T2. GEIPAN also proposed a plausible sequence beginning the previous evening. A balloon released on the Saturday could have cooled after sunset, lost buoyancy and descended into a hollow. As sunlight warmed it the following morning, it could first have drifted close to the ground and later risen. This was a reconstruction rather than proof of a particular launch: investigators did not identify the owner, release point or surviving balloon. That missing chain of custody is why “probable balloon” is more accurate than “balloon proven beyond doubt”.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://geipan.fr/fr/temoignage/5820" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: geipan.fr">[Geipan]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">SAINT-CIRGUES (43) 09.10.1994 T2SAINT-CIRGUES (43) 09.10.1994 T1 · SAINT-CIRGUES (43) 09.10.1994 T2. The Saint-Cirgues reassessment also shows how institutional experience changes conclusions. GEIPAN stated that investigators had relatively little experience of balloon cases when the sighting was first processed. By the later review, comparisons with a larger body of reports made the combination of metallic reflections, slow drifting and silent movement more recognisable. The evidence itself had not become stronger; the range of tested explanations had improved.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://geipan.fr/fr/temoignage/5820" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: geipan.fr">[Geipan]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">SAINT-CIRGUES (43) 09.10.1994 T2SAINT-CIRGUES (43) 09.10.1994 T1 · SAINT-CIRGUES (43) 09.10.1994 T2. An earlier aviation case near Brioude illustrates both the strength and the limits of balloon reasoning. On 20 September 1956, two military pilots attempted to approach an aluminium-coloured sphere but could not close the apparent distance. The Puy-de-Dôme observatory reported a large translucent spherical balloon drifting at about 20 kilometres altitude that day. GEIPAN classified the case B because the observed object was probably that stratospheric balloon, although its origin was never established. Calculations indicated that it remained many kilometres from the aircraft even after the pilots climbed, helping explain why it seemed unreachable.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.geipan.fr/fr/temoignage/200" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: geipan.fr">[Geipan]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on geipan.fr.</span></span></span>

Brioude strengthens the general balloon interpretation because an independently observed high-altitude balloon matched the time and broad appearance. Saint-Cirgues is less tightly demonstrated: its conclusion rests on a close fit between behaviour and a known class of object. In both files, category B communicates warranted confidence without pretending that every factual link has been documented.

## Why an unclassified case is not confirmed evidence

The Salettes report of 19 June 2008 produced a very different official outcome. At about 10.30 p.m., two people travelling on a rural road saw a powerful round yellow light apparently directed towards the ground. It seemed to perform slow clockwise circles, made no noticeable sound and disappeared as they entered the village. The summary also recorded the impression that the light followed their vehicle.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://geipan.fr/fr/cas/2008-06-08565?field_date_value=2007-03-01&amp;field_is_new_value=All&amp;field_is_revisited_value=All&amp;field_type_de_cas_target_id=All&amp;order=field_classification_des_cas&amp;page=142&amp;sort=asc" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: geipan.fr">[Geipan]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on geipan.fr.</span></span></span>

Unlike Auvers, the available material did not contain enough precise information to test an astronomical explanation. Unlike Saint-Cirgues, there was no detailed gendarmerie reconstruction, sufficiently complete pair of witness accounts or well-defined description from which to compare shape and movement with a balloon. The report had reached investigators through a simplified online questionnaire supplied by a UFO association. GEIPAN judged the incident moderately to highly strange but too poorly documented to qualify as a robust unexplained case. It therefore placed it in category C: not workable because reliable information was missing.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://geipan.fr/fr/cas/2008-06-08565?field_date_value=2007-03-01&amp;field_is_new_value=All&amp;field_is_revisited_value=All&amp;field_type_de_cas_target_id=All&amp;order=field_classification_des_cas&amp;page=142&amp;sort=asc" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: geipan.fr">[Geipan]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on geipan.fr.</span></span></span>

This is the most easily misunderstood official result. Category C does not mean that investigators found an extraordinary object but lacked a name for it. It means they could not perform a dependable comparison between the report and possible causes. Important unknowns may include the precise route, viewing direction, elevation, cloud cover, duration, changes at bends, whether both occupants observed continuously, and whether the apparent beam actually illuminated identifiable ground features. Without such data, proposed explanations—an astronomical object, aircraft light, searchlight, balloon or mistaken interpretation of a distant source—cannot be ranked with confidence.

GEIPAN’s method treats “strangeness” and “consistency” as separate measures. Strangeness concerns how poorly ordinary hypotheses appear to fit after analysis. Consistency concerns the quantity, precision and reliability of the information available, including the number and independence of witnesses and the presence of photographs or other records. A case cannot become a strong unexplained classification merely by sounding strange; high strangeness must be supported by correspondingly strong evidence. When that support is absent, category C is the appropriate result.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.geipan.fr/en/node/58787" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: geipan.fr">[Geipan]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on geipan.fr.</span></span></span>

Salettes therefore remains open only in a limited documentary sense. New independent testimony, a contemporary photograph, a precise journey reconstruction or corroborating aviation and weather records could justify renewed analysis. In their absence, the case neither supports a conventional identification nor provides persuasive evidence of an unknown vehicle. Its official status records an evidential failure, not a confirmed mystery.

## What the later findings changed

Taken together, the Haute-Loire files show three ways that official investigation can alter a UFO story.

**Auvers moved from mystery to identification.** Astronomical reconstruction explained both the crescent itself and the disturbing impression that it accompanied the witnesses’ car. The later work substantially weakened the original unexplained interpretation while leaving the witnesses’ honesty intact.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://geipan.fr/fr/cas/1980-08-00795?field_date_value=2007-03-01&amp;field_is_new_value=All&amp;field_is_revisited_value=All&amp;field_type_de_cas_target_id=All&amp;order=title&amp;page=8&amp;sort=asc" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: geipan.fr">[Geipan]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">AUVERS (43) 17.08.1980AUVERS (43) 17.08.1980. **Saint-Cirgues moved from mystery to probability.** Two good accounts and a gendarmerie plan preserved a meaningful case, but the appearance and low, drifting movement fitted a Mylar balloon better than an unknown craft. The conclusion is persuasive rather than absolute because no specific balloon was recovered or traced.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://geipan.fr/fr/temoignage/5820" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: geipan.fr">[Geipan]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">SAINT-CIRGUES (43) 09.10.1994 T2SAINT-CIRGUES (43) 09.10.1994 T1 · SAINT-CIRGUES (43) 09.10.1994 T2. **Salettes remained unusable rather than unexplained in a strong sense.** The story retained unusual features, but its simplified documentation prevented investigators from determining whether those features survived careful reconstruction. Later examination therefore neither confirmed nor debunked the sighting; it exposed how little could safely be concluded.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://geipan.fr/fr/cas/2008-06-08565?field_date_value=2007-03-01&amp;field_is_new_value=All&amp;field_is_revisited_value=All&amp;field_type_de_cas_target_id=All&amp;order=field_classification_des_cas&amp;page=142&amp;sort=asc" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: geipan.fr">[Geipan]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on geipan.fr.</span></span></span>

These outcomes make Haute-Loire a useful small-scale example of GEIPAN’s broader approach. Classification is not a permanent badge attached to the witness’s first description. Old cases may be reassessed when better astronomical software, terrain tools, technical knowledge or accumulated experience provide stronger comparisons. GEIPAN explicitly allows reclassification when new information or improved analysis changes the balance of probability.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.geipan.fr/en/node/58787" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: geipan.fr">[Geipan]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on geipan.fr.</span></span></span>

The department’s later official record therefore offers little support for a continuing pattern of confirmed unknown craft. Its strongest lesson is methodological. A light can appear to pursue a traveller because it is extremely distant; an object can look mechanical because reflective material changes shape and brightness as it drifts; and a genuinely puzzling report can remain scientifically weak because the questions needed to test it were never answered. Official investigation did not make every Haute-Loire sighting certain. It clarified which conclusions the surviving evidence could—and could not—carry.
