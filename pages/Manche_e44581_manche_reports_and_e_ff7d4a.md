## Introduction

[Manche]({{ 'what-manches-ufo-record-actually-shows/' | relative_url }})’s UFO record is valuable less for spectacular mysteries than for the way cases separate into three very different outcomes: reports that cannot be tested, unusual events with plausible but unproved natural explanations, and incidents largely resolved by physical evidence. The contrast is clearest in the department’s 1979 and 1983 files and in the burnt field discovered near Brix in October 1989.



<img src="{{ "/assets/images/Manche_e44581_manche_reports_and_e_ff7d4a-overview.webp" | relative_url }}" alt="Overview image for Cases Explained" loading="eager" decoding="sync" fetchpriority="high">

The early sightings sound dramatic: an orange ball making rapid movements, another unexplained report a month later, and a blue fireball that whistled, exploded and frightened cattle. Yet the official files contain too little dependable information to decide what the witnesses actually saw. Brix began with apparently stronger evidence—a large area of scorched ground and a report of intense blue light—but laboratory analysis pointed towards burning petroleum rather than a landed craft. Together, these cases show why vivid testimony and visible traces are not automatically strong evidence, and why an ordinary explanation can emerge only after a proper investigation.

## The 1979 and 1983 reports had too little evidence

France’s official investigation service, GEIPAN, classifies cases according to both their residual strangeness and the consistency of the available information. A category C case is not a confirmed anomaly. It means that the material is too weak or incomplete for a reliable conclusion. ### The repeating orange ball at Chef-du-Pont

At about 11 p.m. on 22 March 1979, a witness at Chef-du-Pont reported an orange ball high in the sky, apparently about the size of a football at arm’s length. It was said to move horizontally in rapid back-and-forth motions, then vertically while showing green reflections. According to the official summary, a similar display occurred in the same place and at roughly the same time the following night.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.geipan.fr/fr/cas/1979-03-00612" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: geipan.fr">[Geipan]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">CHEF-DU-PONT (50) 22.03.1979CHEF-DU-PONT (50) 22.03.1979. Date d&#x27;observation. 22/03/1979. Région. Basse Normandie. Département. Man...</span></span></span>

That repetition sounds important, because a phenomenon returning on schedule might suggest a regular astronomical object, a distant light seen through unstable air, or recurring activity along the same route. But the file lacks the measurements needed to test those possibilities. There was no second witness, no reliable angular position, no usable estimate of duration and no evidence linking the reported light to a physical object.

The description of a football-sized ball also illustrates a common problem in night-sky reports. Without a known distance, an apparent size cannot be converted into an actual size. A small nearby light and an enormous distant object can occupy the same angle in the observer’s field of view. Apparent darting or oscillation may also be exaggerated when a bright point is watched against a dark, featureless sky, although the surviving evidence is insufficient to show that this happened at Chef-du-Pont.

GEIPAN therefore did not identify the object, but neither did it judge the report strong enough to preserve as an unexplained case. It remains category C: an account that cannot now support much beyond the fact that the witness saw something unusual.

### Saint-Germain-le-Gaillard added another incomplete report

A second Manche observation was recorded at Saint-Germain-le-Gaillard on 27 April 1979. The public GEIPAN index likewise classifies it as category C because of insufficient reliable information.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.geipan.fr/fr/recherche/cas/tab?field_document_existe_ou_pas_value=All&amp;field_is_new_value=All&amp;field_is_revisited_value=All&amp;field_type_de_cas_target_id=All&amp;order=field_classification_des_cas&amp;page=119&amp;sort=asc" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: geipan.fr">[Geipan]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Recherche de cas &#124; GEIPANSAINT-GERMAIN-LE-GAILLARD (50) 27.04.1979 &#124; 27/04/1979 &#124; C &#124; Manche &#124; Manque d&#x27;informations fiables &#124; 22/0...</span></span></span>

Its significance lies mainly in what it does **not** establish. Two reports within five weeks can look like the beginning of a local wave, but proximity in date is not enough to demonstrate a shared cause. The available record does not show matching trajectories, colours, durations, witness groups or environmental conditions. There is no documented radar confirmation, aircraft encounter or physical trace connecting the two incidents.

For Manche’s wider history, the pair should therefore be treated as scattered reports rather than a proven 1979 cluster. They may have involved different stimuli entirely. Once the missing observational detail is recognised, the apparent pattern becomes much weaker.

### The Cerisy-la-Salle fireball

The event dated 29 January 1983, associated in GEIPAN’s file with Cerisy-la-Salle and witness statements from the Savigny area, was more dramatic. Two farmers reported a peculiar bluish luminous phenomenon. One described a fireball approximately the apparent size of a ball, moving horizontally, producing a whistling sound and then exploding. The livestock were reportedly alarmed. Investigators searched for ground traces but obtained no further physical information.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://geipan.fr/fr/cas/1983-01-00962?field_agregation_index_value=&amp;field_classification_des_cas_target_id%5B0%5D=13&amp;field_classification_des_cas_target_id%5B1%5D=14&amp;field_date_d_observation_value%5Bmax%5D=&amp;field_date_d_observation_value%5Bmin%5D=&amp;field_departement_target_id=&amp;field_document_existe_ou_pas_value=All&amp;field_latitude_value%5Bmax%5D=&amp;field_latitude_value%5Bmin%5D=&amp;field_longitude_value%5Bmax%5D=&amp;field_longitude_value%5Bmin%5D=&amp;field_phenomene_target_id=&amp;field_type_de_cas_target_id=All&amp;order=field_date&amp;page=8&amp;select-category-export=nothing&amp;sort=asc" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: geipan.fr">[Geipan]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">CERISY LA SALLE (50) 1983CERISY LA SALLE (50) 1983; Date d&#x27;observation. 29/01/1983; Région. GEIPAN noted that the description suggested a manifestation of lightning. A rare luminous electrical phenomenon, sometimes loosely called ball lightning, could account for the glowing appearance, short duration and association with an explosive sound. However, the file does not contain enough meteorological or physical evidence to demonstrate that explanation. It remains a possibility, not a solved identification.

The case is nevertheless weaker than its vivid narrative suggests. No residue, impact point or damaged object was found. The apparent horizontal movement and size were estimates made during a brief, startling event. The whistle and explosion could have been part of the same phenomenon, a nearby electrical discharge, or sounds whose exact sequence was difficult to reconstruct afterwards.

Cerisy-la-Salle was therefore also placed in category C. This does not imply that nothing happened or that the farmers invented the event. It means the surviving evidence cannot distinguish securely among an unusual electrical event, another short-lived atmospheric phenomenon or a misunderstood combination of light and sound.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://geipan.fr/fr/cas/1983-01-00962?field_agregation_index_value=&amp;field_classification_des_cas_target_id%5B0%5D=13&amp;field_classification_des_cas_target_id%5B1%5D=14&amp;field_date_d_observation_value%5Bmax%5D=&amp;field_date_d_observation_value%5Bmin%5D=&amp;field_departement_target_id=&amp;field_document_existe_ou_pas_value=All&amp;field_latitude_value%5Bmax%5D=&amp;field_latitude_value%5Bmin%5D=&amp;field_longitude_value%5Bmax%5D=&amp;field_longitude_value%5Bmin%5D=&amp;field_phenomene_target_id=&amp;field_type_de_cas_target_id=All&amp;order=field_date&amp;page=8&amp;select-category-export=nothing&amp;sort=asc" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: geipan.fr">[Geipan]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">CERISY LA SALLE (50) 1983CERISY LA SALLE (50) 1983; Date d&#x27;observation. 29/01/1983; Région. ## The Brix burnt field gained an earthly explanation

The Brix case initially appeared stronger because investigators were not dealing only with a distant light. On the morning of 27 October 1989, a farmer discovered a large, irregular burnt area in a pasture. It measured roughly 17 metres in length and up to four metres across. Vegetation had been destroyed, the soil was blackened and parts of the surface appeared sunken by about 10 to 15 centimetres.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/fr/cas/1989-10-01668" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on cnes-geipan.fr.</span></span></span>

A second witness told gendarmes that during the night of 25–26 October a dog had behaved unusually and that an intense bluish beam had been visible above the relevant field. A 20-kilovolt power line ran approximately 50 metres from the mark. Those details gave the incident several features commonly associated with a supposed landing case: disturbed animals, anomalous light and a substantial physical trace.

Unlike the 1979 reports, however, the Brix evidence could be sampled and tested. SEPRA, the predecessor of today’s GEIPAN, attended the site after the local gendarmerie was unable to determine the cause. Investigators took soil cores, documented the mark and arranged laboratory analysis.

The results changed the character of the case:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* The soil had been burnt to a depth of roughly two to three centimetres.
* Grey discolouration in the clay indicated exposure to considerable heat.
* Samples from the affected area gave off a strong petroleum smell.
* Analysis confirmed the presence of kerosene.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/fr/cas/1989-10-01668" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on cnes-geipan.fr.</span></span></span>

</div>

The official hypothesis was that fuel had been released by an aircraft flying low and slowly. The kerosene may then have ignited near the power line, where investigators had noticed strong crackling around some insulator connections. Electrical sparking was considered a possible ignition source. The case was classified B, meaning that the phenomenon was probably identified after investigation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/fr/cas/1989-10-01668" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on cnes-geipan.fr.</span></span></span>

This conclusion requires careful wording. The analysis established petroleum contamination and intense burning; it did not directly photograph or track the aircraft that allegedly released the fuel. The exact chain of events therefore remained partly reconstructed. Yet the kerosene finding provides a direct physical connection to an ordinary industrial or aviation substance. It is substantially stronger than an explanation based only on resemblance.

The reported blue light and the dog’s behaviour do not overturn that evidence. Combustion, electrical arcing or illumination through smoke or mist could produce an unusual glow, while an animal may react to noise, smell or fire. Because the light report was retrospective and not instrumentally recorded, it cannot determine whether something separate was present above the field.

Brix is consequently best described as a probably solved fire rather than an unexplained landing. It also demonstrates that ground traces can mislead when their chemical composition is unknown. A scorched area may initially appear more convincing than a light in the sky, but it becomes useful evidence only when investigators establish what caused the scorching.

## Later cases reveal recurring ordinary explanations

Manche’s more recent files reinforce the same lesson. Once timing, wind, viewing direction and known activity are compared with the testimony, apparently structured or manoeuvring objects often become recognisable phenomena.

At Tourlaville on 6 May 2012, orange lights moving slowly and silently for several minutes were judged probably to be sky lanterns. GEIPAN noted that their movement matched the easterly wind and suggested they may have been released during celebrations following that day’s French presidential election result. The case was classified B rather than fully certain, but the combined match of colour, behaviour, duration and wind direction made the explanation persuasive.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://geipan.fr/fr/cas/2012-05-08243" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: geipan.fr">[Geipan]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on geipan.fr.</span></span></span>

Two later reports were attributed to aircraft condensation trails. Saint-Germain-sur-Ay on 23 September 2013 was classified B, while Saint-Côme-du-Mont on 12 November 2014 was classified A, indicating a more secure identification.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://geipan.fr/en/recherche/cas/tab?field_agregation_index_value=&amp;field_date_d_observation_value%5Bmax%5D=&amp;field_date_d_observation_value%5Bmin%5D=&amp;field_date_value=&amp;field_departement_target_id=&amp;field_document_existe_ou_pas_value=All&amp;field_is_new_value=All&amp;field_is_revisited_value=All&amp;field_latitude_value%5Bmax%5D=&amp;field_latitude_value%5Bmin%5D=&amp;field_longitude_value%5Bmax%5D=&amp;field_longitude_value%5Bmin%5D=&amp;field_phenomene_target_id=&amp;field_type_de_cas_target_id=All&amp;order=field_departement_textuel&amp;page=96&amp;sort=asc" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: geipan.fr">[Geipan]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on geipan.fr.</span></span></span>

Contrails can look especially strange when a high-altitude aircraft travels towards or away from an observer. Perspective may make the trail appear almost stationary or vertical, while low sunlight can colour it orange or red even after the ground has entered dusk. Winds at altitude can distort and broaden the trail until it resembles a luminous object, plume or descending mass.

A bright event observed at Moon-sur-Elle on 20 February 2021 was classified as a bolide—a particularly bright meteor.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.geipan.fr/fr/recherche/cas/tab?field_agregation_index_value=&amp;field_date_d_observation_value%5Bmax%5D=&amp;field_date_d_observation_value%5Bmin%5D=&amp;field_date_value=&amp;field_departement_target_id=&amp;field_document_existe_ou_pas_value=All&amp;field_is_new_value=All&amp;field_is_revisited_value=All&amp;field_latitude_value%5Bmax%5D=&amp;field_latitude_value%5Bmin%5D=&amp;field_longitude_value%5Bmax%5D=&amp;field_longitude_value%5Bmin%5D=&amp;field_phenomene_target_id=&amp;field_type_de_cas_target_id=All&amp;order=field_departement_textuel&amp;page=96&amp;sort=asc" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: geipan.fr">[Geipan]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on geipan.fr.</span></span></span> Such events can produce intense colour, fragmentation and an apparent low trajectory. They are brief but visually powerful, and witnesses may understandably describe them as burning craft or objects falling nearby. Reports from a wider area and agreement on timing and direction can make the meteor explanation much stronger than any single account.

These identifications do not mean that every Manche sighting has the same cause. They show instead that a small set of recurring stimuli—meteors, contrails, lanterns, aircraft-related effects, astronomical lights and atmospheric electricity—can generate very different stories depending on distance, perspective and viewing conditions.

## What separates a mystery from a usable case

The contrast among Chef-du-Pont, Cerisy-la-Salle and Brix provides a practical way to assess the department’s UFO history.

**A dramatic description is not the same as strong documentation.** The 1979 orange ball and the 1983 fireball both contained striking movement, colour or sound. Yet neither supplied enough independent or measurable information to test the leading explanations.

**Multiple witnesses help only when their accounts can be compared.** The Cerisy-la-Salle file involved two farmers, but the absence of traces and detailed reconstruction still prevented a firm conclusion. Witness numbers alone cannot replace accurate timing, direction and environmental data.

**Physical traces are valuable when properly analysed.** At Brix, the scorched soil initially deepened the mystery. Chemical testing then weakened the extraordinary interpretation by identifying kerosene. The trace mattered precisely because investigators did not assume what had produced it.

**A probable explanation can be more reasonable than an impossible standard of proof.** Category B does not claim absolute certainty. It indicates that an identified cause fits the evidence well, even though every step cannot be directly demonstrated. For historical cases, that is often the most justified conclusion available.

**Insufficient evidence is not evidence of an exotic object.** A category C file records the limits of an investigation. It should not be counted alongside a well-documented unresolved case merely because no final identification was reached.

## Manche’s record is stronger when weak cases stay weak

These cases give Manche a sober but informative UFO history. The 1979 reports remain little more than intriguing testimony because the information needed to reconstruct them was never obtained. The 1983 fireball may have been an uncommon electrical event, but the evidence is too sparse to move beyond suggestion. Brix, by contrast, shows how prompt fieldwork and laboratory analysis can turn a supposed landing trace into a probable fuel fire.

Later identifications involving sky lanterns, condensation trails and a bolide reveal no single departmental mystery or sustained wave. They show a changing mixture of natural phenomena and human activity, interpreted under difficult viewing conditions. The most reliable conclusion is therefore not that every report has been explained, nor that the unexplained residue represents extraordinary craft. It is that outcomes depend heavily on evidence quality: some cases remain unresolved because they are genuinely puzzling, while others remain open only because too little was recorded to investigate them properly.
